Our brains selectively edit the past, filtering out the discomfort while preserving the glow of simpler times. This psychological editing creates a false narrative that things were better before—a narrative that shapes how we view the present and approach the future.

The "good old days" often included poverty without safety nets, limited medical care, systemic discrimination, and zero social mobility. What we romanticize as community was sometimes survival by necessity. What we recall as strong character was often trauma hardened into resilience.

This selective memory isn't just harmless nostalgia—it's dangerous. It makes us resistant to progress. It convinces us that solutions that worked (or didn't work) decades ago are still viable today. It blinds us to how far we've come and how far we still need to go.

The Aha Moment: Understanding that our nostalgia is a filter, not a record. That the past we miss often didn't exist as we remember it. That progress, despite its flaws, is real and measurable.
