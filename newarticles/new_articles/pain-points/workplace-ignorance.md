There's a pervasive feeling across workplaces that no one actually knows what's happening. Projects proceed without clear direction. Decisions are made by people who don't understand the implications. Processes exist because "that's how we've always done it" rather than because they make sense. Everyone is faking competence to varying degrees.

This isn't just about incompetence—it's about structural problems. Organizations grow beyond what any single person can comprehend. Information gets siloed in departments that don't communicate. Decisions get pushed upward to people who are too removed from reality to make informed choices. Responsibilities get distributed so broadly that accountability disappears.

The result is inefficiency, frustration, and burnout. Competent people spend their time compensating for systemic failures. Good ideas die in bureaucratic purgatory. Problems fester because no one has both the authority and the information to solve them.

What's particularly demoralizing is that most organizations could function much better with simple changes: clearer communication, documented processes, realistic expectations, and the courage to admit when something isn't working. But admitting ignorance requires vulnerability, and most organizational cultures punish vulnerability.

The Pain Point: Most workplaces operate on collective ignorance that everyone recognizes but no one feels empowered to fix.
