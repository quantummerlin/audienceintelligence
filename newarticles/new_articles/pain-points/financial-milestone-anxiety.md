There's a silent epidemic of financial milestone anxiety. People in their 20s and 30s feel like failures because they haven't hit markers that their parents hit at the same age—home ownership, marriage, career stability, savings. But the comparison is fundamentally flawed.

The economic conditions have changed dramatically. Housing costs have outpaced wage growth. Student debt is a reality that didn't exist at this scale before. Job markets are more precarious. The path that worked one generation ago doesn't work today, yet we measure ourselves against it anyway.

This creates perpetual dissatisfaction. Even when people are doing reasonably well by objective standards, they feel behind because the milestones are anchored in a different economic reality. They make desperate decisions—taking on too much debt, rushing into commitments they're not ready for—trying to catch up to a timeline that was never achievable.

The anxiety itself becomes self-fulfilling. Fear of falling behind drives poor financial decisions. Comparison creates resentment. The constant feeling of inadequacy prevents enjoyment of what has actually been achieved.

The Pain Point: We're running a race to milestones that were set under different economic conditions, beating ourselves up for not hitting them.
