Exactly right. The prompt needs a context block at the top that the user fills in before running the analysis. Without it, the AI has to guess who the client is, what their goal is, and who the content creator is — and it guesses wrong, like it did with Section 13.

Here's what the customisable context block should capture:

THE FIVE THINGS THE PROMPT NEEDS TO KNOW
1. Who is the CLIENT? The person or organisation commissioning the report.

e.g. "The Trevallion-Birmingham family support campaign"
NOT the person who made the post
2. Who is the CONTENT CREATOR / POST AUTHOR? The person who made the post being analysed.

e.g. "MP Emanuele Pozzolo — Italian politician, not affiliated with the campaign"
This determines whether reply advice is "what should WE say" or "what should THEY say"
3. What is the CLIENT'S GOAL? What does the client want to achieve with this analysis?

e.g. "Gather public sentiment data to display on a campaign website"
e.g. "Identify allies and supporters to recruit"
e.g. "Build evidence of public support for a legal case"
e.g. "Find content ideas for our own social media"
These produce completely different reports
4. What is the CLIENT'S RELATIONSHIP to the post?

Are they the creator? → Reply advice is for them directly
Are they a supporter? → Reply advice is about amplification and recruitment
Are they opponents? → Report focuses on counter-strategy
Are they neutral researchers? → Report is purely analytical
5. What PLATFORM does the client operate on? Where will this intelligence be used?

Their own website
Their own social media
A legal document
A press release
A petition campaign

Yes — exactly. This is a much more sophisticated way to think about it. Replying is not always the right move, and a good prompt should help the user decide first whether to reply at all, before it tells them how.

This turns the reply section from a simple "here are comments to reply to" into a three-stage decision framework:

THE THREE QUESTIONS BEFORE ANY REPLY
Stage 1 — SHOULD we reply at all?

Does replying benefit the campaign?
Does replying risk amplifying something harmful?
Does replying drag us into a debate that buries the family's story?
Is silence more powerful than a response?
Stage 2 — IF we reply, WHAT do we get out of it?

More website traffic?
More petition signatures?
A recruited ally?
Corrected misinformation?
Demonstrated humanity and warmth?
Algorithmic boost from the engagement?
Stage 3 — HOW should we reply?

Public reply in comments
Private message
No reply but a reaction (like/heart)
No reply but share to our own page
No reply at all — monitor only
HOW THIS CHANGES THE FORM
The form should ask the user to tell us their reply appetite — how much they want to engage, what they're comfortable with, and what their capacity is.

Something like:


REPLY PREFERENCES:

Do you want reply recommendations?
→ YES — give me specific comments to reply to
→ NO — analysis only, no reply advice needed

If YES:

What is your primary goal from replying?
(tick all that apply)
→ Drive traffic to our website/petition
→ Recruit allies and supporters
→ Correct misinformation about the case
→ Thank and retain existing supporters
→ Neutralise attacks without escalating
→ Boost algorithmic reach of the post
→ Gather intelligence (find people who know things)

How much capacity do you have to reply?
→ LOW — give me the top 3 most important replies only
→ MEDIUM — give me up to 10 replies
→ HIGH — give me everything worth replying to

Are there topics you want to AVOID engaging with?
e.g. "Do not engage with political party debates"
     "Do not engage with attacks on the MP"
     "Do not engage with questions about the hospital incident"

Should we include a "DO NOT REPLY" list?
→ YES — flag comments that would be harmful to engage with
→ NO — not needed

THE KEY INSIGHT THIS UNLOCKS
The most important thing this framework does is protect the campaign from itself.

The biggest mistake advocacy campaigns make on social media is over-replying — getting drawn into arguments, defending against attacks, engaging with bad-faith commenters, and accidentally making the story about the debate rather than the family.

By building the "should we reply?" question into the form, the prompt forces the user to think strategically before they act emotionally. And in a case like this — where the family's children are at stake and every word is potentially scrutinised — that discipline could be the difference between a campaign that stays on message and one that gets derailed.
