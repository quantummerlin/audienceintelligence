The Psychology of Doomscrolling

24,507 upvotes · by u/cant_put_it_down · 12 min read
▲ 24,507 people found this relatable
Advertisement

It starts with checking the news for five minutes before bed. You pick up your phone intending a brief scan, and forty minutes later you are reading a comment thread about a geopolitical crisis while lying in the dark, heart rate elevated, sleep nowhere close, knowing with absolute certainty that this is not helping you and finding yourself unable to stop. Every article produces three more links. Every crisis connects to three more crises. The scroll has no natural bottom and the need to keep going past the bottom, past the moment where information stopped being useful and started being actively harmful, is something your rational mind watches helplessly while some other part of your brain keeps the thumb moving.

Doomscrolling, the compulsive consumption of distressing news and negative content online, entered common usage around 2020 and named an experience that millions of people immediately recognized. The naming was useful. The behavior it describes is not a willpower failure or a character flaw. It is the predictable output of a collision between human cognitive architecture that has not changed in tens of thousands of years and a technological environment that was specifically engineered, with extraordinary resources and sophistication, to exploit the properties of that architecture for commercial purposes. Understanding what is actually happening in your brain during a doomscrolling session doesn't make it stop, but it does clarify why it happens and what actually needs to change to disrupt it.

Negativity Bias: The Feature That Became a Bug

Human attention is not symmetrically distributed across positive and negative information. We are systematically more attentive to, more affected by and better at remembering negative information than equivalent positive information. This is called negativity bias, and it is among the most extensively documented findings in cognitive psychology. Negative events produce stronger and longer-lasting emotional responses than positive events of comparable magnitude. Negative information is processed more thoroughly and retained more reliably. The anticipation of negative outcomes more powerfully motivates behavior than the anticipation of equivalent positive outcomes.

This is not a design flaw in human cognition. It is an extremely sensible adaptation for an ancestral environment where failing to adequately attend to threats had costs that were immediate, severe and irreversible, while failing to adequately attend to opportunities had costs that were real but typically recoverable. The organism that missed a potential food source might go hungry for a day. The organism that failed to notice a predator died. Evolution selected for threat-sensitive attention with considerable efficiency.

In modern media environments this bias becomes a vulnerability. News organizations and platform algorithms discovered, largely empirically rather than theoretically, that negative and threatening content reliably generates more engagement than positive or neutral content. Outrage, fear and anxiety keep people on platforms longer than contentment or satisfaction. The optimization of media and social platforms for engagement metrics has therefore produced a systematic skew toward content that activates threat-processing circuits, because that content is more engaging by the metrics that matter commercially. Your brain's negativity bias is not a problem for the platforms. It is their product.

"I track my screen time now. My average time per session spikes significantly when there's a major news event. I'm not making a conscious choice to spend more time. Something pulls me back to the phone every few minutes. Understanding that it's my threat-detection system being activated doesn't make it less uncomfortable but it makes it less mysterious." — r/DigitalMinimalism, 13,847 upvotes

The Uncertainty Loop

One of the primary drivers of doomscrolling that is often underappreciated is the relationship between uncertainty and anxiety. When a threatening situation is developing, the brain's threat-monitoring system generates an imperative to gather more information, because in ancestral environments more information about developing threats was usually actionable and often life-saving. The problem in modern information environments is that more news consumption almost never produces the resolution that would allow the threat-monitoring system to stand down. You read another article and the situation is still unresolved. You read five more and it remains uncertain. The uncertainty that generated the scrolling remains because the news cannot resolve it, but the imperative to gather more information doesn't get that message.

This creates a loop that feels purposeful but isn't. The person scrolling through news coverage of a geopolitical crisis is not becoming meaningfully better informed with each additional article after the first few. They are feeding an information-seeking drive that cannot be satisfied by more information because the underlying need is for resolution of uncertainty and no amount of information can produce that resolution when the situation itself is genuinely unresolved. The loop ends when the threat-monitoring system exhausts itself or when something else claims attention, not when the information-seeking drive is satisfied, because it can't be satisfied by what the scrolling is providing.

"I've noticed that I scroll most intensely not when things are actually at their worst but when they're uncertain. When something is actually bad and clear I can process it. When it's ambiguous and developing I can't stop looking. The uncertainty is the real driver." — r/Anxiety, 19,283 upvotes

The unpredictability of the content stream is a crucial feature of this dynamic. Variable reinforcement schedules, where rewards arrive unpredictably rather than on a fixed pattern, produce the most persistent and extinction-resistant behaviors in both animal and human learning research. This is why slot machines are more compelling than vending machines: the vending machine always delivers when you put money in, while the slot machine delivers sometimes, and the sometimes is enough to sustain the behavior through long stretches of loss. The social media feed works on the same principle. Most of the content is neutral or unpleasant. Occasionally there is something genuinely interesting, validating or satisfying. The unpredictable ratio of rewarding to neutral content produces engagement that would not exist if the reward were either consistent or entirely absent.

What Makes It Feel Productive When It Isn't

One of the reasons doomscrolling is harder to resist than other forms of time wasting is that it presents itself as a form of responsible civic engagement. Keeping up with the news is a norm in many social milieus. Being informed about current events is associated with intelligence, education and social participation. Scrolling through news and political content can be framed to oneself as staying informed, as caring about the world, as not being the kind of person who buries their head in the sand. This framing provides a cognitive justification for behavior that is otherwise clearly not serving the person engaging in it.

The research on news consumption and actual civic engagement does not support this self-perception. The correlation between the quantity of news consumption and meaningful civic participation, defined as voting, volunteering, contacting elected representatives and other forms of actual political action, is weak and possibly negative at high consumption levels. Heavy news consumers tend to feel more anxious, more powerless and more cynical about institutions than lighter consumers, not more empowered and engaged. The feeling of being informed that news consumption produces is genuine. Its conversion into anything that actually changes outcomes is largely illusory beyond certain thresholds of consumption.

There is also a specific emotional function that doomscrolling serves that has nothing to do with being informed. Anxiety is a state of heightened arousal that seeks resolution. One way to manage an anxious feeling is to redirect it toward an external object: if you are vaguely anxious about your own life, immersing yourself in the specific concrete anxieties of political crisis and social catastrophe provides a temporarily satisfying explanation for why you feel bad. The external situation explains the internal state, which is paradoxically more comfortable than the alternative of sitting with free-floating anxiety that doesn't have a clear object. The news becomes a container for feelings that are really about something else entirely.

"I started noticing that I scrolled most when I was avoiding something personal. A difficult conversation, a work problem, my own feelings about something. The news was a place to put attention that felt more productive and less threatening than my actual life." — r/therapy, 22,914 upvotes

The Sleep Destruction Machine

Doomscrolling's effects on sleep are among its most concretely measurable harms and deserve more attention than they typically receive in discussions that focus on the psychological or informational dimensions. The pathways to sleep disruption are multiple and compounding.

Blue light emitted by screens suppresses melatonin production, delaying the physiological shift toward sleep readiness. This is a fairly well-known mechanism that has received substantial public health attention. Less well-covered but probably more significant is the effect of emotionally activating content on the nervous system's sleep transition. Sleep requires the nervous system to shift from alert to calm states, a transition that is impeded by sustained emotional arousal. Reading distressing news or engaging with high-outrage content immediately before attempting sleep is neurologically equivalent to exercising intensely before bed, activating the sympathetic nervous system at precisely the moment you need to be activating the parasympathetic one.

The consequences compound. Sleep deprivation elevates anxiety, increases emotional reactivity, impairs executive function, and reduces the prefrontal cortex's ability to regulate impulse and moderate the threat-detection circuitry that drives doomscrolling in the first place. The person who sleep-doomscrolled until midnight and woke at 6am is more anxious, less able to manage that anxiety and more susceptible to the pull of distressing content the following evening than they would have been had they slept adequately. The behavior that disrupted the sleep also removes the primary resource for managing the behavior.

Breaking the Loop Without Pretending It's Easy

The standard advice for doomscrolling is straightforward: put the phone down, set app limits, charge your phone in a different room, designate news-free hours. This advice is accurate and not wrong. It is also insufficient on its own because it addresses the behavior without addressing the psychological drives that produce it. Someone whose doomscrolling is serving a threat-monitoring function or an avoidance function will find ways around screen time limits, because the drive producing the behavior is stronger than the behavioral rule opposing it.

More durable change tends to come from addressing the underlying function. If the scrolling is driven by intolerance of uncertainty, practicing sitting with uncertainty through smaller experiments builds the tolerance gradually. If it's serving an avoidance function, identifying and engaging with what's being avoided removes the pressure that makes the scroll attractive. If it's a response to genuine anxiety that needs an outlet, developing alternative outlets (physical exercise, which discharges the physiological arousal of threat-activation, is particularly effective) reduces the pull toward the screen.

Designing the environment matters more than willpower, because willpower is a finite resource that depletes over the course of a day. Physical separation of the phone from the sleeping environment removes the availability that enables late-night scrolling more reliably than any intent to not scroll. Replacing the opening-the-phone-first-thing-in-the-morning habit with a non-screen morning routine removes the window when the day's anxiety baseline gets set by the overnight news before the person has had any cognitive resources to process it. The friction between the impulse and the behavior needs to be higher than the motivation to scroll, and motivation tends to be highest when resources are lowest, so the design needs to work when you're at your most depleted rather than when you're making the plan.

"I put my phone charger in the kitchen. That's it. No special app, no rule, no willpower. Just physical distance at night. My sleep improved within a week. The simplest changes are sometimes the only ones that actually work." — r/nosurf, 31,248 upvotes

The broader question, which doesn't have a clean individual-level answer, is what it means to be informed and civically engaged in an information environment specifically designed to make the distinction between information and stimulation impossible to maintain. Some news is genuinely important to consume. Some proportion of the hours people currently spend in news consumption environments is producing genuine understanding of the world. The rest is feeding biological drives that evolved for different environments and are being exploited for profit at the cost of sleep, wellbeing and the clarity of attention that would make the actually important information more useful when you did encounter it. Knowing which is which requires deliberate attention rather than default consumption, which is the opposite of how most people currently relate to their feeds.
