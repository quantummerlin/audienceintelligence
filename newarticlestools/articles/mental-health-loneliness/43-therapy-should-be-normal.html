Why Therapy Should Be Normal

22,913 upvotes · by u/finally_went · 13 min read
▲ 22,913 people found this relatable
Advertisement

At some point in the past decade, at least in certain demographics and urban contexts, going to therapy stopped being something you admitted to reluctantly and started being something people mentioned in the same breath as going to the gym or seeing a doctor for a checkup. The cultural stigma, while not gone, has genuinely softened in ways that public health advocates spent decades working toward. Young adults in particular have normalized therapeutic language, concepts like boundaries and attachment styles and cognitive distortions, to a degree that would have been unrecognizable a generation ago.

And yet the gap between the cultural normalization of therapy and actual access to it remains enormous. The majority of people who would benefit from professional mental health support are not receiving it, and the reasons span a range from genuine access barriers to subtler obstacles that the destigmatization campaigns haven't touched. Understanding both what therapy actually is, which differs considerably from most people's assumptions before they've tried it, and what the real obstacles to accessing it are seems worth doing thoroughly, because the surface-level conversation around mental health often does more to perform awareness than to actually change the conditions that keep people from getting help.

What Therapy Actually Is and Isn't

The image of therapy that persists in popular culture, a person lying on a couch while a silent analyst takes notes and occasionally asks how something made you feel, bears almost no resemblance to how most contemporary therapy actually works. The landscape of evidence-based therapeutic approaches is wide and includes treatments specifically designed for specific conditions, treatments with defined session counts and structured protocols, and approaches that are considerably more active and directive than the passive "tell me about your childhood" caricature.

Cognitive behavioral therapy, which is the most extensively researched psychological treatment in existence, is a structured, collaborative intervention that involves identifying specific patterns of thought and behavior that maintain psychological problems, then systematically testing and modifying those patterns through a combination of in-session work and between-session assignments. A CBT therapist is active in the room, teaches specific skills, sets homework and measures progress against defined goals. The average course of CBT for many conditions is twelve to twenty sessions, not years of open-ended exploration.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, dialectical behavior therapy, EMDR for trauma, motivational interviewing, interpersonal therapy, solution-focused brief therapy: the range of evidence-supported approaches is significant, and different approaches suit different people and different presentations. The idea that therapy means one thing and either works for you or doesn't ignores the diversity within the field and the importance of finding an approach and a therapist that fits your specific needs and preferences.

"I tried therapy twice and it didn't help. Third time, different therapist, completely different approach, it worked. I wish someone had told me earlier that fit matters enormously and that a bad experience doesn't mean therapy doesn't work for you." — r/mentalhealth, 18,472 upvotes

The therapeutic relationship is itself a primary mechanism of change, not just a pleasant feature of a useful service. The research on what predicts therapy outcomes is surprisingly consistent: the quality of the alliance between therapist and client, defined as agreement on goals and methods and a genuine bond of trust, is among the strongest predictors of outcome across different therapeutic modalities. This means that the relationship matters enormously, which in turn means that the experience of not clicking with a therapist is information about fit rather than information about whether therapy works. Finding a good match is not a luxury. It is a clinical necessity.

What Actually Happens in the Process

Most people going to therapy for the first time have limited realistic expectations for what the experience will involve, partly because the realistic picture has less entertainment value than the dramatic version and partly because the process of change in therapy is genuinely difficult to describe without experiencing it. A few things are worth knowing in advance.

Therapy often gets harder before it gets easier, particularly in the early stages when previously avoided thoughts, feelings and memories are being engaged with rather than managed around. This is not a malfunction of the process. It is a predictable feature of how therapeutic change works: the things that produce psychological suffering in the long run are usually things that have been successfully avoided in the short run, and engaging with them produces temporary discomfort before it produces lasting relief. Clients who stop therapy in the first few weeks because it felt worse than not going are often stopping just before the process would have started helping.

Progress in therapy is also rarely linear. Most people experience sessions that feel deeply productive and sessions that feel like nothing happened, weeks where things clearly improve and weeks where old patterns reassert themselves. This irregularity is normal and not evidence that the work isn't working. Lasting psychological change involves learning new patterns and that learning, like all learning, involves repetition, setback and gradual consolidation rather than a single breakthrough followed by permanent transformation.

"I kept waiting for a big revelation that would fix everything. Instead therapy was like... slowly figuring out a code. Piece by piece. Nothing dramatic. But six months later I looked back and couldn't believe how different things were." — r/therapy, 21,349 upvotes

Therapy is also not the same as venting, though venting can be part of it. The distinction matters because some people enter therapy expecting sustained emotional expression and feel disappointed when the therapist starts asking questions that redirect or reframe rather than simply validating. The goal is not to leave every session feeling heard and validated, though that matters. The goal is to leave the overall process with a changed relationship to the problems that brought you in, which sometimes requires sessions that are uncomfortable or challenging rather than simply warm and supportive.

Who Benefits and for What

The evidence base for psychotherapy covers a wide range of conditions and presentations, and the summary finding is encouraging: for most common mental health conditions, evidence-based therapy produces clinically meaningful improvement in a substantial majority of treated cases. For depression, anxiety disorders, PTSD, OCD, eating disorders, substance use issues and a range of other conditions, specific therapeutic protocols have demonstrated efficacy in controlled trials and in real-world practice. The effect sizes for some specific condition-treatment combinations are among the largest in medicine.

But the case for therapy extends beyond diagnosable mental health conditions to the broader category of ordinary human struggle with difficult life circumstances, relationships, decisions and transitions. People go to therapy for grief, career confusion, relationship problems, identity questions, major life changes and the diffuse dissatisfaction that doesn't have a clean diagnostic category. For these presentations the evidence base is less systematic but the practical value is well-attested by clinical experience and by the large number of people whose testimony is that having a skilled, neutral, trained professional to think alongside has been genuinely valuable in navigating situations that weren't pathological but were genuinely hard.

The idea that therapy is only for people who are seriously struggling is one of the more persistent obstacles to broader access, because it positions seeking help as something you do when things have gotten bad enough to justify it rather than something you do proactively as a form of ongoing maintenance and development. The people who get the most cumulative value from therapy over a lifetime tend to be those who treat it as a resource for growth rather than a rescue service for crisis, but that framing requires access at price points and availability levels that most people don't have.

The Real Access Problems

The destigmatization of mental health, which is genuine and valuable progress, has created a somewhat misleading picture of the state of mental health access. Social media wellness culture has normalized the language and concepts of therapy to such a degree that it can feel as though help is widely available. For most people in most places it is not, or it is available at a cost and under conditions that make it effectively inaccessible.

Cost is the primary barrier. In the United States, where mental health parity laws were supposed to require insurance coverage equivalent to physical health coverage, the practical reality for most insured people is that their network of covered mental health providers is small, often out of date, frequently composed of providers who aren't taking new clients and almost never includes the most experienced practitioners. The therapists who take insurance are overwhelmingly newer to practice, working in settings with high caseloads and limited session flexibility. The therapists with extensive experience and strong track records have almost universally moved to private pay practices where sessions run between $150 and $350 per hour in most urban markets. This is not affordable for most people in the income ranges where mental health problems are most prevalent.

"I've been on a waiting list for a therapist who takes my insurance for seven months. Seven months. The crisis line told me to seek professional support. The professional support told me to wait." — r/mentalhealth, 34,721 upvotes

Geographic access is a compounding problem. Therapist distribution heavily favors urban and suburban areas with higher median incomes, producing significant deserts in rural areas and lower-income urban neighborhoods where people have the fewest resources to navigate alternatives. Teletherapy, which expanded dramatically during the pandemic, has improved geographic access meaningfully for people with reliable internet access and sufficient privacy in their living situations to conduct private sessions. It hasn't solved the problem for the populations with the greatest need and the fewest resources.

Therapist workforce composition also creates access barriers for specific populations. Research consistently shows that clients from marginalized racial and ethnic backgrounds experience better outcomes with therapists who share their background or have specific training in culturally responsive practice. The clinical workforce remains predominantly white in most Western countries, with meaningful representation gaps that translate into experience gaps for clients whose experiences and cultural contexts are outside the mainstream of training and practice.

The Substitutes People Use Instead

When professional therapy is unavailable or unaffordable, people find substitutes, and the substitutes range from genuinely helpful to actively harmful. At the helpful end, peer support communities, whether online or in-person, provide connection with others who share specific experiences and can offer validation, practical information and a sense that the struggle is shared. Twelve-step programs and other structured peer recovery communities have demonstrated meaningful efficacy for substance use issues at scale with no cost barrier. Self-help materials from evidence-based therapeutic approaches, particularly the CBT-based workbooks and structured programs, can produce genuine benefit for mild-to-moderate presentations in people who engage with them seriously.

At the less helpful end, social media mental health content presents a complex picture. The democratization of psychological concepts has genuine value in reducing stigma and helping people understand their experiences, but it also enables a kind of perpetual self-analysis that can sustain rumination rather than resolving it, and a consumption of therapeutic concepts without the relational process through which those concepts actually produce change. Knowing what an anxious attachment style is does not, in itself, change an anxious attachment style. It requires practice, reflection, repeated real-world experience and usually a relational context in which the change can occur.

The normalization of therapy as a concept hasn't been matched by the normalization of therapy as a resource that people can actually reach when they need it. Both parts of that project matter, and we have made considerably more progress on one than the other. The conversation about mental health has opened in ways that matter. The infrastructure to support what that conversation implies people should have access to has not kept pace, and pretending otherwise, through wellness aesthetics and therapeutic language and destigmatization campaigns that don't address structural barriers, does a disservice to the people who need actual help and can't get it.
