Praise, Self-Esteem and the Effort Myth

In 1969, the California legislature passed a bill creating the California Task Force to Promote Self-Esteem and Personal and Social Responsibility. The task force spent three years and nearly a million dollars investigating the relationship between self-esteem and social problems, operating on the hypothesis that low self-esteem was a root cause of crime, welfare dependency, academic underachievement and substance abuse, and that raising self-esteem would address all of these simultaneously. The task force released its report in 1990. The problem was that the research their own commissioned scientist had compiled simply did not support the central hypothesis. The correlations between self-esteem and positive outcomes were weak and inconsistent. Criminologist Roy Baumeister, one of the researchers involved in evaluating the evidence, later described the self-esteem literature as "the largest body of social science research to produce the smallest and most inconsistent findings."

The task force report was largely ignored, and the self-esteem movement went on to transform American parenting and education for the next three decades. Trophy participation, grade inflation, constant affirmation, protection from failure, the elevation of self-belief above evidence -- all of these became standard features of how children were raised and schooled in the belief that building up their sense of worth would produce resilient, high-achieving, socially functional adults. The research on what actually happened to that generation is complicated and contested, but the simple version of the story -- that high self-esteem is the key input and praise is the key mechanism -- has not survived empirical scrutiny.

The Self-Esteem Research Problem

Roy Baumeister and his colleagues published what became one of the most widely cited reviews in social psychology in 2003: a comprehensive evaluation of the self-esteem literature that found high self-esteem reliably predicts exactly one thing with consistency: feeling good about yourself. It predicts academic achievement weakly and inconsistently, with most of the correlation explained by prior achievement rather than self-esteem independently driving performance. It predicts interpersonal success weakly. It does not predict reduced aggression -- Baumeister's own research on narcissism and violence suggested that high unstable self-esteem (positive view of self threatened by criticism) was associated with more rather than less aggressive behavior. People with genuinely high, stable self-esteem are not dramatically more successful or prosocial than those with moderate self-esteem.

More damaging to the self-esteem movement's assumptions was research on narcissism by Twenge and Campbell, which documented a significant increase in narcissistic traits among young Americans between the 1980s and 2000s that correlated with the period of most intense self-esteem movement influence. The Narcissistic Personality Inventory scores of college students had risen markedly; rates of students agreeing with statements like "I am an important person" had climbed substantially. If the self-esteem movement had been working as intended, this inflated self-assessment should have predicted better outcomes. Instead it predicted more fragility, more difficulty with negative feedback, more interpersonal conflict, and more discontent when reality failed to meet inflated expectations.

"I grew up in the height of 'everyone gets a trophy' culture and I can tell you from the inside what it actually taught us: not to value achievement, because the trophy came regardless of whether you won or lost. What it also taught us is that adults can't be trusted to tell you true things, because they're giving you trophies you didn't earn. It wasn't confidence-building. It was a kind of epistemic corruption." r/Millennials · 24,392 upvotes

The distinction between self-esteem and self-efficacy is crucial and frequently lost in popular discussions. Self-efficacy, developed theoretically by Albert Bandura, refers to the belief in one's ability to accomplish specific tasks -- not a global sense of worth but a competence-based confidence derived from actual experience of mastery. Self-efficacy predicts academic achievement, persistence and resilience much more robustly than self-esteem does, and its developmental pathway is completely different: it is built through mastery experiences (doing hard things and succeeding), through vicarious learning (watching similar others succeed), and through feedback from credible sources. It cannot be installed through unconditional positive regard or by protecting someone from failure. The failure is often the mechanism through which it develops.

Carol Dweck and the Mindset Research

Carol Dweck's research on implicit theories of intelligence, summarized in her 2006 book "Mindset," produced findings that directly contradicted the self-esteem movement's approach to praise and became enormously influential in education and parenting, not entirely without their own subsequent complications. Dweck distinguished between a "fixed mindset" (the belief that intelligence and ability are innate and stable) and a "growth mindset" (the belief that intelligence and ability can be developed through effort and learning). Her research showed that children with fixed mindsets avoided challenges, gave up more easily after failure, and interpreted difficulty as evidence of inadequate ability. Children with growth mindsets embraced challenges as learning opportunities, showed more persistence and achieved more over time.

The critical finding for parenting was that praise type influenced which mindset children developed. In a series of elegant experiments, Dweck and Claudia Mueller gave fifth-grade students a set of problems, then randomly assigned them to receive either intelligence praise ("You must be smart at this") or effort praise ("You must have worked really hard"). Children then chose whether to attempt a harder challenge or an easier one, worked on harder problems and were told they hadn't done well, and then were given the original difficulty level again. The intelligence-praised children overwhelmingly chose the easier task when given the choice, attributed their failures to lack of ability, showed decreased enjoyment and performed worse on the final set. The effort-praised children chose the harder task, attributed difficulty to insufficient effort, maintained enjoyment and improved their performance. The type of praise received in a single session produced measurable effects on cognition, motivation and behavior.

The mechanism Dweck proposed is elegant: telling a child they are smart locates their success in a fixed quality. When they subsequently fail -- which they inevitably will -- the failure becomes evidence that the fixed quality isn't there. Failure under a fixed mindset is threatening to the self, which produces avoidance of the challenges that might reveal the deficiency. Telling a child their success reflects their effort locates the success in something controllable and mutable. Failure becomes information about what to do differently, not evidence about who they are. Difficulty is expected and manageable rather than threatening.

"What Dweck's research captures is something genuinely important: the story you tell a child about why they succeeded changes how they approach future challenges. 'You're so smart' is a story that makes failure devastating. 'You worked hard' is a story that makes failure instructive. That's not a small difference." r/psychology · 18,473 upvotes

The Replication Problem

Growth mindset research achieved extraordinary cultural penetration: it became the basis for educational interventions in thousands of schools, corporate training programs, parenting advice and popular self-help literature. And then, like many findings that achieved premature certainty in their application, it ran into replication difficulties that complicated the picture significantly.

A large-scale replication study by Sisk and colleagues in 2018, published in Psychological Science, conducted meta-analyses on both laboratory studies of growth mindset and intervention studies aimed at changing students' mindsets to improve academic outcomes. The results were sobering. The relationship between growth mindset and academic achievement was very small across the literature as a whole (r = .10). Growth mindset interventions showed similarly small average effects on achievement outcomes. The original Dweck laboratory findings proved robust, but the translation from laboratory effects to real-world educational outcomes was weaker than the cultural uptake of growth mindset theory had assumed.

Further complicating matters, the effects that did appear were concentrated in specific populations: low-income students and students who were otherwise at academic risk showed more benefit from growth mindset interventions than high-achieving students from advantaged backgrounds. The intervention was most useful for people facing specific obstacles to achievement that growth mindset could address, and least useful for people whose challenges were more structural. A growth mindset cannot compensate for inadequate instruction, poverty, learning disabilities that haven't been identified, or school environments with insufficient resources and support.

David Yeager at the University of Texas, one of the more rigorous researchers working in this area, has done important work on the conditions under which mindset interventions are and aren't effective. His research suggests that brief one-time interventions have small effects that often disappear over time, while interventions that are embedded in a supportive school culture, delivered by credible adults, and reinforced by an environment that genuinely offers opportunities for growth show larger and more durable effects. The mechanism requires a context in which effort genuinely leads somewhere -- where trying harder actually produces better outcomes. In classrooms where teaching is poor, resources are limited, or success depends primarily on factors outside the student's control, effort praise rings hollow and growth mindset interventions land with limited purchase.

What the Praise Research Actually Shows

Setting aside the specific growth mindset findings, the research literature on praise and feedback has produced a more nuanced picture than either "praise freely" or "praise only effort" captures. Several consistent findings have emerged from decades of work on the psychology of feedback and motivation.

Informational feedback is more valuable than evaluative feedback. Telling a child specifically what they did well ("Your argument here is logically organized and you supported each point with evidence") provides more useful information for future performance than global evaluation ("Good job!"). The specificity matters because it gives the child actionable information about what to replicate and what to improve, rather than just a valence signal that something good happened.

The credibility of praise matters. Research by Jennifer Henderlong Corpus and Mark Lepper found that praise that children perceive as accurate and deserved increases intrinsic motivation, while praise they perceive as inflated, unconditional or inaccurate decreases it. Children are not fools: they know when praise doesn't match their actual performance, and when they consistently receive praise that they don't believe is earned, they update their model of the praiser as unreliable rather than updating their self-assessment upward. The child who receives trophies for last place learns that adults cannot be trusted to give accurate feedback, which is the opposite of the intended effect.

"My daughter was in a class where the teacher praised everything effusively. After a few months my daughter stopped showing me her work because she said 'Ms. Johnson just says everything is great no matter what.' She'd lost access to useful feedback and she knew it. I had to actively rebuild her sense that her own judgment about her work was worth trusting." r/Parenting · 19,284 upvotes

Process praise, a broader category than effort praise, includes praising strategy, persistence, help-seeking, revision, and problem-solving approaches alongside raw effort. This is arguably more useful than effort praise alone because it directs attention to the modifiable processes that produce good outcomes, not just whether the child tried hard but whether they tried smart. A child who works very hard using ineffective strategies needs information about their strategies, not just affirmation that they tried hard. Process praise given honestly for actual engagement with process provides that information while maintaining the motivational benefits of effort attribution.

Unconditional Regard vs. Unconditional Positive Regard

Carl Rogers' concept of "unconditional positive regard" -- acceptance of the person regardless of their behavior, without judgment -- has been consistently misapplied in parenting contexts to mean something closer to unconditional positive evaluation: always affirming the child's performance, never expressing disappointment, always framing every output as wonderful. This is not what Rogers meant, and the evidence suggests it is actively harmful in a parenting context.

What the research supports is something more like what Diana Baumrind called "authoritative parenting" and what Wendy Grolnick calls "autonomy-supportive parenting": warm, engaged, responsive relationships with high standards and honest feedback. The warmth is unconditional -- the child knows they are loved regardless of performance -- but the evaluation of performance is not unconditional. This distinction matters enormously. A child who knows they are loved can tolerate honest feedback about their work without feeling that the feedback is a verdict on their worth as a person. A child who has been raised to conflate parental approval with parental love experiences negative feedback as threatening to the relationship itself, which makes them avoidant of the honest assessment that growth requires.

The goal is to separate person from performance in the child's own mental model, not by pretending performance is irrelevant but by making it consistently clear that the parent's view of and love for the child does not rise and fall with the quality of the work. This is easier to say than to do, particularly for parents who are themselves achievement-oriented and have strong emotional responses to their child's performance. But it is the distinction that the evidence most consistently supports.

Self-Compassion as the Alternative to Self-Esteem

Kristin Neff at the University of Texas has developed an influential alternative to self-esteem as the target of wellbeing-focused parenting and psychological intervention. Self-compassion, in Neff's framework, involves three components: self-kindness (treating oneself with the same care one would offer a good friend in distress), common humanity (recognizing that suffering and failure are universal human experiences rather than evidence of personal inadequacy) and mindfulness (holding painful experiences in awareness without over-identification or suppression).

The research on self-compassion and its outcomes is substantially more consistent than the research on self-esteem. Self-compassion predicts lower rates of anxiety and depression, more adaptive responses to failure, greater emotional resilience, less need for social comparison and external validation, and paradoxically higher motivation than self-esteem does. The critical distinction is that self-esteem is contingent on success and positive self-evaluation, which means it is fragile when things go wrong. Self-compassion is explicitly activated by difficulty and failure, which means it is most available precisely when it is most needed.

For parenting, Neff's framework suggests that modeling self-compassion in the parent's own response to failure may be as important as any specific praise strategy. A parent who responds to their own mistakes by saying "That was hard, I didn't do my best, let me figure out what to do differently" is teaching a fundamentally different approach to difficulty than one who either collapses in self-criticism or defensively insists they were right. Children learn how to relate to their own fallibility largely by watching how the adults they love relate to theirs. The most honest advice the research produces is this: be careful how you talk to yourself in front of your children. They are taking notes.
