When Parenting Erases You

▲ 79,384 people found this relatable

There is a certain kind of conversation that happens among new parents, usually in the small hours, usually with the slightly hollowed quality of people operating at the edge of their reserves. Someone says something like "I don't know who I am anymore" and the response is typically either a cheerful reassurance that this will pass, a competitive performance of contentment ("I've never felt more myself!"), or a kind of wistful shrug that treats the loss as the price of admission and not worth examining too carefully. The culture of parenthood doesn't have good language for what it feels like when a major life transition doesn't just add a role but seems to dissolve the person who existed before.

The word that describes this specific experience, one that has only recently entered public conversation after decades of near-complete invisibility, is "matrescence." Coined by anthropologist Dana Raphael in 1973 and substantially developed by reproductive psychiatrist Alexandra Sacks in the 2010s, matrescence refers to the transition to motherhood as a developmental process comparable in magnitude to adolescence: a period of profound identity reorganization, hormonal upheaval, relational restructuring and psychological transformation that is frequently difficult, disorienting and poorly supported by the surrounding culture. The concept was never widely adopted when Raphael introduced it, partly because the cultural script for new motherhood at the time was almost entirely positive -- you were supposed to be completed, not upended, by becoming a mother.

What Identity Loss Actually Looks Like

Research on parental identity change has been building steadily, and it has produced findings that are both more complex and more uncomfortable than the dominant cultural narrative about the joys of parenthood accommodates. A 2020 neuroimaging study by Hoekzema and colleagues published in Nature Neuroscience found structural changes in maternal gray matter that persisted at least two years postpartum -- the brain was literally reorganized by the transition to motherhood, with changes concentrated in regions involved in social cognition and the processing of others' needs. An earlier 2017 study by the same group found that the brain volume changes were associated with stronger maternal attachment, which sounds positive, but also that the reduced volume in certain regions overlapped with patterns seen in conditions involving difficulty managing boundaries between self and other.

The psychological literature on what parents report experiencing is vivid and consistent. Studies on new parental identity typically find several overlapping experiences: loss of biographical continuity (the sense of being the same person who existed before), loss of embodied autonomy (particularly profound for mothers, who may spend months or years with their physical body in near-constant service to another person's needs), loss of professional identity (especially acute for those who took significant career breaks), loss of recreational and social identity (activities, friendships and interests that defined the pre-parent self become inaccessible or much harder to access), and loss of the relational identities that predated parenthood (partner, friend, child, sibling are all reorganized around the new role).

"I had been a runner for fifteen years. Running was how I processed everything -- stress, grief, joy. It was part of how I knew who I was. And then I had a baby and for the first two years I almost never ran. When I finally got back to it, it felt strange, like meeting someone you used to know. I didn't feel like 'me' running anymore. I felt like 'a mom' who was also running. I still don't entirely know how to explain that." r/NewParents · 34,829 upvotes

The experience is not identical for mothers and fathers, though it affects both. Research consistently finds that mothers experience more profound and more disorienting identity disruption, partly because of the biological and physical dimensions of pregnancy, birth and often nursing, partly because of the asymmetric distribution of infant care labor that persists across nearly all Western societies despite significant changes in stated parenting ideals, and partly because of cultural expectations that make complete self-sacrifice the mark of a good mother in ways that simply don't apply to fathers with the same force. But fathers are not immune: research on paternal identity transition finds significant reorganization of self-concept, changes in relationship with their own fathers and families of origin, and the specific challenge of integrating a new identity that is frequently invisible or undervalued in workplace contexts that treat fathers as basically unchanged by parenthood.

Matrescence and the Ambivalence Problem

One of the most important things the matrescence framework does is legitimize ambivalence. The culturally mandated emotional experience of early parenthood is overwhelming love and joy with perhaps some politely acknowledged exhaustion. The actual emotional experience of many new parents, particularly mothers, includes significant grief, anxiety, loss, rage, claustrophobia and profound ambivalence about the choice that cannot be reversed. The gap between the mandated experience and the actual experience creates its own suffering, because parents who feel the difficult emotions believe they are uniquely deficient in the love they're supposed to feel, rather than experiencing the normal range of human response to an enormous and irreversible change.

Adrienne Rich's "Of Woman Born" (1976) remains one of the most honest accounts of maternal ambivalence in print: Rich described the experience of loving her children with ferocity while simultaneously feeling trapped, erased and enraged by motherhood as an institution -- a distinction she drew carefully between the experience of loving children and the institution that shapes how that love is supposed to be performed and what it costs the person performing it. This distinction between motherhood-as-experience and motherhood-as-institution has been largely lost in popular discussion, which tends to treat any expression of maternal difficulty as either a pathology to be treated or a betrayal of the children who did not ask to be born.

Orna Donath's research on "regretting motherhood," published in 2015 based on interviews with Israeli mothers who regretted having children without regretting the children themselves, generated enormous controversy and enormous response from women around the world who described feeling seen for the first time. Donath was not arguing that motherhood is bad or that these women were bad mothers. She was describing a real and previously publicly unspeakable experience: loving your child while genuinely wishing you had not become a parent. The scale of the response -- the tens of thousands of messages she received from women across different cultures and backgrounds -- suggests the experience is far from rare. It is simply very rarely acknowledged.

"The thing that helped me most wasn't therapy or medication or a better support network, though I needed all of those. It was reading Adrienne Rich and realizing that what I was feeling wasn't a problem with me -- it was a rational response to an institution that demands a kind of self-erasure that we would recognize as abusive in any other context. I'm not saying I don't love my kids. I do, completely. But I'm also allowed to notice what the institution costs." r/TwoXChromosomes · 28,492 upvotes

The Hidden Labor of the Mental Load

Much of the identity erosion that parents -- particularly mothers -- experience is driven by what sociologists call the "mental load" or "invisible labor" of family management: the cognitive and organizational work of tracking everyone's needs, scheduling, planning, anticipating problems and coordinating the household's functioning. This work is largely invisible to those who don't do it, generates no recognition or compensation, and consumes significant cognitive bandwidth that was previously available for other things.

Sociologist Arlie Hochschild coined the term "second shift" in her 1989 book of the same name to describe the household labor that employed women returned home to after their paid workday. The mental load concept, popularized partly through Emma's 2017 viral comic "You Should've Asked," extends this to encompass not just the labor but the cognitive management of the labor: holding in mind all the things that need to be done, by whom, when and in what order. Research by Allison Daminger published in the American Sociological Review in 2019 found that across socioeconomic groups and levels of stated commitment to gender equality, the cognitive labor of household management was disproportionately carried by women. Even in households where men did substantial amounts of household task execution, women predominantly anticipated what needed doing, identified options and monitored whether the task had been done adequately.

This invisible labor has a direct identity cost: the cognitive bandwidth it consumes is bandwidth that is no longer available for the projects, interests, relationships and reflections that constitute the self outside of parenting. A person who is constantly tracking their child's appointment schedule, their partner's emotional state, what's in the refrigerator, whether the school forms have been returned and whether the babysitter has confirmed Thursday is a person whose cognitive life has been substantially colonized by coordination tasks. This is not a complaint about love. It is an observation about cognitive resources, and the way in which one person's unacknowledged absorption of coordination labor represents both an identity cost and a relational injustice that the relationship has to find a way to address if it's going to remain sustainable.

The Paternal Experience

Fathers' identity transitions have been substantially under-researched compared to mothers', partly because of the cultural assumption that fatherhood changes men less, and partly because fathers themselves often have fewer community supports for processing the experience. The research that does exist suggests the transition to fatherhood is more significant than the culture acknowledges.

William Marsiglio's sociological research on father identity finds that men navigate competing definitions of fatherhood -- the breadwinner model inherited from their own fathers, the involved-father ideal promoted by contemporary parenting culture, and their own actual experience of what feels natural and manageable -- and that this navigation frequently produces significant identity strain. Men who strongly identify with involved fatherhood may struggle with the ways in which their workplaces are structured around the assumption of fatherhood as secondary, creating conflict between stated values and structural reality that generates its own identity stress.

Research on postpartum paternal depression, which affects approximately ten percent of fathers (compared to roughly fifteen percent of mothers with postpartum depression), suggests that the psychological difficulties of new parenthood are not limited to the biological parent or the primary caregiver. Paternal depression is associated with lower quality parent-child interaction, child behavioral problems, and reduced couple relationship quality, which means it affects not just the father but everyone around him. Yet the cultural visibility of paternal postpartum mental health is a fraction of the visibility of maternal postpartum mental health, leaving many fathers without the normalization, language or support that makes the transition more manageable.

Why Maintaining Yourself Matters for Your Children

There is a strong cultural tendency to frame parental self-preservation as selfish, as taking something from the children in order to serve the adult. The research on parenting outcomes does not support this framing at all. It suggests essentially the opposite: parents who maintain meaningful identities outside of parenthood are better parents by virtually every metric that matters.

Parental wellbeing is the single strongest predictor of parenting quality across the research literature. Parents who are depressed, burned out, identity-depleted and resentful of their role parent worse by measurable criteria: they show less warmth, less responsiveness, more irritability, more use of harsh discipline and more difficulty reading and responding to their children's emotional cues. This is not a moral judgment. It is a predictable consequence of trying to give from an empty vessel, of trying to be consistently warm and present when the person who is supposed to be warm and present has been systematically deprived of the things that sustain them.

"My therapist said something that reoriented everything for me: 'Your children don't need a perfect parent. They need a real one. A real parent has their own needs, their own frustrations, their own life. A parent who has erased themselves to be everything their child needs is teaching their child that love requires self-destruction. Is that the lesson you want them to learn?'" r/Parenting · 42,847 upvotes

Children whose parents model sustained identity and personal interest outside of family life show better outcomes on measures of emotional independence, occupational aspiration, relationship quality and tolerance for their own future partners' needs for autonomy. The parent who demonstrates that adults can have their own passions, maintain their own friendships, pursue their own goals, and take genuine pleasure in aspects of life that have nothing to do with their children is teaching something vital: that selfhood is not incompatible with love, that relationship does not require dissolution of self, and that a life well-lived involves multiple sources of meaning and connection rather than the total sacrifice of one identity for another.

What Recovery Looks Like

The literature on parental identity recovery is less developed than the literature on identity loss, but several consistent themes emerge from both research and clinical accounts. The first is that naming the experience matters. The concept of matrescence gives people a framework for understanding a genuinely difficult transition rather than interpreting their difficulty as personal failure. Having language for an experience is the beginning of being able to address it.

Community matters enormously, and it is specifically the kind of community in which honest accounts are welcome. The competitive performance of parental contentment that characterizes so much of public parenting culture is actively harmful because it prevents the disclosure that enables support. Research on maternal social support consistently finds that the quality of support (whether people can talk honestly about difficult experiences) matters more than quantity. One friend who can hear "this is really hard and I don't feel like myself" without immediately redirecting to solutions or reassurances is worth more than a large network of people who require performance of happiness.

Intentional identity maintenance -- the deliberate preservation of aspects of the pre-parent self that are non-negotiable to the person's wellbeing -- appears in both clinical and research accounts as important but not automatically recognized. The things that sustained identity before parenthood (exercise, creative work, friendships, professional engagement, time in nature, whatever they happen to be) don't disappear in value when a child arrives. They become harder to access, which requires treating them with more intentionality rather than allowing them to be perpetually crowded out by the urgent demands of family life. The parent who waits for permission to need things will wait a very long time. The parent who decides that their needs are not negotiable because the research is clear that meeting them makes them a better parent is closer to the truth.

And perhaps most fundamentally, the cultural story about parental self-sacrifice as the highest expression of love needs revision at its root. The most honest thing the research says is that children do not need parents who have given up everything. They need parents who are genuinely there -- present, warm, engaged and also recognizably themselves. The person who loved certain things before the child arrived, who had relationships and interests and a particular way of moving through the world, is not an obstacle to good parenting. They are the foundation of it. Losing that person is not a virtue. Getting them back is not selfishness. It is what the children actually need, whether the culture makes that easy to believe or not.
