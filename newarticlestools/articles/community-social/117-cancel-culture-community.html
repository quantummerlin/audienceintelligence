Cancel Culture and the Fragile Edges of Community

52,047 upvotes · by u/communal_friction · 13 min read
▲ 52,047 people found this relatable
Advertisement

The phrase "cancel culture" has become one of the most reliably polarizing terms in contemporary discourse, which is partly why it is so difficult to discuss productively. Depending on who you ask, it is either a long-overdue mechanism for holding powerful people accountable or a form of social mob justice that silences dissent and destroys lives over minor transgressions. Both descriptions are occasionally accurate. Neither captures what is actually happening most of the time.

What makes this topic genuinely interesting, and genuinely worth taking seriously, is not the celebrity cases that dominate headlines but what the phenomenon reveals about the deep structure of community itself. Every human group has always had mechanisms for managing deviance, enforcing norms and expelling members who violate them badly enough. What social media has changed is not the existence of those mechanisms but their speed, their scale, their asymmetry and the permanent record they leave behind. Understanding cancel culture means understanding something real about how communities function, how they fail and what accountability requires that punishment cannot provide.

What We Actually Mean When We Say "Canceled"

The word "cancel" in its contemporary sense appears to have emerged from Black Twitter culture around 2015 and 2016, where it carried a relatively specific meaning: withdrawing personal support from a public figure whose behavior or statements one found unacceptable. It was, originally, a consumer and fan-culture concept. You cancel your subscription. You stop attending their concerts. You unfollow them. The power was entirely individual and the consequences were proportional to how many individuals made the same choice.

What the term describes today is considerably messier. It encompasses everything from a celebrity losing a sponsorship deal after a decade-old offensive tweet resurfaces, to an academic losing a job offer after expressing heterodox views on a politically sensitive topic, to a private individual being dogpiled by thousands of strangers after a video of them behaving badly goes viral, to a novelist facing organized pressure to have their book dropped by a publisher. These are not the same phenomenon. They involve different targets (public vs. private, powerful vs. ordinary), different mechanisms (employer pressure vs. social ostracism vs. public shaming), different underlying grievances (racism vs. sexism vs. political disagreement vs. simply being rude) and vastly different proportionality questions.

Collapsing them all under one term serves a rhetorical purpose for those who want to argue that accountability has gone too far. It also obscures the genuine cases where something has, in fact, gone too far.

"I want to be clear about what happened. I said something stupid and offensive on a podcast seven years ago. I've changed. I've apologized repeatedly and sincerely. But none of that seems to matter. Every time I get a job opportunity, someone finds the clip and sends it to the employer. I've been unemployed for two years. I'm not a public figure. I'm just a person. At what point does this end?" — r/offmychest, 31,847 upvotes

The "at what point does this end" question is one the phenomenon has no good answer for. Traditional community sanctions, including formal punishment, have always had endpoints. A fine is paid, a sentence is served, an apology is accepted and community membership is restored. Online public shaming has no such endpoint. The record is permanent, retrievable by anyone and there is no governing body with the authority to declare the matter closed. This is not a feature that serves accountability well. It is a design flaw that serves punishment in a way accountability never requires.

The Research on Public Shaming and Its Effects

Jon Ronson's 2015 book "So You've Been Publicly Shamed" was among the first mainstream accounts to document what actually happens to people at the center of pile-ons, and the results were not particularly flattering to those who argued that social accountability produces growth and change. His subjects, people who had made genuine mistakes that became viral outrages, described the aftermath in terms of PTSD symptoms: hypervigilance, social withdrawal, loss of professional identity, inability to trust others and the constant dread of the next wave.

What the research on shame more broadly suggests is that shame and guilt are neurologically and behaviorally distinct in ways that matter enormously for whether they produce the outcomes accountability ostensibly seeks. Guilt, which is localized to a specific behavior, tends to motivate repair: apologizing, making amends, changing the offending behavior. Shame, which is a global negative evaluation of the self, tends to produce the opposite: withdrawal, defensiveness, denial and sometimes aggression. June Price Tangney's decades of research on shame and guilt consistently found that shame-prone individuals were more likely to engage in destructive coping strategies and less likely to take constructive action to repair harm.

Public pile-ons, almost by definition, produce shame rather than guilt. The target is not told "this specific thing you did was wrong." They are told "you are a bad person." Hundreds or thousands of strangers confirm this judgment simultaneously. The predictable psychological response is not genuine accountability but psychological self-defense, which tends to look like exactly the denial and minimization that critics of inadequate apologies find infuriating. The structure of the process reliably produces the response that those engaged in it find most enraging, which then prolongs and intensifies the pile-on, which deepens the shame response, which produces more denial.

"My therapist explained something that really shifted my perspective. She said that shame makes us want to hide and disappear. Guilt makes us want to fix things. So when we shame someone publicly we're essentially doing the thing most likely to prevent them from actually doing better. We're satisfying our own anger, not creating change." — r/psychology, 28,934 upvotes

This does not mean that public accountability is never appropriate. There are figures whose power has insulated them from private accountability for years or decades, and public pressure is sometimes the only mechanism capable of forcing institutional response. The Harvey Weinstein case is the paradigm here: journalists and victims had tried for years to address his behavior through private channels and those channels were systematically blocked. The public reckoning of #MeToo accomplished what nothing else had. That is a legitimate use of collective social pressure.

The problem is that the mechanism does not reliably distinguish between Weinstein and the random private individual who said something stupid online. The same tool that exposed serial predators has been used to destroy the careers of people who made a single joke in poor taste. A mechanism that cannot make this distinction is not functioning as accountability. It is functioning as something else.

Community Boundaries and the Norm Enforcement Function

Sociologists have long understood that every community requires some mechanism for enforcing its norms, and that the expulsion of norm violators serves a function beyond simple punishment. Émile Durkheim argued in "The Division of Labour in Society" that the collective emotional response to deviance, what he called "collective effervescence" in its positive form, serves to reinforce shared values and strengthen group cohesion. When a community responds strongly to a norm violation, it is not only sanctioning the offender but also communicating to all members what the community stands for and how seriously those values are held.

This is a real and important function. Online communities have genuine values worth defending. The vigorous response to racist content, harassment, sexual exploitation of minors, and other clear violations serves this norm-reinforcement function legitimately. The problem arises when the mechanism designed for genuine violations gets triggered for much smaller transgressions, because the psychological and social mechanisms don't automatically scale down. The same amount of collective fury gets mobilized for a clumsy tweet as for predatory behavior, because the engagement dynamics of social media platforms amplify outrage consistently regardless of its underlying cause.

Kai Erikson's classic 1966 study "Wayward Puritans" tracked deviance prosecution rates in the Puritan colonies and found something counterintuitive: the rate of deviance prosecution stayed remarkably stable even as the population's behavior changed, because communities need a certain rate of boundary enforcement to maintain their sense of collective identity. If major violations decrease, attention turns to minor ones. The threshold for what counts as a boundary-threatening transgression adjusts downward to maintain the supply of cases that serve the norm-reinforcement function.

This is a genuinely concerning dynamic in online communities. As social media platforms have become larger and more sophisticated at surfacing outrage-provoking content, and as the mechanisms of pile-on have become more practiced and efficient, the range of behaviors that trigger collective condemnation has expanded dramatically. Things that would have generated mild social disapproval in a face-to-face context, a poorly worded joke, an opinion that's out of step with the group's current consensus, a defensible-but-unpopular position on a contested ethical question, can now generate thousands of responses calling for professional consequences.

The Power Asymmetry Problem

One of the strongest empirical objections to the claim that cancel culture has gone too far is the observation that the most prominent alleged victims are themselves powerful people. A senator who claims to be "canceled" for their views has a Senate seat. A cable news commentator who claims to have been silenced has a national television audience. These are not people whose voices have been suppressed. They are people who have faced criticism, sometimes intense criticism, and experienced it as persecution partly because they are unaccustomed to having their views challenged in ways they cannot control.

This observation is important and largely correct for the most high-profile cases. But it does not address what happens further down the power ladder. The same social media dynamics that produce accountability for powerful bad actors also regularly destroy the lives of ordinary people who have no platform, no institutional support and no resources to weather an extended campaign of online hostility. The difference in impact between losing a sponsorship deal and losing a minimum-wage job is enormous, and both can be consequences of the same mechanism.

"My daughter was 19 when a video of her saying something racist got shared. She was wrong and I told her that. She apologized, genuinely, and she's spent years since trying to make it right. But she's 26 now and she still can't get a professional job in her field. Every interview she gets, someone finds the video and sends it to the company. She's not a public figure. She wasn't wielding power. She was a teenager who said something terrible and paid for it in ways that should have ended years ago." — r/parenting, 44,293 upvotes

The power asymmetry runs in a more complicated direction than the standard defenses of cancel culture acknowledge. It is true that powerful people have resources to survive pile-ons that ordinary people lack. It is also true that the targets of pile-ons are overwhelmingly not powerful people. They are people who happened to be in the wrong place, said the wrong thing in a context that became visible, or made an enemy who had more social media leverage than they did. The mechanism is indiscriminate in a way that is difficult to reconcile with any serious account of accountability as a value.

What Communities Actually Need to Handle Wrongdoing

The most useful frame for thinking about what cancel culture gets wrong is not "free speech vs. accountability" but "what do communities actually need when someone causes harm within them?" This is a question restorative justice practitioners have spent decades developing answers to, and their answers look very different from either criminal punishment or social media pile-ons.

Restorative justice processes, which originated in Indigenous community traditions and were formalized in academic and criminal justice contexts in the 1970s and 1980s, center three questions: What harm was done? Who was affected? What would it take to repair the harm? These questions require the participation of the person who caused harm, the people who were harmed and often the broader community. They are time-consuming, emotionally demanding and require good faith from everyone involved. They do not scale easily.

What they produce, when they work, is substantially better than punishment by almost any measure the research examines. Victims report higher satisfaction with restorative processes than with criminal prosecution. Recidivism rates are lower. Community relationships are repaired rather than permanently damaged. The person who caused harm typically develops a more genuine understanding of the impact of their behavior, because they are required to encounter, face-to-face, the people they hurt.

None of this is possible in the context of a Twitter pile-on. The person who caused harm is not encountering the people they hurt. They are encountering an anonymous mass of strangers, most of whom have no personal stake in the matter and are participating for reasons that have more to do with their own emotional needs, whether outrage, righteous solidarity or the pleasures of group condemnation, than with the repair of any specific harm. The "victim" in most of these cases is not a specific individual but an abstraction: a community, a value, an identity category.

This is not to say that diffuse social harms are not real or that only harms to identifiable individuals count. It is to say that the remedy for diffuse social harm is not the same as the remedy for specific interpersonal harm, and that deploying interpersonal harm mechanisms against diffuse social harms tends to produce bad outcomes while generating an emotionally satisfying sense of having done something.

The Online Disinhibition Effect and Who Participates

Psychologist John Suler identified the "online disinhibition effect" in a 2004 paper that has become something of a foundational text for understanding online behavior. He documented two forms: benign disinhibition, in which people share personal difficulties, extend generosity or express vulnerability more easily online than in person, and toxic disinhibition, in which people behave with cruelty, aggression or recklessness they would not display face-to-face. Both effects arise from the same set of factors: anonymity, invisibility, asynchronous communication, the absence of normal social feedback cues and what Suler called "dissociative imagination," the tendency to experience online interactions as fictional or consequence-free.

What this means for pile-ons is that most people participating in them are behaving in ways they would not recognize as their own face-to-face. The person sending a threatening message to a stranger who said something offensive is very likely not someone who would say those same words to that same stranger's face. The anonymity of the online context and the social permission granted by participation in a group condemnation temporarily suspends the normal inhibitions that make coexistence possible. This produces a collective behavior that is genuinely uglier than the behavior of most of the individuals composing it.

"I participated in a pile-on once. I sent some harsh replies to someone who said something I thought was terrible. A few months later I found out more context and realized I had it badly wrong. I looked at what I'd written to this stranger and I was genuinely shocked. I would never say those things to another person in real life. I've never participated in one again and I don't think I ever will." — r/self, 19,284 upvotes

Research on who participates in online harassment campaigns consistently finds that most participants are not habitual harassers. They are ordinary people who join a specific campaign because the social context makes it feel justified and because the threshold for participation is extremely low: a reply, a retweet, a single message. This low threshold means that outrage campaigns draw in enormous numbers of people who would never describe themselves as engaging in harassment, which creates a collective harm that vastly exceeds what any individual intended to contribute.

The Epistemological Problem: What We Know When We Pile On

One of the most consistent features of high-profile cancellations is how often the initial framing turns out to be incomplete or wrong. The clip that circulates rarely includes the full context. The accusation that goes viral is frequently based on one person's account of a complex interpersonal situation. The tweet that generates fury sometimes means something different than it appears to mean out of context. This is not a rare exception to the general rule; it is frequent enough to be considered a structural feature of the phenomenon.

This matters because the people participating in a pile-on have almost always formed a confident moral judgment on the basis of extremely limited information. They know what they've seen in their feed, which is a highly edited, algorithmically selected fragment of whatever the underlying situation is. They are making strong decisions, including decisions that affect someone's livelihood and psychological wellbeing, on the basis of information whose accuracy they have no way to verify.

The epistemological humility required to say "I don't know enough about this situation to participate in public condemnation of this person" is in direct tension with the social dynamics of online outrage, which reward immediate, strong and unambiguous takes. The person who tweets "we don't have all the facts yet" is not rewarded with engagement. The person who tweets the most cutting condemnation of the person who is currently the target is. The platform incentive structure reliably selects for confident wrongness over cautious correctness.

When Accountability Is Genuine and Necessary

None of the above is an argument that public accountability for powerful bad actors is wrong or that the post-2017 reckoning in various industries was a mistake. It wasn't. Countless people in film, media, academia, politics and other sectors had been protected by institutional structures that prioritized their continued functioning over the wellbeing of the people they harmed. It required the cumulative weight of many public accounts and the collective pressure of public attention to break through those protections in ways that private channels had failed to accomplish for years or decades.

The useful distinction, which the term "cancel culture" obscures by treating all cases as equivalent, is between collective action that targets people with real institutional power who have used that power to harm others and whose institutions have failed to address it, versus collective action that targets people with no particular power, whose behavior caused diffuse rather than specific harm, who may or may not be accurately characterized by the framing that went viral, and whose punishment serves primarily the emotional needs of those doing the punishing rather than the repair of any concrete harm.

The first category is legitimate and sometimes necessary. The second is not accountability. It is a form of recreational cruelty that has been dressed in the language of accountability because that language provides social permission for behavior that would otherwise be recognized as bullying.

"The test I've started using: would the people doing this be doing it if no one was watching? Because real accountability isn't about performing your values to an audience. It's about actually changing things. A lot of what I see online is performance. People who don't care about the person who was allegedly harmed. They care about being seen to care." — r/socialjustice101, 33,718 upvotes

Community and Belonging in the Age of Instant Exile

Perhaps the deepest problem with cancel culture as a community phenomenon is what it does to the experience of belonging. Belonging requires not only inclusion but a degree of security in that inclusion. The prospect of instant exile, for a single bad statement, an opinion that becomes unfashionable, a joke that doesn't land, or simply having the wrong enemy with enough social media leverage, makes that security unavailable. Communities that cannot offer secure belonging produce members who are constantly performing their worthiness rather than genuinely participating, because the cost of being found wanting is potentially devastating and the standards by which worthiness is judged can change without notice.

This dynamic has a chilling effect that is difficult to measure but widely reported. People self-censor not because their views are wrong but because the risk of expressing them is too high. They participate in communities while carefully monitoring every contribution for anything that could be taken out of context. They avoid genuine disagreement because disagreement makes enemies and enemies can trigger a pile-on. The result is a kind of false consensus, in which everyone appears to agree because the cost of appearing not to agree is too high, and in which genuine community, which requires the capacity for honest difference and repair, cannot develop.

Brené Brown's research on belonging, developed over decades of qualitative study, distinguishes consistently between belonging and fitting in. Fitting in requires conforming to the group's expectations and suppressing the parts of yourself that don't fit. Belonging requires being accepted as yourself, including the difficult and imperfect parts. The community dynamics that pile-ons produce optimize for fitting in and make belonging functionally impossible, because belonging requires a level of psychological safety that instant-exile mechanisms eliminate.

Toward Communities That Can Actually Do Accountability

If pile-ons are not accountability, what is? The honest answer is that genuine accountability is slow, expensive, context-dependent and requires relationships that most online communities don't have. It requires someone who knows the person who caused harm well enough to have a productive conversation with them. It requires willingness to hear multiple perspectives including the perspective of the person being held accountable. It requires agreement about what repair would look like and the patience to see whether repair is actually occurring. None of this is available when the community in question is ten thousand strangers who share only the fact of having seen the same viral clip.

This does not mean online communities are incapable of norm enforcement. They clearly are. What it means is that the mechanism available to them, collective public shaming, is a blunt instrument that tends to overshoot, tends to produce shame rather than guilt, tends to operate on incomplete information and tends to serve the emotional needs of participants more than the repair of actual harm. Using it as though it were a precision tool will continue to produce the results it has been producing: a mixture of genuine accountability for a minority of serious cases and disproportionate destruction in the majority of cases where the transgression did not warrant the response.

The communities that manage this best are ones that have developed norms around evidence and proportionality, that distinguish between the person who has caused harm and the harm they caused, that provide paths to readmission after genuine repair and that are willing to be explicit about the difference between "this person's behavior was bad" and "this person should be permanently exiled." These are communities that have thought about what they are actually trying to accomplish when they respond to wrongdoing, rather than simply allowing the available mechanism to run.

That kind of deliberate community governance is harder than a pile-on. It requires leadership, sustained attention and willingness to slow down when slowing down is uncomfortable. But it produces communities that can actually function as communities, meaning places where people can be honestly themselves, make genuine mistakes, take responsibility and continue to belong. That is, ultimately, the thing communities are for. The version of accountability that makes that impossible is not protecting community. It is destroying it.
