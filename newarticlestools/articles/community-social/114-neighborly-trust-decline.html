Why We Don't Know Our Neighbors Anymore: The Collapse of Neighborly Trust

A 2023 Pew Research survey found that only about a third of Americans say they know most or all of their neighbors by name. Nearly half say they know only some of their neighbors, and roughly one in five say they know few or none. This is not primarily a feature of urban anonymity: suburban and rural Americans show comparable patterns, with neighborly familiarity declining across settlement types. The survey also found that knowing your neighbors correlates with trusting them, and trust in neighbors has declined as a component of the broader decline in generalized social trust that has been documented across several decades of polling.

The numbers are striking because the expectation of knowing one's neighbors was, until recently in historical terms, a near-universal feature of American residential life and a marker of both personal normality and community health. The neighbor who borrowed a cup of sugar, who watched the house while you were away, who you called when something seemed wrong next door and who showed up when someone needed help moving furniture was not a nostalgic fiction. It was a documented social pattern with practical welfare implications, particularly for older adults, families with young children and others whose safety nets depended substantially on geographic proximity to known and trustworthy others.

The decline of neighborly familiarity and trust is not a trivial social change. It is connected to the loneliness epidemic, to the decline of civic participation and to a measurable reduction in the informal mutual aid capacity of communities. Understanding what caused this decline requires looking at urban design, economic organization, media and the psychological dimensions of how people decide whether to invest in local relationships that require ongoing proximity management.

The Architecture of Avoidance

Physical design shapes social behavior more directly than most people realize, and the post-war suburban development pattern was not designed with neighborly interaction in mind. The single-family house with a private yard, a garage that allows the resident to move from car to home without exterior exposure and a front yard designed for aesthetics rather than occupation creates a residential environment that systematically minimizes incidental contact between neighbors. The contrast with older urban row house neighborhoods, where front stoops, narrow sidewalks and the absence of private outdoor space pushed residents into shared public space as a matter of course, is stark and measurable.

Research on the relationship between urban design and social interaction has consistently found that walkability, density and the presence of front porches and front yard activity space predict neighborly interaction and community familiarity. Studies of specific housing developments have found that units whose entrances face shared spaces generate significantly more neighbor relationships than units whose entrances face private parking areas. The design choice of whether a front door faces a street or a parking lot is not neutral with respect to the social life it produces. It is a choice that shapes interaction patterns for the lifetime of the building.

The garage door is, in this context, a meaningful symbol. The transition from houses where residents entered and exited through front doors onto sidewalks to houses where the garage door was the primary interface between home and community eliminated the incidental daily encounters that had historically maintained neighborly familiarity. The neighbor you saw every morning from your front porch as they left for work, whose patterns you knew and whose presence you registered, became a stranger whose front door you had no reason to pass and whose schedule you had no occasion to observe. The garage door suburbanized isolation as comprehensively as any design choice in modern residential development.

Residential Mobility and the Economics of Investment

Neighborly relationships require investment: time, the willingness to be known, the reciprocity of help offered and help accepted and the tolerance for the ongoing proximity of someone you did not choose but cannot easily avoid. That investment only makes rational sense if the relationship is expected to last long enough to generate returns, which means that high residential mobility systematically reduces the incentive to invest in neighborhood relationships.

Americans move more frequently than residents of most comparable countries. The average American moves approximately 11 times in their lifetime. Renters, who now constitute approximately 36 percent of American households, have much higher mobility rates than homeowners and move frequently enough that the investment calculus for deep neighborhood engagement is genuinely different. Research on neighborhood social cohesion has found that length of residence is one of the strongest predictors of neighborly familiarity and trust, with long-term residents knowing significantly more neighbors than recent arrivals, all else equal.

The economic driver of residential mobility, the housing cost crisis that has made homeownership increasingly inaccessible in high-opportunity urban areas, has created a pattern in which the people most socially invested in neighborhood life are increasingly the long-term owners of high-value properties in stable neighborhoods, while the people most likely to benefit from neighborhood social infrastructure are increasingly those moving through rental housing in cycles too short to build the relationships that would provide it. Gentrification adds a specific dimension: the long-term residents who constituted the social fabric of a neighborhood are displaced, and their replacements, regardless of their individual social intentions, arrive in a disrupted social context that has lost the institutional memory and relationship networks that made the neighborhood cohesive.

The Surveillance Substitute

Ring doorbells, Nest cameras, security systems and neighborhood watch apps like Nextdoor represent a revealing substitution for the neighborly social capital that has declined. Rather than knowing neighbors well enough to identify suspicious strangers by contrast with familiar regulars, many Americans have replaced that social knowledge with surveillance technology that performs a technically similar function, identifying unknown people in the neighborhood, through a fundamentally different mechanism that does not require relationship, reciprocity or trust.

The Nextdoor platform is particularly instructive. Launched in 2011 with the explicit aim of rebuilding neighborhood social connection, the platform quickly became associated with something quite different: the reporting of suspicious individuals, complaints about noise and property violations and the expression of anxious concerns about neighborhood safety. Research by MIT and others on Nextdoor content found that posts reporting suspicious behavior disproportionately targeted Black men, with the platform becoming a mechanism for the expression of racial anxiety in the language of neighborhood safety.

The substitution of surveillance for relationship reflects a broader shift in how safety is conceptualized in communities with low social capital. In a neighborhood where residents know each other, safety is produced partly by the social knowledge that allows unusual behavior to be recognized against a background of normalcy, and partly by the network of relationships that makes people willing to intervene or assist. In a neighborhood where residents are strangers, safety becomes a technological problem: more cameras, more data, more reporting to authorities. The technological solution to the absence of social capital does not rebuild the social capital. It manages the insecurity produced by its absence while deepening the surveillance infrastructure that, among other effects, treats the neighborhood's own residents as potential threats.

The Role of Social Trust Decline

Neighborly trust does not exist in isolation. It is one component of generalized social trust, which measures the extent to which people believe that most other people can be trusted in the absence of personal knowledge of them. Generalized social trust in the United States has declined significantly since the 1960s. The percentage of Americans who agree that "most people can be trusted" fell from approximately 55 percent in 1972 to approximately 32 percent by the 2020s according to GSS data. This is a large change in a fundamental social attitude, and its consequences extend well beyond neighborly interaction.

Robert Putnam's analysis in "Bowling Alone" linked declining social trust to the decline of civic participation, community organization membership and informal social interaction, identifying a syndrome of mutual reinforcement: declining trust reduces participation, reduced participation reduces the experiences that build trust, and declining trust further reduces participation. The causal arrows run in multiple directions simultaneously, making the syndrome resistant to single-point intervention.

Political polarization has added a specific dimension to neighborly trust decline. Research on politically mixed neighborhoods has found that neighbor relationships have become more strained as residential self-sorting by political identity has increased. The "Big Sort" that Bill Bishop documented in his 2008 book of the same name, in which Americans have become increasingly concentrated in politically homogeneous communities through residential choice, means that people who do live near neighbors with different political identities are doing so against an expectation of sameness that makes the difference feel more salient. The neighbor whose yard signs signal different political values is harder to treat as a familiar stranger whose help might be needed in an emergency than the neighbor whose political views are simply unknown.

What Neighborly Relationship Actually Provides

The welfare case for neighborly familiarity is not primarily about the warmth of community feeling. It is about the practical services that neighbor relationships provide that no commercial or institutional substitute adequately replaces. Research on social support networks has consistently found that geographic proximity, meaning the presence of known and trusted people within walking distance, is one of the most important predictors of resilience in acute crises and chronic difficulty.

During the 2021 Texas freeze, the neighborhoods where residents survived the power and water outages most effectively were those where neighborly networks allowed information to spread, where residents checked on vulnerable neighbors and where informal resource sharing compensated for infrastructure failure. The neighborhoods where the crisis was most severe were those with lower social cohesion, where residents did not know each other well enough to mobilize mutual assistance. This is not a hypothetical or theoretical claim about the value of neighborly trust. It is a documented post-crisis finding from a natural experiment in the consequences of social infrastructure quality.

For older adults specifically, neighborly familiarity provides a safety net that formal care systems and family support alone do not replicate. The neighbor who notices that the newspapers have piled up for three days, who sees that the car has not moved all week, who knocks to check whether everything is all right, provides a surveillance function not in the technical sense of monitoring but in the human sense of caring enough to notice absence. As the population ages and as older adults increasingly live alone in residential contexts where they may not know their neighbors, this function becomes increasingly critical and increasingly unavailable.

Children in neighborhoods where adults know each other and feel mutual responsibility benefit from what researchers call "collective efficacy," the shared willingness to intervene in neighborhood life to protect shared values. Research by Robert Sampson and colleagues, which became foundational in criminology and urban sociology, found that collective efficacy was a stronger predictor of neighborhood crime rates than poverty, demographic composition or housing conditions. The degree to which neighbors know each other and are willing to act on each other's behalf is, in aggregate, a structural determinant of community safety that no policing strategy fully substitutes for.

Simple Acts and Structural Change

The research on what actually rebuilds neighborly familiarity is encouragingly simple at the individual level, even if structural change requires policy. Studies of interventions designed to increase neighbor interaction have found that the most effective approaches involve creating low-stakes, repeated, casual contact rather than formal community events. Front porch sitting, sidewalk gardens, community bulletin boards and dog walking all generate incidental contact that, accumulated over time, builds the familiarity that underlies trust. Block parties and formal community events matter but are supplements to routine contact rather than substitutes for it.

The most effective individual action is also the most counterintuitive: simply saying hello to neighbors consistently, across weeks and months, without expectation of deeper engagement. Research by social psychologists including Nicholas Christakis has found that even weak social ties with known neighbors provide measurable wellbeing benefits and that the costs of initiating those ties are systematically overestimated by people who are considering whether to make the effort. The neighbor you are not sure wants to be acknowledged almost certainly does. The awkwardness you anticipate in the first exchange almost certainly will not materialize in the way you fear.

At the structural level, urban planning decisions that create pedestrian-oriented environments, that include genuinely public gathering spaces, that design residential buildings around shared rather than private circulation space and that provide the conditions for the incidental contact that precedes intentional relationship are investments in neighborly social capital that pay returns over the lifetime of the development. They are also investments that the market does not make on its own, because the social capital produced does not appear on any balance sheet. That is precisely why they require public policy rather than private initiative, and why the decline in neighborly trust is, at its root, a policy failure as much as a cultural one.
