Salary Transparency Is Coming: What It Means for Workers and Companies

21,583 upvotes · by u/paycheck_visibility · 12 min read
▲ 21,583 people found this relatable
Advertisement

For most of the history of employment, what your coworkers earned was a secret you were implicitly or explicitly forbidden from knowing. Many employers maintained informal cultures of pay secrecy enforced through social norms: discussing your salary with colleagues was considered impolite, indiscreet, or, in some workplaces, grounds for discipline. Some employers went further, including pay secrecy clauses in employment contracts that explicitly prohibited salary disclosure to colleagues, a practice that the National Labor Relations Act technically forbids in the United States but that nonetheless persisted with remarkable durability because most workers did not know the law or did not feel safe enough to invoke it.

This is changing, faster than most people realize. Colorado became the first US state to require salary ranges in job postings in 2021. New York City followed in 2022. California, Washington, Illinois and a growing list of states have passed similar legislation or are actively considering it. The European Union's Pay Transparency Directive, passed in 2023, requires all EU member states to implement legislation by 2026 requiring employers to disclose pay ranges in job postings, give employees the right to information about pay levels for comparable roles and report on gender pay gaps with statistical rigor. The movement toward pay transparency is not a fringe policy idea. It is becoming law in a widening share of the developed world.

Why Pay Secrecy Exists and Who It Benefits

Pay secrecy is not a neutral convention. It is an information asymmetry that systematically benefits employers at the expense of workers. When workers cannot easily find out what their colleagues earn, they cannot effectively evaluate whether their own compensation is fair relative to the market, relative to colleagues doing comparable work or relative to colleagues with comparable experience. This informational disadvantage weakens their bargaining position in initial salary negotiations, in conversations about raises and in decisions about whether to seek employment elsewhere.

The mechanism is straightforward. Employers typically have extensive information about what comparable workers earn, through salary surveys, industry benchmarks and internal pay data. Individual workers have fragmentary information at best: what they earn, what they have heard anecdotally from colleagues who were willing to share, and what they can infer from publicly available data sources of varying reliability. Negotiating a salary in this information environment is like playing poker against an opponent who can see your cards. The game is not fair, and the outcomes reflect that unfairness.

Research on the gender pay gap consistently finds that information asymmetry is one of its mechanisms. Women negotiate salaries less aggressively than men on average, a pattern that is partly explained by different socialization around self-advocacy and partly by the real penalty women face for negotiating assertively in contexts where the same behavior is rewarded in men. But a significant portion of the gap is also explained by women lacking the salary comparison information that would tell them they are underpaid relative to male colleagues and give them grounds for negotiation. Pay transparency removes this information disadvantage and, research suggests, does reduce gender pay gaps when it is implemented with sufficient rigor.

What the Research Says About Transparency's Effects

The empirical research on pay transparency's effects is more nuanced than either proponents or opponents typically acknowledge. A landmark 2012 study by economists David Card, Alexandre Mas, Enrico Moretti and Emmanuel Saez used a natural experiment created by a California law requiring state employees' salaries to be published online. They found that workers who discovered they were paid below the median for their occupation and employer reported significantly lower job satisfaction and higher intentions to leave. Workers who discovered they were paid above the median showed no similar change in satisfaction. The asymmetry is important: transparency did not make below-median workers happier once they knew where they stood; it made them more discontented, more likely to seek alternative employment and more aware of what they were not receiving.

From an equity standpoint, this is arguably exactly the right response: workers who are underpaid relative to their peers should be dissatisfied with that situation and should have the information required to act on it. From an employer standpoint, it means that pay transparency creates pressure to address pay inequities that might previously have remained invisible and therefore tolerated. Organizations with large internal pay disparities that cannot be justified by documented differences in performance, experience or responsibility face a genuine challenge when those disparities become visible. The transparency advocates argue this is a feature, not a bug. Employers with arbitrary or discriminatory pay practices argue it differently.

A study of a Norwegian natural experiment, where individual tax records became publicly searchable online in 2001, found that the availability of this information reduced income inequality modestly, increased job mobility among underpaid workers and improved the accuracy of wage-setting in the labor market. Denmark, where wage data has been more transparent than in most countries for several decades, shows pay gaps that are somewhat narrower than comparable economies, a pattern that researchers attribute partly to the transparency effects on wage-setting norms.

The Salary Range Posting Reality (and Its Games)

Pay transparency laws that require salary ranges in job postings have produced a predictable set of employer adaptations that vary from genuine good-faith compliance to creative technical compliance that undermines the law's intent. The most obvious adaptation is the extremely wide range: a job posting that lists a salary range of $45,000 to $145,000 is technically compliant with range disclosure requirements while providing almost no useful information to the applicant about what the job actually pays. This is not rare. Analysis of job postings in states with range disclosure requirements found a significant share featuring ranges wide enough to encompass essentially the entire possible salary distribution for the role.

A second adaptation is geographic ring-fencing: posting roles as "remote except Colorado" or similar formulations that exclude the jurisdictions with disclosure requirements, thus maintaining salary secrecy for all candidate pools not covered by transparency law. This practice became widespread and somewhat notorious after Colorado's law took effect, attracting enough public attention and mockery that some employers reversed it, but it illustrates the degree of creative resistance that transparency requirements encounter from employers who regard their pay data as proprietary competitive information.

Good-faith compliance looks different. It involves posting ranges that reflect where the organization actually intends to pay for the role, explaining what determines where within the range a specific candidate will land and maintaining internal pay structures that can survive the scrutiny that external posting requirements implicitly invite. Organizations that have genuinely engaged with pay transparency as a management challenge rather than a compliance burden consistently report that the process forced them to develop more rigorous and defensible pay practices than they had previously maintained, which they regard as a net positive for organizational health even when the initial audit was uncomfortable.

"We had to actually audit our pay structure when New York's law passed. It was uncomfortable. We found we were paying people on the same team with similar tenure very differently for reasons nobody could clearly articulate. We spent three months fixing it. It cost us some money to bring people up. It would have cost us more in turnover and morale if those gaps had become visible without us addressing them first." — u/hr_director_anon, r/humanresources · ▲ 12,847

The Morale and Teamwork Question

One of the most frequently raised concerns about pay transparency is the effect on workplace morale and collaboration when pay differences between colleagues become known. The fear is that workers who discover peers earning more will become resentful and disengaged, and that the resulting interpersonal friction will damage team cohesion and productivity. This concern is not baseless. The Card et al. study found evidence of reduced satisfaction among below-median earners, and workplace experience provides plenty of anecdotal evidence of the disruption that can follow when pay information becomes known.

However, the research suggests that the moral hazard of this concern is often mislocated. Pay disparities that produce resentment when revealed are typically pay disparities that exist for reasons that cannot be clearly justified. A pay difference that reflects documented differences in performance, seniority, specialized skill or market value tends to be accepted as legitimate when it is visible and explained. A pay difference that reflects negotiating savvy, managerial favoritism, demographic bias or historical accident tends to produce legitimate grievance when it is revealed because it reflects something genuinely unfair.

The transparency advocates' response to the morale concern is essentially this: if the pay structure cannot withstand the scrutiny of being visible to the people subject to it, the problem is the pay structure, not the transparency. Organizations that pay people fairly for defensible reasons have little to fear from their employees knowing what their colleagues earn. The moral weight of pay secrecy arguments tends to concentrate in organizations that benefit from workers' ignorance of inequities they are sustaining.

What Workers Should Know and Do

Whether or not you work in a jurisdiction with pay transparency requirements, the landscape of available salary information has changed substantially in the past decade. Platforms like Glassdoor, Levels.fyi for technology roles and LinkedIn Salary provide user-contributed compensation data that, while imperfect, gives workers access to salary comparison information at a level that simply did not exist a generation ago. These platforms have partially disrupted pay secrecy as a practical matter even where it persists as a legal or cultural norm.

The research on salary negotiation consistently finds that workers who negotiate initial offers receive higher starting salaries than those who do not, and that the benefit compounds over time because raises and future offers are typically anchored to the current compensation level. The information environment created by transparency laws and salary data platforms improves the quality of salary negotiation by giving workers a defensible factual basis for their asks rather than requiring them to negotiate from intuition or social inference about what seems normal.

In workplaces without formal transparency requirements, the National Labor Relations Act in the US explicitly protects the right of non-supervisory employees to discuss wages with colleagues. Enforcing this right requires knowing it, and knowing it is a meaningful first step toward the informal pay transparency that formal law is gradually making universal. The cultural taboo against discussing pay with colleagues is one of the mechanisms through which pay inequity sustains itself. Removing that taboo, where workers feel safe doing so, is itself a form of organizing for better compensation outcomes.
