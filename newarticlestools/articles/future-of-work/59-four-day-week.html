The Four-Day Work Week Argument: What the Trials Actually Found

22,647 upvotes · by u/workweek_watcher · 12 min read

The five-day, forty-hour work week is not a natural law. It is a specific historical artifact, the product of labor union organizing in the early twentieth century that won a reduction from the six-day, sixty-plus-hour weeks common in industrial capitalism's early decades. Henry Ford's much-cited adoption of the five-day week in 1926 was not an act of generosity; it was a calculated business decision based on his observation that worker productivity improved with rest, and that workers needed leisure time to spend money on consumer goods. The eight-hour day and five-day week were fought for, then normalized, and then gradually naturalized until they came to feel like a permanent and obvious feature of how work should be organized.

The conversation about the four-day work week is, in this sense, a familiar kind of conversation. It asks whether the current settlement is optimal or whether a different arrangement might be better for workers, for organizations and for society. What is new is the accumulation of actual experimental evidence from trials large enough to say something meaningful. Iceland, the United Kingdom, Japan, Iceland, New Zealand and a growing number of individual organizations have run structured trials of reduced-hour working, and the results are considerably more interesting and nuanced than either the triumphant headlines or the skeptical dismissals tend to capture.

The Iceland Trials and What They Found

The most frequently cited evidence for the four-day week comes from Iceland, where two large-scale trials run between 2015 and 2019 involved approximately 2,500 workers across a range of public sector organizations, representing about one percent of the country's entire workforce. The trials reduced hours from forty to thirty-five or thirty-six per week while maintaining full pay. They were evaluated by researchers from the Association for Sustainability and Democracy and the UK-based think tank Autonomy, who published their findings in 2021.

The results were striking. Productivity, measured across a range of metrics appropriate to different workplace types, remained the same or improved in the large majority of participating organizations. Worker wellbeing showed significant improvements across measures of stress, burnout, work-life balance and general health. Importantly, the improvements were not short-term novelty effects; they persisted through the duration of the trials and were maintained in the organizations that adopted reduced hours permanently afterward, which the majority chose to do. By the time the research was published, organizations representing roughly 86 percent of Iceland's workforce had either adopted or were in the process of adopting reduced-hour working arrangements.

The mechanism through which productivity was maintained despite fewer hours worked involves what the researchers called "work intensification" combined with elimination of inefficiency. Organizations participating in the trials scrutinized their meeting cultures, their administrative processes and their communication patterns. They found, repeatedly, that a substantial portion of the working day was occupied by activities of low or marginal value, particularly meetings that could be shorter, less frequent or replaced by asynchronous communication. The discipline of working within a shorter time frame focused attention and energy in ways that partly compensated for the reduced hours.

"We cut all meetings by a third. Removed standing meetings that nobody knew why we had anymore. Made email response a block-time activity rather than a constant interruption. Honestly? I do more actual work in 32 hours now than I did in 40. What I lost was the performative sitting-at-desk time that felt like work but wasn't." — u/iceland_trial_participant, r/samplesize · ▲ 14,829

The UK 4 Day Week Global Trial

A 2022 UK trial coordinated by 4 Day Week Global, the think tank Autonomy and researchers at Cambridge and Boston College involved 61 companies and approximately 2,900 workers across a range of sectors including technology, professional services, hospitality and nonprofit organizations. It was the largest coordinated four-day week trial yet conducted and followed a "100:80:100" model: 100 percent of pay for 80 percent of the time in exchange for a commitment to maintain 100 percent of productivity.

The results broadly replicated the Icelandic findings. Company revenue did not decline; in fact, average revenue across participating companies rose slightly during the trial period compared to the same period in the prior year. Employee wellbeing improved significantly on multiple measures. Burnout, stress and fatigue declined. Life satisfaction, mental health scores and physical health ratings improved. Crucially, 56 of the 61 companies that participated chose to continue the four-day week after the trial ended, and 18 of those made the change permanent immediately. This near-universal retention rate is a more meaningful endorsement than the trial results themselves, because it reflects what business leaders concluded after experiencing the arrangement rather than anticipating it.

The trial also produced some findings that the enthusiastic coverage tended to underemphasize. Not all sectors found the transition equally straightforward. Companies where work is inherently time-bound, where customer service coverage requires staffing during specific hours, where project delivery has external deadlines that cannot be rescheduled, required more significant structural adaptation than knowledge-work environments with greater schedule flexibility. Some companies found that the 100 percent productivity commitment created its own form of pressure, compressing the intensity of work in ways that offset some of the wellbeing gains. The trial was not a universal endorsement of the four-day week for all contexts; it was evidence that for a particular range of knowledge and service work organizations, the transition is viable and generally beneficial.

Japan's Experiments and What They Reveal About Culture

Japan is an interesting case precisely because it approaches the four-day week from the opposite direction of Iceland and the UK. Where Northern European workers are often already operating in relatively healthy work cultures with reasonable hours and adequate vacation, Japan is addressing one of the most extreme overwork cultures in the developed world. "Karoshi," death from overwork, is a recognized medical and legal category in Japan. Estimates suggest that thousands of deaths per year are attributable to cardiovascular events and suicide related to excessive work hours. In this context, the four-day week is not simply an optimization experiment; it is a response to a public health crisis.

Several Japanese companies, including Microsoft Japan and Panasonic, have piloted four-day schedules with interesting results. Microsoft Japan's 2019 trial found a 40 percent increase in productivity, measured by sales per employee, over the trial period. The company also found significant reductions in electricity use, printing costs and meeting time. However, subsequent reporting has noted that translating pilot results into sustained organizational change in Japanese corporate culture is considerably more difficult than the initial enthusiasm suggested. The cultural norms around visible working time, the expectation that leaving early signals insufficient commitment, and the structural reliance on informal communication through after-hours socializing all present barriers that productivity data alone does not overcome.

Japan's experience highlights something that the four-day week literature sometimes underemphasizes: the work schedule is embedded in a broader organizational and cultural context that determines its effects. Reducing hours in an environment where the remaining hours are highly efficient and where workers feel genuine permission to stop working when their hours are done is quite different from reducing hours in an environment where the cultural expectation of overwork simply compresses into the remaining four days. The four-day week is not a stand-alone intervention; it works in combination with the organizational cultures and management practices that determine how the remaining hours are experienced.

The Arguments Against and Their Merits

The objections to the four-day week are worth taking seriously rather than dismissing. The most substantive critique is the selection bias problem: organizations that voluntarily participate in four-day week trials are not a random sample of employers. They are organizations that were already interested in the experiment, probably already had relatively functional work cultures, and may have self-selected toward sectors and business models where reduced hours are most viable. The results from these organizations may not generalize to sectors with different structures, or to organizations with cultures and management approaches less conducive to efficient working.

A second substantive objection concerns workers in jobs where the work is fundamentally time-bound or where reduced staffing hours have direct service consequences. The four-day week is genuinely harder to implement in healthcare, retail, food service, emergency services and other sectors where coverage requirements dictate staffing patterns. These sectors disproportionately employ lower-wage workers who might benefit most from reduced hours, and the logistical complexity of providing four-day schedules while maintaining service coverage is real. The conversation about the four-day week sometimes proceeds as if the benefits were universally accessible when they are, in practice, more readily available to knowledge workers with schedule flexibility than to service workers whose hours are structurally constrained.

A third objection concerns the productivity claim itself. The assertion that productivity does not decline is measured over trial periods of months, in organizations that are paying particular attention to their working practices and where the novelty and enthusiasm of the experiment may be contributing to short-term intensity. Longer-term evidence, over years rather than months, and across a wider range of organizational types, is still accumulating. The confidence with which some advocates assert that the four-day week definitively maintains productivity outruns the current evidence base somewhat.

The enthusiasm in office-world articles about this subject is a little hard to take when you're a nurse who never gets to leave on time as it is. The policy conversation needs to start from the different realities of different kinds of work." — u/nightshift_rn, r/nursing · ▲ 18,392

What the Evidence Suggests About the Right Framing

The four-day week is probably best understood not as a single policy prescription but as a prompt to question the assumption that current working time arrangements are optimal and that any change from them is inherently risky. The evidence from trials is sufficient to say that for a wide range of knowledge-work and service organizations, reducing working hours to around thirty-two hours per week while maintaining full pay is feasible without productivity decline and produces significant wellbeing benefits. This is a meaningful finding that challenges the reflexive assumption that more hours always means more output.

The deeper contribution of the four-day week discussion is perhaps the audit of how time is actually used in organizations. The consistent finding that moving to reduced hours requires eliminating or shortening meetings, streamlining administrative processes and reducing low-value interruptions is not an argument uniquely for the four-day week. It is an argument for thinking seriously about how working time is organized regardless of how many days comprise the week. Organizations that have gone through the discipline of asking "what would we cut if we had fewer hours" frequently find that the answer reveals substantial inefficiency that was always present but never examined because there was no external pressure to do so.

The political economy of working time deserves more serious public debate than it currently receives. The history of working time reduction is a history of organized political pressure winning changes that were initially opposed by employers and then, in many cases, adopted as standard practice once the transition demonstrated its viability. The five-day week followed this pattern. The eight-hour day followed this pattern. There is no particular reason to think that thirty-two hours represents a natural floor of necessary working time any more than forty hours did. The evidence suggests it is a viable alternative for a substantial range of work. Whether it becomes a norm is, as these things have always been, a question about power, politics and the willingness of workers to organize around the conditions they want.
