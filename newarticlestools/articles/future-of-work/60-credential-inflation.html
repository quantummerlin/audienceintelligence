Credential Inflation Is Real: Why a Degree Buys Less Than It Used To

19,204 upvotes · by u/degree_and_disappointed · 12 min read
▲ 19,204 people found this relatable
Advertisement

There is a specific kind of disillusionment that belongs to people who did everything they were told. Studied hard. Got into college. Graduated with a degree, maybe two. Did the internships. Graduated with debt that felt reasonable because a degree was supposed to be a reliable return on investment. Then entered a labor market that asked, politely but firmly, for more. More experience. More credentials. More evidence. The job that their parents got with a high school diploma now required an associate degree. The job their older sibling got with a bachelor's now asked for a master's. The entry-level position wanted three to five years of experience. And somehow none of this was considered unusual enough to remark upon.

Credential inflation is the process by which the educational requirements for jobs increase over time without a corresponding increase in the actual skill demands of those jobs. It is distinct from the legitimate increase in educational requirements that accompanies genuine increases in job complexity. When medicine requires more training because medical knowledge has expanded, that is not inflation; that is a reasonable response to changed content requirements. When administrative assistant positions require a bachelor's degree that was not required a generation ago for roles with essentially unchanged duties, that is inflation: credentials being used as a screening mechanism rather than as a meaningful signal of job-relevant capability.

How It Happens and Why Employers Do It

Credential inflation emerges from a confluence of supply and demand dynamics that each make rational sense individually while producing collectively irrational outcomes. On the supply side, the dramatic expansion of higher education over the past several decades has increased the proportion of the workforce with college degrees substantially. In 1970, approximately 11 percent of American adults had a bachelor's degree. Today that figure is approximately 37 percent. When a signal that was once rare becomes common, its discriminating power diminishes, and employers seeking to differentiate candidates look for the next level of credentialing to serve as a filter.

On the demand side, employers using educational requirements as a proxy for general cognitive ability, work ethic, socialization and trainability find that raising credential requirements is a low-cost screening mechanism that reduces the volume of applications to a manageable level. A position that would receive five hundred applications without a degree requirement may receive fifty with one, and the employer reasons that the fifty are probably more like the kind of employee they want than the undifferentiated five hundred. This reasoning may or may not be correct in any particular case, but it is nearly costless for the employer to implement and shifts the cost entirely onto job applicants who must now obtain additional credentials to clear a filter that was not previously present.

A 2017 Harvard Business School study of degree inflation documented this pattern in detail. The study found that job postings for positions that had not previously required degrees were increasingly listing degree requirements, particularly for middle-skill jobs in fields like production, manufacturing, healthcare support and food service. Most striking was the finding that employers hiring people into these roles from within the organization, promoting existing workers rather than hiring from outside, required degrees at substantially lower rates than external postings for the same roles. This implies that the degree requirement is functioning primarily as a screening and sorting mechanism rather than as a genuine reflection of skills required to perform the job.

If I applied for my own job today, I wouldn't get an interview. Nothing about the job has changed. The credential requirement just went up because the job market got tighter and they could." — u/office_mgr_credentialed_out, r/jobs · ▲ 16,714

The Student Debt Connection

Credential inflation does not happen in a vacuum. It interacts with the economics of higher education in ways that compound its harm. When credentials are required for jobs that do not actually require the knowledge those credentials certify, people pay for those credentials partly or wholly through debt. The United States has approximately $1.7 trillion in outstanding student loan debt, a figure that reflects both the rising cost of higher education and the escalating credential requirements that make that education feel mandatory rather than optional.

The average student loan debt at graduation for borrowers in the United States is approximately $37,000, though this figure conceals enormous variation. Graduate degree holders, whose additional credentials are increasingly required for professional positions that previously required only bachelor's degrees, carry significantly higher debt loads. The argument that this debt represents a reasonable investment in human capital rests on the assumption that the credential generates a reliable earnings premium. In fields with genuine credential-skill alignment, this may be true. In fields where the degree requirement is primarily a screening artifact of credential inflation, the calculus is considerably less favorable.

The interaction of credential inflation and student debt creates a particular trap for first-generation college students and students from lower-income families. Students from wealthier backgrounds who attend more selective institutions receive a bundle of benefits from higher education that includes genuine skill development, social network formation, credential signaling and institutional prestige, each of which has independent labor market value. Students from lower-income backgrounds who attend less selective institutions receive a subset of these benefits, often primarily the credential signal. When credential requirements inflate, the credential signal loses relative value while the debt burden remains fixed. The gap between the return to education received by privileged students and the return received by less privileged students may therefore widen as credentials inflate.

What Happens to the Jobs That Get Inflated Away

A consequence of credential inflation that receives less attention than it deserves is what happens to the labor supply for jobs where degree requirements have been imposed artificially. When plumbing, electrical work, HVAC installation and other skilled trades are compared to credential-inflated office jobs with similar or lower real earnings, the economic calculus increasingly favors the trades: the training is shorter, less expensive, the debt burden is lower and in many cases earnings are comparable or superior. Several structural labor shortages in skilled trades in the United States and other wealthy countries are partly explained by the educational pipeline having been systematically redirected toward four-year college for a generation while trade apprenticeship programs were defunded or allowed to atrophy.

The cultural devaluation of non-degree work is both a cause and a consequence of credential inflation. When "going to college" is established as the expected life trajectory for any student with reasonable academic performance, and when not going to college is implicitly coded as failure or as a choice made by those without better options, the labor supply to non-college-track work is reduced in ways that create real shortages in sectors whose skill requirements are genuine and demanding but whose educational pathways do not require four-year degrees.

The irony is that many skilled trades jobs are substantially more automation-resistant than the credentialed knowledge work for which they have been implicitly traded. A plumber troubleshooting a complex drainage problem in an unusual building configuration is doing something that current AI tools cannot replicate. A copywriter producing templated marketing content is doing something they can. The credential hierarchy that positions office work above trade work is not well correlated with the durability of that work in the face of technological change.

The Experience Paradox for New Graduates

Credential inflation at the degree level has been accompanied by a related phenomenon at the experience level: the persistent requirement for prior experience in entry-level job postings. The entry-level position that requires two to three years of relevant experience is a reliable source of dark humor among recent graduates, but the structural problem it reflects is real. Employers screening for candidates who already have experience are trying to reduce training costs and hiring risk by externalizing the cost of developing early-career workers onto either previous employers, unpaid internships or the job market itself.

The internship system that has grown to fill this gap is worth examining critically. Professional internships, particularly unpaid ones, effectively require candidates to subsidize their own hiring process with their labor, in exchange for the experience credential that future employers will require. This system disproportionately advantages candidates who can afford to work for free or near-free, typically those with financial support from families, concentrating the most desirable early-career opportunities among people who need them least. The normalization of unpaid internship labor as a legitimate credential pathway is a structural feature of credential inflation that has significant equity consequences.

The combination of degree requirements, experience requirements and internship pathways creates a layered credentialing system in which the cost of entry to professional employment has risen substantially over a generation while the purchasing power of the credentials obtained has declined relative to housing costs, healthcare costs and general inflation. The generation entering the workforce today is spending more on the credentials required to compete for jobs than any previous generation and receiving, in many cases, less reliable access to the economic stability those credentials were supposed to provide.

Signs of a Correction and What It Might Mean

There are some tentative signs that credential inflation may be approaching a reversal point in at least some sectors. Several major technology companies, including Apple, IBM, Google and Tesla, publicly removed four-year degree requirements from large portions of their job postings in the late 2010s, replacing them with skills-based assessments and portfolio requirements. The skills-based hiring movement, which argues for evaluating candidates on demonstrated competence rather than educational credentials, has gained momentum in human resources circles and has been endorsed by a range of large employers.

Skills-based hiring is not a complete solution to credential inflation, and the evidence for its widespread adoption in practice, as opposed to in stated policy, is mixed. Credential requirements are deeply embedded in applicant tracking systems, hiring manager preferences and organizational habit, and announcing a shift away from degree requirements while leaving those systems unchanged produces cosmetic rather than substantive change. However, the direction of movement in at least some large employers is worth noting as evidence that credential inflation is not simply a one-way ratchet.

The broader structural question is whether the labor market can find its way to a more honest relationship between educational credentials and job requirements, where credentials signal relevant capability rather than functioning primarily as filters that shift sorting costs onto candidates and amplify existing inequalities. This would require employers willing to invest in assessment systems that actually measure job-relevant skills, educational institutions willing to articulate clearly what they develop rather than resting on credential prestige, and policy environments that expand access to affordable skills development pathways that do not require four-year degree debt. None of these changes are impossible. All of them are currently moving more slowly than the credential inflation they would need to reverse.
