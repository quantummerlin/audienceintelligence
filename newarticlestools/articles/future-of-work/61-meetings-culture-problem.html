The Meetings Culture Problem: Why We Can't Stop Having Them

26,118 upvotes · by u/calendar_hostage · 11 min read
▲ 26,118 people found this relatable
Advertisement

At some point in the evolution of the modern knowledge workplace, meetings stopped being a tool and became a culture. The distinction matters. A tool is something you pick up when you need it and put down when you do not. A culture is something you inhabit, something that shapes your behavior and your sense of what is normal independently of whether it is serving you. Meeting culture is the reason your calendar is a patchwork of overlapping rectangles that leaves no contiguous block of time long enough to do anything requiring sustained thought. It is the reason you have been in five conversations today about work you have not had the chance to start. It is the reason the phrase "I could have been an email" has achieved the status of a universal truth that everyone agrees with and almost nobody acts on.

The scale of meeting proliferation is staggering when you look at it directly. Research from Microsoft's Work Trend Index found that the time employees spent in meetings increased by 252 percent between 2020 and 2022. A 2022 study by Steven Rogelberg, a professor of organizational science at the University of North Carolina who is probably the world's leading researcher on meeting science, estimated that unnecessary meetings cost the United States economy approximately $37 billion per year in unproductive time. Surveys consistently find that workers consider a third to a half of their meetings unnecessary, a figure that managers and executives report at even higher rates than their direct reports. Everyone agrees meetings are out of control. The meetings keep happening.

What Meetings Actually Do (Beyond Their Stated Purpose)

Understanding why meeting culture persists despite near-universal acknowledgment of its costs requires understanding what meetings do beyond the agenda items they ostensibly address. Meetings serve a range of organizational functions that are real even when they are rarely acknowledged explicitly.

Meetings are a visibility mechanism. In organizations where presence and participation are equated with contribution and commitment, attending meetings is a way of demonstrating that you are engaged, available and working. This function has become more significant in remote work environments where the physical visibility that once performed this function is absent. The proliferation of video calls after 2020 was partly a response to organizational anxiety about worker visibility and partly a mechanism through which managers and workers signaled mutual engagement in the absence of physical presence. The meeting replaced the open-plan office as the primary technology of visibility.

Meetings are also a decision-diffusion mechanism. Bringing multiple stakeholders into a decision-making conversation distributes accountability in ways that protect individual participants from being the sole owner of a choice that might go badly. The committee decision, the cross-functional alignment meeting, the stakeholder review: these structures spread the risk of being wrong across enough people that no single person bears full accountability for the outcome. This is often genuine risk management, particularly for decisions with significant organizational consequences. But it also produces a systematic tendency to add more participants to decisions than necessary and to hold more meetings than the decision actually requires.

"I tracked my meetings for a month. Out of about 200 hours of scheduled meeting time, I estimated I was actually necessary for maybe 40 hours. The rest was stakeholder alignment theater, status updates that could have been Slack messages and decisions that got made by two people after the meeting that could have been made before it. Nobody scheduled any of those meetings maliciously. The system just generates them." — u/meeting_auditor, r/productivity · ▲ 18,247

The Cognitive Cost That Gets Ignored

The most significant cost of meeting culture is not the time spent in meetings but what meeting fragmentation does to the time outside them. Deep work, the kind of sustained, focused cognitive effort required to solve complex problems, produce high-quality writing, write complex code or develop genuinely new ideas, requires extended blocks of uninterrupted attention. Research by Cal Newport and others on knowledge worker productivity consistently finds that the capacity for deep work is both a primary determinant of the quality and quantity of valuable output and something that degrades rapidly under conditions of frequent interruption and context-switching.

A calendar with meetings at 9am, 10:30am, noon, 2pm and 4pm does not leave four blocks of free time. It leaves four fragments of time, each too short and too psychologically bracketed by the approaching next meeting to permit the kind of immersive focus that deep work requires. Research on context-switching finds that the cognitive cost of transitioning between tasks is substantial, and that workers do not fully recover their focus after an interruption for an average of twenty-three minutes. A meeting every ninety minutes means that a worker is essentially never in uninterrupted focus; they are either in a meeting, recovering from one or preparing for the next.

The particular cruelty of this dynamic is that the work that actually requires deep focus, the work that creates the most value and that workers themselves often find most satisfying and meaningful, is continuously deferred to the margins: before 9am, after 5pm, on weekends. Meeting culture does not simply consume time. It displaces the most valuable work to the least sustainable time slots and then wonders why workers are exhausted and output quality is declining. The phrase "I'll have to do the actual work tonight" is so common in knowledge work environments that it has stopped being remarkable.

Status Meetings and the Information Theater Problem

The status meeting deserves particular scrutiny because it is both the most common meeting type in most organizations and the one with the weakest justification for synchronous attendance. A status meeting typically involves multiple people reporting on the current state of their work to an audience that could receive the same information asynchronously through a written update, a shared document or a project management tool. The synchronous format is chosen either out of habit, because the organization has always done it this way, or because the manager running the meeting values the visible participation and directional control that a synchronous format provides.

Research on what makes status meetings valuable, when they are valuable, suggests that their primary legitimate function is the opportunity for emergent discussion: the conversation that arises when one person's status update triggers a connection, question or concern from another participant that neither would have raised in isolation. This emergence is genuinely more likely in synchronous conversation than asynchronous communication. It is also, in many status meetings, rare. When the status meeting has a fixed format, a fixed attendee list and a culture in which participants report in sequence without significant cross-discussion, the emergence value is minimal and the synchronous format is primarily a visibility and accountability mechanism that could be served more efficiently by other means.

The uncomfortable implication is that many status meetings are attended by participants who are present primarily to demonstrate that they are on top of their work, in front of a manager who is reassured by that demonstration, conducting a ritual of mutual visibility that serves organizational anxiety more than organizational productivity. This is not a criticism of the individuals involved, who are responding rationally to incentives. It is a criticism of the organizational culture that generates those incentives.

"My team switched to async standups six months ago. Each person writes three sentences in Slack: what I did yesterday, what I'm doing today, any blockers. Takes two minutes. We freed up four hours a week of meeting time and the project actually moves faster because everyone can see everyone else's status without sitting through a thirty-minute call to get it. I don't understand why we didn't do this five years ago." — u/async_convert, r/agile · ▲ 14,603

Meeting Inflation and Organizational Complexity

Meeting proliferation tends to track organizational complexity. As organizations grow larger, the number of stakeholders with legitimate interests in any given decision increases, and the coordination required to ensure that relevant parties are informed and aligned grows with it. This creates a self-reinforcing dynamic: larger organizations need more coordination, more coordination requires more meetings, more meetings require more participants to ensure all relevant perspectives are included, more participants increase the likelihood of follow-up meetings to address unresolved questions, and the cycle continues.

This dynamic has been significantly amplified by the proliferation of collaboration tools that make it easier to invite more people to more things. The "add to invite" function in calendar applications, the @-mention in Slack that triggers notification, the document sharing permission that includes someone in a workflow they might not otherwise have been part of: these tools, designed to improve coordination, have in practice lowered the friction of including people in communications and meetings to the point where over-inclusion has become the path of least resistance. It is almost always easier to add someone to a meeting than to make the judgment call that their attendance is unnecessary, because the cost of making that call incorrectly is borne by the person who failed to include them, while the cost of including them unnecessarily is diffused across their entire schedule.

What Actually Works: Organizational and Individual Strategies

Meeting culture is an organizational phenomenon that requires organizational solutions, but individuals do have some agency within systems they do not fully control. At the organizational level, the interventions with the strongest evidence base involve creating explicit policies that protect uninterrupted time, not as a nice-to-have but as a structural design requirement. "No meeting Wednesdays" or equivalent blocks of protected focus time have been implemented by organizations including Asana, Shopify and numerous others with consistently reported improvements in deep work output and worker wellbeing. The value of these protections comes from their normative force: when not scheduling Wednesday meetings is the default, the friction of violating that norm falls on the person seeking to schedule, reversing the usual asymmetry.

Meeting length defaults deserve specific attention. The Microsoft Work Trend Index found that switching from sixty-minute default meeting lengths to fifty minutes, and from thirty-minute defaults to twenty-five minutes, reduced back-to-back meeting fatigue significantly and produced no measurable reduction in meeting effectiveness. Calendar software that books in round-number increments by default is an artifact of pre-digital scheduling conventions that no longer have a technical justification. Shorter defaults reduce both the amount of time spent in meetings and the tendency for meetings to expand to fill their allotted time regardless of how much time the actual content requires.

The practice of requiring a written agenda with a specific decision or outcome to be reached before scheduling a meeting, and the related practice of canceling or declining meetings where no such outcome is specified, are individually available to workers with enough organizational standing to implement them without career consequence. This standing is not equally distributed. More junior workers may not be able to decline a senior leader's meeting invitation on the grounds that it could have been an email, regardless of the accuracy of that assessment. The organizational cultures in which meeting reduction is most successfully implemented are those where the norms are set from the top and where declining or shortening unnecessary meetings is explicitly modeled and rewarded by senior leaders rather than being left to individuals to navigate alone.

The longer arc of this conversation leads back to a fundamental question about what knowledge work is for and how organizational value is actually created. If the answer is that value comes from sustained thinking, creative synthesis, careful analysis and the production of genuinely useful work, then protecting the conditions in which that work can happen, uninterrupted blocks of time, freedom from performative visibility rituals and the cognitive overhead of excessive context-switching, is not a perk or a benefit. It is a basic operational requirement that meeting culture systematically violates, and that most organizations have not yet seriously reckoned with the cost of violating.
