The Real Story Behind Quiet Quitting

19,244 upvotes  ·  by u/job_description_says  ·  11 min read
▲ 19,244 people found this relatable
Advertisement

The phrase arrived in the summer of 2022 and immediately triggered a media cycle that was almost perfectly designed to miss the point. Quiet quitting, the term used to describe workers who do their job as described and nothing more, who stop volunteering for extra projects that don't pay and stop checking email after hours and stop treating their employer's emergency as their own personal emergency, was presented by the majority of business press commentary as a generational pathology. Young workers were lazy. They lacked commitment. They didn't understand that going above and beyond was how you built a career. The quiet quitting discourse was, in large part, a moral panic dressed in management language.

What it actually was, as anyone who spent time reading what workers themselves said about it rather than what executives said about workers, was something quite different. It was a mass recalibration. Millions of workers, many of them not young at all, simultaneously noticed and named a pattern that had been building for years: they were routinely expected to contribute significantly more than their job descriptions required, their compensation reflected none of this additional contribution, and the implicit promise that exceptional effort would eventually be rewarded with proportional recognition had stopped coming true with any reliability. Quiet quitting was not workers doing less than their jobs. It was workers doing exactly their jobs, and the outrage in the business press was precisely the outrage of an industry accustomed to getting more than it paid for.

"I worked in corporate communications for eight years. I took calls on weekends, drafted documents on vacation, trained new staff while also doing my own full job, took on projects from three different teams because I was 'reliable.' I got two percent raises every year regardless of what I did. When I started doing exactly what my job description said and nothing more, my manager called it a performance issue. That reaction told me everything I needed to know about what had actually been happening." — r/antiwork, 27.3k upvotes

What the Business Press Got Wrong

The framing of quiet quitting as a worker problem rather than a management problem required ignoring a substantial body of evidence about what drives worker engagement. Decades of organizational psychology research have established fairly clearly that engagement, the discretionary effort that workers voluntarily contribute beyond minimum requirements, is not primarily a function of individual character or generational values. It is primarily a function of whether workers feel treated fairly, whether their contributions are recognized, whether they trust their management and whether they believe the implicit employment contract is being honored.

In the years leading up to the quiet quitting discourse, several of these engagement drivers had been systematically undermined. The decade after 2008 was characterized in many industries by wage stagnation despite significant productivity growth, meaning workers were generating more value per hour while receiving a smaller proportion of that value in compensation. Layoffs during the financial crisis created a pattern where the work of multiple positions was distributed among remaining workers without corresponding pay increases, and this pattern of workload intensification became normalized in ways that outlasted the immediate crisis conditions that had initially justified it.

The pandemic accelerated this dynamic in a specific way. Remote work eliminated the physical separation between work and home, meaning the hours that had previously been structurally protected by geography, the commute home, the act of leaving the office building, became porous. Work followed workers into their homes and stayed there. The boundary that had maintained some separation between employed time and personal time became entirely dependent on individual discipline rather than structural reality, and employers in many cases took advantage of this accessibility rather than establishing new norms that respected it.

The Invisible Second Job

One of the most useful framings for understanding what quiet quitting was actually a response to comes from organizational researchers who have studied "organizational citizenship behavior," the academic term for all the work that employees do beyond their formal job requirements that nonetheless makes organizations function. This includes mentoring colleagues, staying late to help with a crisis, taking on projects outside your role, volunteering for committees, orienting new employees, troubleshooting problems that technically belong to another team and the dozens of other contributions that keep an organization running but that don't appear in anyone's job description.

Research on organizational citizenship behavior has consistently found that it is unequally distributed within organizations, with highly conscientious, senior and female employees carrying significantly more of it than their colleagues, and that this unequal distribution is rarely acknowledged, let alone compensated. The workers performing the most organizational citizenship behavior are often the same workers passed over for promotions because they're "too valuable in their current role," the same workers who get the most additional assignments because they're "so reliable," and the same workers whose exits, when they finally happen, cause the most organizational disruption because so much informal infrastructure was resting on their invisible labor.

"When I finally left my job after seven years, three people's worth of informal responsibilities had been distributed across my role without ever being formalized or compensated. When I left, my manager asked me to write a transition document. I started writing and realized it would take months to document everything I actually did. I had been doing the job I was paid for plus roughly sixty percent of another job I was not. The 'quiet quitting' label was applied to people like me when we started noticing this." — r/work, 18.7k upvotes

The Loyalty Bargain That Stopped Being Honored

The implicit employment contract that made extra effort rational once looked something like this: employees who went beyond their formal requirements and demonstrated loyalty, flexibility and commitment would be rewarded over time with job security, promotions, above-market compensation and genuine investment in their career development. This bargain was honored with varying degrees of reliability in different organizations and different eras, but it was widely understood to be the deal.

The 2008 financial crisis accelerated a shift in this contract that had been developing for years. Mass layoffs of long-tenured employees who had believed themselves to be in the loyal-employee category demonstrated clearly that the security side of the bargain was not secure. Companies that had laid off employees with decades of service in pursuit of quarterly earnings targets had, in one stroke, communicated something important to every surviving employee: the implicit deal was not actually a deal. It was a management preference that would be honored when convenient and discarded when costly.

Subsequent years of productivity gains without wage gains communicated a second message: the extra effort was being captured as profit rather than shared as compensation. Workers who had internalized the message that discretionary effort would eventually be rewarded noticed that the reward wasn't arriving. The raises were modest and uniform rather than merit-differentiated. The promotions went to people with the right relationships rather than the best performance records. The career development investment never materialized. Over years, the evidence accumulated that the implicit contract was not being honored, and the rational response was to stop performing obligations under a contract the other party was not keeping.

Productivity, Engagement and What the Data Actually Shows

Gallup has been measuring employee engagement annually for decades. In the years surrounding the quiet quitting discourse, Gallup's data showed declining engagement across most categories of workers in most sectors. The decline was not primarily among young workers, as the generational framing implied. It was broadly distributed across age cohorts, with some of the steepest declines among mid-career workers who had been with their employers long enough to have tested the implicit contract and found it wanting.

The business press coverage of this data almost universally framed it as a worker problem requiring solutions from the worker side: workers needed to find meaning in their work, workers needed to take ownership of their engagement, workers needed to understand that engagement was their responsibility. This framing is not only incomplete but is almost exactly backward from what the organizational research suggests. Engagement is primarily a function of what organizations do, not what workers feel. Organizations with clear expectations, genuine recognition, fair compensation, competent management and reasonable workloads have high engagement. Organizations without these things have low engagement regardless of how many resilience workshops they offer or how many inspirational posters they hang in break rooms.

"My company had an all-hands meeting about declining engagement scores. The CEO talked for forty minutes about the importance of passion and caring about what we do and how lucky we are to work somewhere with such a meaningful mission. Not once did he mention compensation, workload, management quality or any of the things that actually drive engagement. We got a new set of company values printed on cards at the end. Engagement did not improve." — r/corporate, 22.8k upvotes

What the Best Organizations Did Instead

The organizations that navigated the post-pandemic labor market most successfully were not the ones that pushed back hardest against quiet quitting or the ones that demanded the most visible commitment theater from their workers. They were the ones that responded to the recalibration by honestly examining whether the implicit contract was being honored and making genuine adjustments where it wasn't.

This meant auditing compensation relative to market rates and correcting gaps. It meant examining workload distribution and addressing the structural dynamics that concentrated invisible labor on specific employees. It meant training managers in the skills that actually drive engagement: clear communication, genuine recognition, psychological safety and fair dealing. It meant creating explicit career paths so that extra effort had a visible and believable connection to advancement. None of this is complicated in principle. All of it requires sustained organizational commitment that many companies found more difficult than issuing statements about the importance of employee wellbeing.

The workers who coined and celebrated quiet quitting were not announcing that they no longer wanted to do good work or contribute meaningfully to their organizations. They were announcing that they had stopped contributing to a deal that wasn't being kept, and that they required evidence the deal would be honored before extending additional credit. That is not laziness. It is rationality. The organizations that heard that message correctly and responded to it have done better. The ones that responded with surveillance software, return-to-office mandates as control mechanisms and performative culture initiatives have, mostly, continued to lose the workers they most wanted to keep.
