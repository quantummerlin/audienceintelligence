Why Whistleblowers Need Protection: The People Who Tell Us the Truth

12 min read  |  Corporate Accountability
79,384 upvotes

In 2016 Frances Haugen was a mid-level product manager at Facebook working on civic integrity. She had joined the company believing, as she later testified, that Facebook had a "unique opportunity" to have a positive effect on society. What she found instead was a company aware that its products were causing documented harms to users, including mental health damage to teenage girls and radicalization through its news feed algorithm, and choosing to prioritize growth metrics and engagement over user welfare. When she raised concerns internally, the civic integrity team she worked on was disbanded. Haugen made a decision that few people in her position make: she copied tens of thousands of internal Facebook documents, left the company and gave them to the Wall Street Journal, to Congress and to regulators in the United States and Europe.

The documents she disclosed became the "Facebook Papers," one of the most consequential corporate whistleblowing events in recent American history. They revealed that Facebook knew its Instagram platform was harmful to teenage girls' mental health, that it had suppressed internal research showing this, that its algorithms amplified misinformation and inflammatory content because that content drove more engagement and that the company had systematically prioritized profit over the public interests it claimed to serve. Haugen testified before the Senate, before the British Parliament and before the European Parliament. Facebook disputed her characterizations but did not challenge the authenticity of the documents.

What made Haugen's case unusual was not the content of what she disclosed but the relative speed with which she was able to tell her story. Most whistleblowers do not get Senate hearings. Most face years of retaliation, legal harassment and professional destruction before, if ever, their disclosures achieve any consequence. Understanding why requires understanding the gap between the rhetoric of whistleblower protection and its reality, a gap that has allowed corporate and government misconduct to continue, in documented cases, for decades after insiders first tried to stop it.

What Whistleblowers Have Given Us

The accumulated public benefit of whistleblowing is enormous and spans virtually every domain of consequence. Without whistleblowers, many of the most significant accountability events of the past half-century would not have happened.

Daniel Ellsberg's disclosure of the Pentagon Papers in 1971, the classified history of US decision-making in Vietnam, revealed that the government had systematically deceived the public and Congress about the war's progress and prospects. The disclosure helped shift public opinion and congressional action on the war. Ellsberg was prosecuted under the Espionage Act; his case was dismissed after evidence emerged that the Nixon administration had engaged in illegal actions against him, including breaking into his psychiatrist's office.

Karen Silkwood, a Kerr-McGee nuclear plant worker who discovered evidence of safety violations and plutonium contamination at the facility where she worked, died in a car accident in 1974 while on her way to meet a New York Times reporter with documents. The circumstances of her death have never been fully explained. Her case became a symbol of both the importance and the danger of workplace safety whistleblowing.

Jeffrey Wigand, a Brown and Williamson tobacco executive, disclosed in a 1995 interview that the company had known for decades that nicotine was addictive, had manipulated nicotine levels to increase addictiveness and had suppressed internal research showing the health effects of smoking. His disclosure contributed to the Master Settlement Agreement through which tobacco companies paid $206 billion to states and acknowledged manipulating nicotine. Wigand was subjected to a sophisticated campaign of legal harassment and personal destruction by Brown and Williamson before his disclosures became public, a campaign later dramatized in the film "The Insider."

Sherron Watkins, an Enron vice president, wrote a memo to CEO Kenneth Lay in August 2001 warning that Enron's accounting practices would cause the company to "implode in a wave of accounting scandals." Her warning was not acted on; Enron collapsed spectacularly four months later, wiping out the retirement savings of thousands of employees and billions in shareholder value. Watkins was one of Time magazine's "Persons of the Year" in 2002 alongside Cynthia Cooper of WorldCom and Coleen Rowley of the FBI, a rare moment of public recognition for corporate and government accountability whistleblowers.

Edward Snowden's 2013 disclosures of NSA surveillance programs revealed that the US government had built a mass surveillance infrastructure that collected the communications metadata of essentially all Americans and much of the world without meaningful judicial oversight and in ways that the responsible officials had misrepresented to Congress. Snowden fled to Russia to avoid prosecution and remains there. The disclosures produced lasting reforms including the USA FREEDOM Act of 2015, multiple court decisions finding surveillance programs unconstitutional and a significant shift in public and legislative understanding of domestic surveillance.

The Legal Framework: Comprehensive in Theory, Inadequate in Practice

The United States has more whistleblower protection statutes than any other country in the world, covering federal employees, nuclear workers, aviation workers, financial sector employees, defense contractor employees and private sector workers in a dozen other specific contexts. The Whistleblower Protection Act protects federal employees. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act, passed after the Enron and WorldCom scandals, protects employees of publicly traded companies who report securities fraud. Dodd-Frank, passed after the 2008 financial crisis, created a robust financial whistleblower program at the SEC with significant financial rewards and strong anti-retaliation provisions. OSHA administers whistleblower protection programs under more than 25 federal statutes covering everything from environmental violations to pipeline safety to airline safety.

The practical problem is that most of these programs fail most of the people who use them. Studies of OSHA's whistleblower programs consistently find dismissal rates above 70 percent at the initial investigation stage. The Government Accountability Project, which represents whistleblowers, has found that the vast majority of federal employee whistleblowers who pursue formal complaints through the Merit Systems Protection Board do not obtain reinstatement or meaningful relief. A comprehensive 2016 study by the Government Accountability Office found that OSHA's aviation whistleblower program, one of the most active, had a dismissal rate of approximately 79 percent at the merit findings stage, with most dismissed for procedural or technical reasons rather than lack of substantive merit.

The structural failures are multiple and interacting. Most protection statutes have short filing deadlines, some as short as 30 days from the adverse action, that employees experiencing retaliation often do not know about until after they have passed. Retaliation frequently takes forms, marginalization, isolation, exclusion from meetings, reassignment to dead-end roles, that are difficult to document as formal adverse employment actions. Employers have sophisticated legal teams that can manage performance documentation, create pretextual reasons for termination and stretch proceedings over years in ways that exhaust employees who cannot afford sustained litigation. The burden of proof on employees alleging retaliation is higher than in many comparable legal contexts.

The SEC Program: A Partial Success Story

The Dodd-Frank whistleblower program at the Securities and Exchange Commission is the most significant exception to the general pattern of inadequate protection. Since its launch in 2011, the SEC Office of the Whistleblower has paid more than $1.5 billion in awards to more than 300 individuals, with awards ranging from 10 to 30 percent of sanctions collected in excess of $1 million. The program received more than 12,000 whistleblower tips in fiscal year 2022 alone. Several of the largest tips have led to enforcement actions collecting hundreds of millions or billions of dollars from financial firms.

The program's success reflects several design features that other programs lack. Financial awards create strong incentives for reporting that do not depend on the whistleblower's primary motivation being public interest altruism. Anonymity protection, allowing reports to be filed through an attorney without initially identifying the reporter, reduces the career risk of initial disclosure. Strong anti-retaliation provisions with robust enforcement have created meaningful consequences for employers who retaliate. And the SEC's institutional strength and resources mean that credible tips receive serious investigation rather than administrative dismissal.

But even the SEC program has limitations. It only covers securities fraud, leaving vast categories of corporate misconduct outside its scope. The multi-year timeline from tip to award means that employees who report in good faith may face years of retaliation and financial stress before any award arrives. Confidentiality protections can be undermined by overly specific tip details that allow employers to identify the source. And the program's financial incentive structure, while effective, has been criticized for potentially encouraging whistleblowers to wait for misconduct to reach sufficient scale to generate a large award rather than reporting early when the harm might be smaller.

Corporate Retaliation: The Reality Behind the Law

The gap between legal protection and actual experience is explained in large part by the sophistication of corporate retaliation. Large corporations facing a whistleblower employ a coordinated response that goes well beyond simple termination, which would be legally visible and legally risky. The standard playbook, documented in whistleblower advocacy research and in court records from retaliation cases, has several elements.

Managed performance reviews create a paper trail that appears to document performance problems predating the protected activity, providing a pretextual basis for termination that gives employers plausible deniability. The documentation is often genuine in the narrow sense that the employee actually received the reviews and the reviews actually reflect negative assessments, but the timing of the negative assessments' appearance, after or coinciding with the protected activity, reveals the retaliatory motivation to anyone who looks carefully at the chronology.

Legal harassment through counterclaims, threats of prosecution for taking documents or disclosing confidential information, and suits based on non-disclosure agreements serves both as punishment and as deterrence to future whistleblowers. The threat of personal financial ruin through litigation can be as effective as actual litigation. Non-disclosure agreements have become standard employment documents, and their enforceability against whistleblowers is a contested legal frontier; many NDAs explicitly attempt to prohibit disclosures to regulators, which is not legally enforceable, but employees who have not consulted lawyers may not know this.

Professional blacklisting, coordinated informally through industry networks, can make it impossible for a whistleblower to find employment in their industry even after winning a retaliation case. In highly specialized fields, the industry may be small enough that a whistleblower's story is universally known among potential employers. The value of winning a whistleblower retaliation case is substantially reduced if winning does not restore the ability to work in one's profession.

Government Whistleblowers and the National Security Problem

Whistleblowers who disclose government misconduct face a different and in some ways more severe legal environment. The Espionage Act of 1917, a World War I statute designed to prosecute spies, has been used against government whistleblowers with increasing frequency since the Obama administration, which prosecuted more people under the Espionage Act than all previous administrations combined. The Act does not provide for a public interest defense: a defendant cannot argue that they disclosed information because doing so served the public interest and the information revealed government wrongdoing. The act of unauthorized disclosure is itself the crime, regardless of the content disclosed or the motivation for disclosure.

This creates a particularly unjust situation for national security whistleblowers. The Inspector General framework for classified information provides an internal reporting channel, but Inspectors General are appointed by and serve at the pleasure of the executive branch agencies they oversee. The Intelligence Community Inspector General who received a whistleblower complaint about President Trump's phone call with Ukrainian President Zelensky in 2019 eventually transmitted the complaint to Congress after initially being blocked by the Director of National Intelligence. Other national security whistleblowers have raised concerns through internal channels and received nothing but retaliation. The internal reporting requirement is a prerequisite for protection in some statutes, but internal reporting itself frequently triggers the very retaliation the system is supposed to prevent.

Thomas Drake, a senior NSA official who raised concerns about a wasteful and legally questionable surveillance program through internal channels and eventually to the NSA Inspector General and Congress, was prosecuted under the Espionage Act and faced years of legal proceedings that the government eventually dropped. He was left with hundreds of thousands of dollars in legal debt, no job and a destroyed career. His case is regarded by whistleblower advocates as emblematic of a system that punishes accountability rather than rewarding it.

What Genuine Protection Would Look Like

Researchers, advocates and former whistleblowers who have studied the problem have identified a set of reforms that would meaningfully improve protection, though implementing them requires overcoming the resistance of organizations that benefit from the current inadequate system.

Reversing the burden of proof in retaliation claims would require employers to demonstrate that an adverse employment action would have been taken absent the protected disclosure, rather than requiring employees to prove that the protected disclosure motivated the action. This is the standard in the most effective programs, including Dodd-Frank, and reflects the informational asymmetry between employers, who control employment records, and employees, who must reconstruct the connection between disclosure and retaliation from external evidence.

Extending whistleblower protection to all forms of protected activity disclosure, regardless of internal or external channel and regardless of whether the information proves correct, would address the current framework's requirement that the underlying concern be substantiated. Employees who report in good faith based on reasonable belief should not lose protection because an investigation concludes differently, or because they chose to report externally rather than internally, or because the form of their report does not match a technical statutory definition.

Automatic stays of adverse employment actions pending investigation would prevent the current pattern of termination before proceedings begin, which reduces the practical value of eventual reinstatement orders. Providing legal support for employees who face retaliation, through funded legal representation or attorney fee shifting, would reduce the financial attrition that exhausts many whistleblowers even when the merits of their cases are strong.

The culture question is harder than any statutory change. Organizations that genuinely protect internal dissent, that treat employees who raise concerns as assets rather than threats, that investigate concerns rather than the people who raise them, produce better safety records, fewer fraud incidents and better long-term outcomes by virtually every measure. The research on "speak-up culture" in organizations is clear on this point. Building that culture requires leadership commitment that cannot be legislated but can be modeled, required and measured in ways that current accountability systems do not demand. The gap between the public value of whistleblowing and the private cost whistleblowers bear is not just a legal failure; it is a governance failure that tolerates harm we could prevent if we were willing to protect the people who try to stop it.
