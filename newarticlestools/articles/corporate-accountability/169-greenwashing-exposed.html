Greenwashing Exposed: When Corporate Environmentalism Is Just Marketing

12 min read  |  Corporate Accountability
84,293 upvotes

In 2020, BP ran a social media campaign inviting individuals to calculate their personal carbon footprints and take responsibility for climate change. The campaign was, in its way, a masterpiece: it directed public attention toward individual consumer choices and away from the fossil fuel industry's own emissions at a moment when scrutiny of that industry was intense. It was also a sequel. BP had popularized the concept of the personal carbon footprint in a 2004 advertising campaign that came with a carbon footprint calculator, a marketing tool that shifted the frame of climate responsibility from industrial emitters to individual consumers. The company that had done more than almost any other to produce the atmospheric carbon causing climate change had successfully made climate change about whether you drive a hybrid car.

This is greenwashing at its most sophisticated: not a simple lie about a specific product but a strategic framing exercise designed to redirect attention, preempt regulation and maintain the social license to continue profitable but damaging operations. It is one end of a spectrum of corporate environmental performance theater that ranges from meaningless claims on product labels to elaborate sustainability reports that obscure more than they reveal. Learning to recognize it, and to distinguish it from genuinely meaningful environmental action, has become one of the most practically important analytical skills available to anyone who cares about the planet.

The Term and Its Origins

The word "greenwashing" was coined by environmentalist Jay Westerveld in a 1986 essay about the hotel industry's practice of placing cards in rooms asking guests to reuse towels "to save the environment." Westerveld noted that the same hotels were expanding their physical plants, increasing their total environmental footprint, while generating favorable publicity from a gesture that saved them laundry costs. The term resonated because it named a dynamic that people had sensed but struggled to articulate: the use of environmental language and imagery to create an impression of environmental virtue that exceeds or contradicts actual environmental behavior.

The practice has expanded dramatically since 1986 as environmental concern has become a mainstream consumer value. A 2021 study by the European Commission found that 42 percent of green claims by companies could not be verified or were exaggerated, vague or false. A 2022 analysis by researchers at Columbia University found that 74 percent of corporate net-zero pledges from the world's largest companies were not backed by credible implementation plans. The volume of sustainability marketing has grown far faster than actual sustainability performance, creating an information environment in which distinguishing genuine commitment from performance is genuinely difficult even for sophisticated observers.

The Taxonomy of Greenwashing

TerraChoice, an environmental consulting firm acquired by UL Environment, developed a widely used framework for categorizing greenwashing tactics in its "Sins of Greenwashing" research. The framework identified seven categories that cover most of what passes for environmental marketing.

The "sin of the hidden trade-off" involves claiming environmental virtue based on a single attribute while ignoring larger harms. "Recycled content" paper may use more water and bleach in its production than virgin fiber paper. "Energy-efficient" appliances may contain materials mined through environmentally devastating processes. The single positive attribute is real but the overall environmental profile, which requires lifecycle assessment to evaluate properly, may be no better or may be worse than the alternative.

The "sin of no proof" covers environmental claims that cannot be substantiated. "All natural" ingredients on a cosmetics label tells you nothing about whether those ingredients were sustainably sourced, whether the manufacturing process was clean or whether the packaging is recyclable. "Eco-friendly" as a product description is essentially meaningless without a standard to measure friendliness against. The Federal Trade Commission's Green Guides provide some framework for acceptable claims in the United States, but enforcement is limited and the guides have not kept pace with the sophistication of current marketing practices.

The "sin of vagueness" covers claims so broad or poorly defined that they convey nothing specific. "Green," "sustainable," "natural," "earth-friendly" and "environmentally responsible" are marketing terms, not scientific measurements. Their use is governed primarily by the marketing department's creativity and the legal department's risk tolerance, not by any consistent external standard. A product with these words on the label may be genuinely better than its alternatives or may be indistinguishable from them or may be worse on dimensions not highlighted.

The "sin of worshipping false labels" involves creating or invoking the appearance of third-party certification without genuine third-party oversight. Many "certifications" that appear on products are self-awarded or created by industry groups with no independence from the companies being certified. The proliferation of certification logos has made it difficult for consumers to distinguish rigorous third-party certifications like ENERGY STAR, Fair Trade Certified or the Forest Stewardship Council from wholly invented badges that exist only to create an impression of independent validation.

I spent two hours trying to figure out if a jacket I liked was actually "sustainable" as the brand claimed. Their sustainability page was beautifully designed and completely uninformative. I found words like "commitment," "journey" and "better choices" but no data, no third-party verification, no emissions figures. It's not that they're lying. Carbon Offsets: The Alchemy of Environmental Claims

Carbon offsets deserve special attention because they have become the mechanism through which corporations make "net zero" and "carbon neutral" claims while continuing to emit greenhouse gases at scale. The basic concept is straightforward: if you cannot eliminate your own emissions, you can fund activities elsewhere that reduce emissions or absorb carbon by an equivalent amount, theoretically leaving you "net" zero. Airlines sell offset options at checkout for flights. Oil companies announce offset programs to claim carbon neutrality for their products. Companies across every sector have incorporated offset purchases into sustainability pledges.

The validity of this concept depends entirely on whether the offset actually represents real, additional, permanent carbon reduction. Research has repeatedly found that a substantial fraction of offset projects fail to meet these criteria. A 2023 investigation by The Guardian, Die Zeit and Source Material analyzed carbon credits from Verra, the world's largest carbon offset certifier, and found that more than 90 percent of its rainforest offset credits were likely "phantom credits" that did not represent genuine carbon reductions. The analysis compared areas certified as protected forests to counterfactual estimates of what would have happened without the certification, and found that the certified areas experienced essentially the same rate of deforestation as uncertified comparable areas, meaning the "protected" forest would not have been cut anyway and the offsets represented no real emissions reduction.

The additionality problem, whether the carbon reduction would have happened anyway without the offset funding, is endemic to offset markets. Renewable energy projects in countries where renewable energy is already economically competitive do not need offset funding to proceed, meaning funding them produces no additional emissions reduction beyond what would have occurred without the payment. Tree-planting projects frequently use monoculture plantations that provide little biodiversity value, may fail within a decade due to drought, disease or fire, and do not replicate the carbon sequestration capacity of mature natural forests. Carbon capture from methane production can reduce one very potent greenhouse gas while enabling continued fossil fuel extraction that produces carbon dioxide.

A growing number of climate scientists and policy experts argue that the offset market is primarily a mechanism for corporations to avoid reducing their own emissions while claiming to have addressed the problem. The net-zero pledge, which most major corporations and many governments have now made, is only as credible as the pathway to actual emissions reduction that underlies it. Pledges that reach net zero primarily through offsets rather than genuine emissions reduction are not climate solutions; they are accounting strategies.

The Fashion Industry: Fast Fashion's Green Makeover

Few industries illustrate the gap between sustainability claims and reality more vividly than fashion. The global apparel industry accounts for approximately 8 to 10 percent of global carbon emissions, more than aviation and shipping combined. It is also one of the most water-intensive and chemically intensive industries, responsible for approximately 20 percent of industrial water pollution globally. Fast fashion's business model, which depends on producing enormous volumes of inexpensive clothing designed to be worn a few times and discarded, is structurally incompatible with meaningful sustainability.

The response of major fast fashion companies to growing sustainability concern has been the creation of "conscious" lines and "sustainability collections" that represent a small fraction of total production while generating disproportionate marketing attention. H&M's Conscious Collection uses "sustainably sourced" materials in a subset of garments while the company continues to produce billions of garments annually from conventional materials. Zara, H&M and other fast fashion companies have announced various sustainability initiatives while continuing to increase total production volumes. The Norwegian Consumer Authority found in 2022 that H&M's sustainability marketing violated Norwegian consumer protection law and referred the company for potential legal action.

A 2021 analysis by the Changing Markets Foundation examined 46 sustainability claims by major fashion brands and found that 60 percent were unsubstantiated or misleading. The UK Competition and Markets Authority launched investigations into sustainability claims by ASOS, Boohoo and Asda. The European Commission proposed a Directive on Green Claims in 2023 that would require substantiation of environmental claims and ban generic "eco-friendly" labeling without evidence. The United States has no comparable proposed legislation, and enforcement of the FTC's Green Guides remains limited.

Financial Sector Greenwashing: ESG and Its Discontents

Environmental, Social and Governance investing has grown from a niche strategy to a mainstream one, with ESG-labeled funds holding trillions of dollars in assets. The premise is that investors can align their portfolios with their values by choosing funds that screen for positive environmental and social performance or avoid companies with poor records on these dimensions. The problem is that ESG ratings and classifications are produced by multiple competing rating agencies using inconsistent methodologies, with the result that the same company can receive radically different ESG scores from different raters.

Research by MIT Sloan finance professor Roberto Eccles and colleagues found that correlations between major ESG rating providers, including MSCI, Sustainalytics and Refinitiv, averaged approximately 0.54, compared to 0.99 for credit ratings from Moody's and S&P. An ESG score, in other words, depends more on which rater you ask than on the company's actual environmental and social performance. Investigations have found that some ESG funds hold significant positions in oil and gas companies, weapons manufacturers and companies with documented labor violations, on the grounds that these companies score well on some ESG dimensions even if not on others.

The Securities and Exchange Commission charged Goldman Sachs in 2022 with ESG greenwashing, finding that its asset management unit had failed to follow the ESG policies it claimed to apply to certain mutual funds. The agency has proposed rules requiring more standardized and substantiated ESG disclosures from funds marketing themselves as ESG-focused. The EU's Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation has created a more rigorous classification system, distinguishing between funds that merely integrate ESG considerations from those that have sustainability as a core objective and those that target specific environmental or social outcomes. Whether these regulatory frameworks will meaningfully reduce greenwashing in financial products or will primarily generate compliance paperwork remains to be seen.

Corporate Climate Pledges: The Net Zero Theater

More than 90 percent of the world's largest companies now have some form of net-zero climate pledge. Many have pledged to reach net zero by 2050, with ambitious-sounding interim targets. The credibility of these pledges varies enormously. The Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi) validates corporate emissions reduction targets against what is required to limit warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius and has approved targets for hundreds of companies. Targets validated by SBTi represent a meaningfully higher standard than self-declared pledges. But SBTi-validated targets are still a small fraction of corporate net-zero claims, and validation addresses the ambition of the target rather than whether the company is actually achieving it.

The most sophisticated analysis of corporate climate pledges comes from the Net Zero Tracker, a collaborative project of Oxford Net Zero, Energy and Climate Intelligence Unit, Data-Driven EnviroLab and NewClimate Institute. Their 2022 State of Climate Action report found that only 4 percent of corporate net-zero pledges met minimum quality standards for credibility, defined as having near-term targets, covering all emissions scopes including supply chain emissions (Scope 3), providing transparent reporting and not relying primarily on offsets. The vast majority of pledges were "low integrity": vague, long-dated, not covering Scope 3 emissions or relying heavily on unverified offset purchases.

Scope 3 emissions are particularly revealing. For most companies, the largest share of emissions does not come from their own operations (Scope 1) or the electricity they buy (Scope 2) but from their supply chains and the use of their products (Scope 3). For an oil company, product use is the dominant emissions source by orders of magnitude. A net-zero pledge that covers only Scope 1 and 2 emissions for an oil company is not a climate commitment; it is a promise to run clean offices while continuing to sell fossil fuels at the same or greater scale. Many corporate net-zero claims, examined carefully, have exactly this structure.

The difference between a real climate commitment and a PR exercise is whether the company is reducing its own emissions or buying its way to a "net" number. Regulatory Responses

Regulatory action against greenwashing has accelerated in recent years as the volume and sophistication of misleading environmental claims have grown. The EU has been most aggressive. The European Green Claims Directive, proposed in 2023, would require companies making environmental claims to substantiate them with lifecycle assessments and independent third-party verification before making them. Unsubstantiated generic claims like "eco-friendly," "green" and "natural" would be prohibited. The directive would also require transparency about the scope of environmental claims, preventing companies from claiming carbon neutrality for a product line when Scope 3 emissions from the same company are not addressed.

Several European countries have already taken enforcement action under existing consumer protection laws. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority issued Green Claims Code guidance in 2021 and has opened investigations into multiple sectors, including fashion, food and cleaning products. The Dutch Consumer and Market Authority fined KLM Airlines for misleading passengers about the environmental impact of carbon offset programs. Austria banned a Ryanair advertising campaign claiming the airline was "Europe's favourite low CO2 airline."

In the United States, the FTC's Green Guides, last updated in 2012, are under review for the first time in a decade. The agency has brought enforcement actions against specific greenwashing claims, including actions against companies making misleading "biodegradable" claims for plastic products, but has not addressed the broader landscape of vague sustainability marketing. State attorneys general have been more active: California's law enforcement community has pursued cases against multiple companies for misleading environmental claims, and several other states have enacted or are considering more specific requirements for substantiation of green marketing claims.

How to Identify the Real Thing

Against this backdrop, what does genuine corporate environmental commitment look like? Several indicators distinguish meaningful action from performance.

Companies with credible environmental commitments set absolute emissions reduction targets rather than intensity targets. An intensity target, such as reducing emissions per unit of revenue, can be met while total emissions increase if the company is growing. Absolute targets require actual reduction regardless of business growth. Scope 3 coverage is essential: a commitment that excludes supply chain and product use emissions is not addressing the most important part of most companies' environmental footprint.

Third-party verification by genuinely independent bodies, not industry-created or company-funded certification schemes, provides meaningful assurance. Rigorous certifications like B Corp, Cradle to Cradle, the Forest Stewardship Council and Rainforest Alliance Certified involve substantive standards and auditing. Science Based Targets initiative validation provides credible assessment of climate target ambition. These contrast with company-created "certifications" and vague claims unsupported by external verification.

Transparency about what is not yet solved is a marker of genuine commitment. Companies doing real sustainability work know where their hard problems are and discuss them openly. Companies doing sustainability theater avoid specificity and acknowledge no failures. A sustainability report that is entirely positive, with no honest discussion of persistent challenges, is more likely to be marketing than genuine accounting.

Finally, alignment between a company's public environmental positions and its lobbying and political activities matters enormously. A company that claims environmental commitment while funding lobbying against climate legislation, carbon pricing or clean energy standards is telling you through its actions what its actual priorities are. The Carbon Disclosure Project's research has found that a substantial fraction of companies claiming strong climate commitment simultaneously fund industry associations that lobby against climate policy. That contradiction is, in the end, the most reliable indicator of greenwashing: not what a company says about its values, but whether its political and lobbying behavior reflects those values when the costs of doing so are real.
