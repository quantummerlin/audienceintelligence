Regulatory Capture: When Watchdogs Become Lapdogs

12 min read  |  Corporate Accountability
73,492 upvotes

In April 2010 the Deepwater Horizon oil rig exploded in the Gulf of Mexico, killing eleven workers and triggering the largest accidental marine oil spill in history. Eleven million barrels of crude oil poured into the Gulf over 87 days, devastating fisheries, wetlands and coastal ecosystems across five states. Congressional investigations and independent commissions produced extensive post-mortems, and one finding was consistent across them: the Minerals Management Service, the federal agency responsible for regulating offshore drilling, had failed catastrophically in its oversight role. MMS had permitted BP to use a blowout preventer that its own engineers had flagged as inadequate. It had allowed industry to self-certify safety plans rather than conducting independent verification. It had exempted BP's Macondo well from environmental review under a process originally designed for routine operations. In some cases, MMS staff had literally been sharing meals, hunting trips and in a documented instance, cocaine, with the industry representatives they were supposed to regulate.

The MMS story is an extreme case of a pervasive phenomenon: regulatory capture, the process by which regulatory agencies, created to serve the public interest, come to prioritize the interests of the industries they regulate instead. The concept was formalized by economist George Stigler in a landmark 1971 paper, "The Theory of Economic Regulation," in which he argued that regulation is typically designed for the benefit of regulated industries and operated in their interest rather than the public's. Stigler's argument was deliberately provocative and overstated for effect, but the underlying dynamics he identified are real, extensively documented and of enormous practical consequence for understanding how modern economies actually work.

The Mechanisms of Capture

Regulatory capture does not typically happen through explicit corruption, though explicit corruption exists. It happens through a set of structural dynamics that do not require any individual to act in bad faith and that are, in many cases, difficult to distinguish from legitimate regulatory behavior.

The revolving door is the most widely discussed mechanism. Senior officials at regulatory agencies frequently move to the industries they regulated, and industry executives frequently move to regulatory positions. The movement is in both directions, creating dense networks of professional relationships, shared perspectives and mutual obligations across the regulator-regulated boundary. A regulator who expects to work in the regulated industry after leaving government has obvious career incentives to maintain relationships with industry and to avoid actions that would damage those relationships. An industry executive who moves to a regulatory role brings the industry's perspective, assumptions and priorities with them.

The phenomenon is not difficult to document because it is not illegal. It is routine. The five Securities and Exchange Commission chairs who served between 2005 and 2020 all came from or went to law firms or financial institutions they had regulated. The six FDA commissioners who served between 2000 and 2020 almost all moved to pharmaceutical or biotechnology positions after leaving the agency. The Federal Communications Commission has been led by former telecommunications industry executives and has produced multiple former chairs who went on to senior positions in telecom companies shortly after leaving the agency. The pattern is so consistent that observers have begun treating it as a structural feature of the regulatory system rather than exceptional behavior.

Information asymmetry is a second major mechanism. Regulators need to understand the industries they regulate in order to write effective rules, assess compliance and evaluate the claims that regulated companies make in rulemaking proceedings. In highly technical industries, including pharmaceuticals, finance, aviation and energy, that understanding is only available in depth from people who have worked in those industries. Agencies dependent on industry for technical knowledge are structurally positioned to receive information filtered through industry perspectives, use consultants with industry relationships and encounter analysis whose parameters were defined by people with industry interests. The informational dependence does not require anyone to lie; it operates through which questions get asked and which framings become the default.

The concentrated interest problem, identified by Mancur Olson in "The Logic of Collective Action" and elaborated by others in the public choice tradition, explains why regulated industries exert disproportionate influence on regulatory processes. The companies in a regulated industry face regulation as a direct, concentrated threat to their profitability, creating strong incentives for sustained political engagement. The public interest in effective regulation is diffuse, with the per-person benefit of any specific regulatory outcome too small to justify the individual cost of active participation. Industry trade associations, with full-time staff dedicated to regulatory monitoring and intervention, routinely dominate notice-and-comment rulemaking processes in which the supposed counterweights, public interest organizations and individual citizens, lack the resources to participate comparably.

The FAA and Boeing: A Case Study

The Federal Aviation Administration's relationship with Boeing during the development and certification of the 737 MAX provides a detailed contemporary case study in regulatory capture's catastrophic potential. Two crashes of the 737 MAX killed 346 people in 2018 and 2019, grounding the entire global fleet and triggering one of the most extensive aviation investigations in history. Congressional investigations and reporting by the Seattle Times, the New York Times and others revealed a certification process in which the FAA had delegated substantial oversight to Boeing employees designated as Organization Designation Authorization representatives, who were technically FAA employees but worked inside Boeing and were supervised by Boeing managers.

The system, designed to allow the FAA to certify a larger volume of aircraft than its own staff could handle, had created a structure in which Boeing was effectively certifying its own aircraft. Employees who tried to raise concerns about the MCAS stall prevention system that was ultimately responsible for both crashes were pressured by Boeing managers and felt unable to bring concerns to FAA oversight effectively because the oversight system was staffed by people embedded in Boeing's culture. The congressional report on the crashes concluded that the FAA had suffered "a culture of deference to Boeing" and had failed in its fundamental safety oversight function.

The FAA case illustrates a dynamic common to regulatory capture in safety-intensive industries: the agency becomes so focused on facilitating industry operations that its safety mandate becomes secondary. The FAA was under pressure to approve the MAX quickly to allow Boeing to compete with Airbus's A320neo. The agency had created a certification model that was efficient for high-volume approval but inadequate for independent safety verification. The result was the deadliest aviation disaster attributable to regulatory failure in recent American history.

Financial Regulation and the 2008 Crisis

The 2008 financial crisis is the most consequential modern example of regulatory capture's economic consequences. The crisis was not a natural disaster or an unforeseeable event; it was the product of specific regulatory choices made over two decades that systematically reduced oversight of the financial sector at the behest of that sector.

The story begins with the Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000, which explicitly prohibited federal regulation of over-the-counter derivatives markets. The legislation was pushed by Treasury Secretary Larry Summers, Federal Reserve Chairman Alan Greenspan and SEC Chairman Arthur Levitt over the objections of Commodity Futures Trading Commission chair Brooksley Born, who had attempted to assert CFTC jurisdiction over derivatives in 1998 and been overruled by the Greenspan-Summers-Levitt triumvirate. The unregulated OTC derivatives market grew to a notional value of more than $600 trillion by 2008, with credit default swaps on mortgage-backed securities at the center of the crisis that followed.

The SEC's decision in 2004 to allow the five largest investment banks to use mathematical risk models to determine their own capital requirements, rather than the fixed leverage limits previously in place, is a textbook example of captured rulemaking: the regulator adopting an industry proposal that benefited the industry while weakening the regulatory protection that was the regulator's fundamental purpose. Goldman Sachs, Merrill Lynch, Lehman Brothers, Bear Stearns and Morgan Stanley all subsequently leveraged up dramatically under the new rule, with most reaching debt-to-equity ratios above 30:1. Three of the five no longer existed as independent entities by the end of 2008.

The financial crisis cost approximately $20 trillion in household wealth destruction. The subsequent government bailouts transferred several trillion dollars of private financial sector losses to public balance sheets. The regulatory failures that enabled the crisis cannot be attributed to any single act of capture; they were the cumulative product of decades of regulatory philosophy that treated financial markets as self-regulating and financial sector participants as the appropriate designers of their own oversight.

Pharmaceutical Regulation and the Opioid Crisis

The FDA's handling of OxyContin's approval and post-market surveillance represents one of the most tragic examples of capture in a public health context. Purdue Pharma's 1996 application for OxyContin approval included claims about the drug's abuse potential that the FDA should have scrutinized more carefully. The approval included language on the label suggesting that OxyContin's controlled-release formulation made it less susceptible to abuse than immediate-release opioids, a claim that was medically dubious and that Purdue's sales force used aggressively to market the drug to primary care physicians who had no training in addiction medicine or pain management.

The FDA's drug approval process, which relies on clinical trial data generated and analyzed by the pharmaceutical company seeking approval, is structurally vulnerable to information capture: the agency evaluates what the applicant provides and has limited capacity to conduct independent research. Post-market surveillance of safety outcomes has historically been weak, with reporting of adverse events both voluntary for physicians and filtered through the company's own pharmacovigilance systems. Purdue Pharma was aware from early in OxyContin's market life that its drug was being widely abused; internal documents revealed in litigation showed the company tracking "pill mills" and "abusers" in sales data without reporting this information to the FDA or taking action to restrict distribution.

The FDA eventually strengthened OxyContin's labeling in 2001, required an abuse-deterrent reformulation in 2010 and tightened prescribing guidelines for opioids more broadly in the following years. Critics argue that these actions came years too late, after an epidemic of opioid addiction and overdose deaths was already well established. The opioid crisis has killed more than 500,000 Americans since 1999. The regulatory failures that enabled Purdue Pharma to market an addictive opioid to primary care physicians with misleading safety claims for years without consequence are a significant contributor to that toll.

Agricultural and Environmental Regulation

The Environmental Protection Agency's handling of pesticide registration illustrates capture dynamics in environmental regulation. FIFRA, the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide and Rodenticide Act, requires pesticides to be registered with the EPA before sale. The registration process evaluates safety data submitted by the pesticide manufacturer; the EPA has limited capacity for independent toxicological research. Chemicals remain registered and in use while the agency reviews evidence of harm, a process that can take years and typically requires the agency to overcome industry opposition supported by the manufacturer's own scientists and regulatory consultants.

Glyphosate, the active ingredient in Monsanto's Roundup herbicide, illustrates the challenge. The International Agency for Research on Cancer, part of the World Health Organization, classified glyphosate as "probably carcinogenic to humans" in 2015. The EPA reviewed the evidence and maintained its classification of glyphosate as "not likely to be carcinogenic to humans," a divergence from the IARC finding that critics attributed in part to the agency's use of studies funded by industry and its reliance on unpublished regulatory studies not subject to independent scientific review. The EPA's 2020 interim registration review reiterated its non-carcinogenic finding. California listed glyphosate as a known carcinogen under Proposition 65. Monsanto settled thousands of lawsuits from people claiming to have developed non-Hodgkin lymphoma from glyphosate exposure for approximately $10.9 billion without admitting liability.

The broader agricultural regulation picture reflects the structural influence of agricultural lobbying on both the EPA and the USDA. The Pesticide Registration Improvement Act created a system in which manufacturers pay registration maintenance fees and the EPA has service agreements committing to timely registration decisions, creating a customer service dynamic in which the regulated industry is paying for a service rather than submitting to oversight. Multiple former EPA officials have described the pressure to approve registrations and registration renewals within timeframes that do not allow adequate safety review.

Structural Reforms and Their Limits

The policy toolkit for addressing regulatory capture is reasonably well understood, even if implementation is politically difficult. Revolving door restrictions, including longer cooling-off periods before former officials can work in regulated industries, were tightened under the Obama administration's ethics pledges and weakened under subsequent administrations. The statutory foundation for these restrictions in the Ethics in Government Act and the Lobbying Disclosure Act provides a base, but enforcement is inconsistent and the cooling-off periods currently in place are shorter than critics argue is sufficient to meaningfully disrupt the career incentive structure.

Funding independence is important for agencies that currently depend on industry fees for their operating budgets. When the regulated industry funds the regulator, the customer service dynamic is structural. Shifting agency funding entirely to general revenue appropriations would reduce the direct financial dependence, though it would not eliminate other capture mechanisms and might be opposed by fiscal conservatives who prefer user-fee funding models.

Mandatory public participation in rulemaking, with funded support for public interest representation, would address the concentrated interest problem by reducing the participation gap between organized industry and diffuse public interests. The "intervenor funding" model used in some utility regulatory proceedings, where consumer advocates receive funding to participate in complex technical proceedings, has shown that meaningful public interest participation is possible if resources are provided. Federal rulemaking processes lack anything comparable.

Independent safety research, conducted by agencies or academic institutions without industry funding or control, would reduce the informational dependence that underlies much scientific capture. Requirements that regulatory decisions not rely primarily on manufacturer-submitted data, combined with investment in public sector research capacity, would change the informational environment in which regulatory decisions are made.

But the deeper challenge is political. The regulated industries that benefit from captured regulation have every incentive to resist reforms that would reduce their influence, and they can deploy substantial political resources in that resistance. Regulatory reform that threatens industry profitability will be fought as vigorously as any other threat to the bottom line. The history of attempted regulatory reform is largely a history of ambitious initiatives that were narrowed, delayed or eventually reversed by the political power of the interests they threatened to constrain. This is not a reason for resignation; it is an honest account of why the gap between the theory of democratic regulatory governance and its practice has proven so difficult to close.
