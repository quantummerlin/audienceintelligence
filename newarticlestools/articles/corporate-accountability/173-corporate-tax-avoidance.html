How Corporations Avoid Taxes: The Legal Architecture of Fiscal Escape

13 min read  |  Corporate Accountability
86,294 upvotes

In 2017 Apple held approximately $252 billion in cash overseas, more than any other American company in history. This was not because Apple had been particularly successful selling products in low-cost markets; it was because the company had structured its global operations to shift profits earned from customers in high-tax jurisdictions through entities in low-tax ones, accumulating the proceeds where tax obligations were minimal. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 offered a one-time reduced rate for repatriating overseas cash, and Apple brought back approximately $38 billion, paying roughly $38 billion in taxes on it. The rest stayed offshore for additional years. The entire architecture had been legal at every step.

Apple is not exceptional; it is exemplary. The world's most profitable corporations have devoted extraordinary resources over decades to constructing legal tax structures that allow them to enjoy the infrastructure, rule of law, educated workforces and consumer markets of high-tax developed countries while paying taxes primarily in jurisdictions with no meaningful connection to where their economic activity occurs. The gap between statutory corporate tax rates and effective corporate tax rates for large multinationals reflects not evasion, which is illegal, but avoidance, which is legal because armies of tax attorneys, accountants and lobbyists have spent decades ensuring it remains so.

The scale of this gap matters enormously for public finance. Estimates by economists including Gabriel Zucman at UC Berkeley suggest that multinational profit shifting costs governments globally approximately $650 billion in annual tax revenue. For the United States alone, the Congressional Budget Office has estimated revenue losses from corporate profit shifting in the hundreds of billions annually. This is not money that disappears; it accrues to shareholders and is not available for the public services, infrastructure investments and safety net programs that tax revenue funds.

The Basic Architecture: Transfer Pricing

The foundation of corporate tax minimization is transfer pricing: the prices that different entities within the same corporate group charge each other for goods, services and intellectual property. When a German subsidiary buys components from an Irish subsidiary within the same corporate group, the price at which that transaction occurs determines how much profit is recognized in Germany, where corporate tax rates are approximately 30 percent, versus Ireland, where the rate is 12.5 percent. Set the transfer price low and the Irish entity makes a small profit on the transaction while the German subsidiary pays more for the components, reducing its taxable German profit. Set it high and profit flows to Ireland.

Tax authorities require that transfer prices reflect the "arm's length principle," meaning they should approximate what unrelated parties would charge in similar transactions. For commodity goods with observable market prices, this constraint is meaningful. For intellectual property, which is unique, non-traded and subjectively valued, it is almost meaningless. The value of a pharmaceutical patent, a software algorithm, a brand name or a trade secret can be legitimately estimated across an enormous range, and corporations have every incentive to ensure their valuations place those assets in low-tax jurisdictions. Once the intellectual property is located in, say, the Cayman Islands, royalty payments from operating subsidiaries in high-tax countries to the Cayman Islands IP holding company can shift essentially arbitrary amounts of profit offshore.

The specific structures corporations use evolve continuously in response to regulatory changes, and their names have become a vocabulary of fiscal engineering. The "Double Irish," used famously by Google and others, routed profits through two Irish entities, exploiting a mismatch between Irish and US residency rules to create an entity that was tax resident in neither country. The "Dutch Sandwich" added a Netherlands entity to take advantage of favorable Dutch withholding tax treatment. "Check-the-Box" regulations in the United States allowed multinational subsidiaries to be treated as tax-transparent for US purposes while being treated as opaque entities in other countries, enabling elaborate mismatches. Ireland closed the Double Irish in 2014 under European Commission pressure; new structures quickly emerged to replace it.

The Irish Example

Ireland occupies a uniquely prominent place in corporate tax avoidance geography. The country's 12.5 percent corporate tax rate is the explicit foundation of its industrial development strategy, designed to attract foreign direct investment from multinationals seeking a low-rate EU base. The strategy has been spectacularly successful on its own terms: Ireland hosts the European headquarters of Apple, Google, Facebook, Microsoft, LinkedIn, Twitter, Pfizer, Johnson and Johnson and dozens of other major multinationals. The Foreign Direct Investment sector employs approximately 250,000 people in a country of five million.

But Ireland's role in the global tax avoidance architecture extends beyond the headline 12.5 percent rate. Special arrangements with individual companies have allowed effective rates far below even that. The European Commission's 2016 state aid ruling found that Apple's effective tax rate on European profits had been, through special tax rulings granted by the Irish Revenue Commissioners, as low as 0.005 percent in 2014. The Commission ordered Ireland to collect approximately 13 billion euros in unpaid taxes from Apple. Both Apple and the Irish government appealed the ruling; the European Court of Justice ultimately upheld it in 2024, but the case had taken eight years to resolve.

The Irish government's appeal of the EC ruling on its own behalf was economically rational but politically revealing. Ireland argued that it had not provided special treatment to Apple and that the Commission's ruling was legally flawed. Critics argued that Ireland's appeal illustrated the fundamental conflict between a country's interest in attracting corporate investment through favorable tax arrangements and the interest of the broader European community, and ultimately of all taxpayers globally, in a tax system that requires corporations to pay meaningful taxes where their economic activity occurs. Ireland was effectively appealing a ruling that would have given it 13 billion euros because accepting the finding would have damaged its competitive position in attracting corporate headquarters.

It's more like a sustained incredulity that we've built a system where individual people pay three times the rate of the entity that pays their salary. Intellectual Property and the BEPS Problem

The OECD's Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) project, launched in 2013 in response to growing public and political concern about corporate tax avoidance, produced a fifteen-point action plan aimed at aligning taxable profits with the location of economic activity. The BEPS project represented the most significant coordinated effort at international corporate tax reform in decades. It produced new rules for transfer pricing, hybrid mismatches, controlled foreign corporation taxation and treaty shopping, and it created country-by-country reporting requirements that require large multinationals to disclose their profits and taxes paid in each jurisdiction where they operate.

The BEPS action plan made a real difference at the margins, closing some of the most egregious mismatches and requiring more transparency than had previously existed. But its fundamental limitation was the same as all previous international tax coordination: it required voluntary adoption by sovereign states with competing interests, and states that derive competitive advantage from low tax rates have no incentive to adopt rules that would reduce that advantage. The action plan also struggled with the digital economy, where the value creation process is particularly difficult to locate geographically and where traditional nexus rules based on physical presence are easily avoided.

The intellectual property problem is structural. Under BEPS Action 5's "substance requirements," IP can only be located in low-tax jurisdictions if the company actually performs significant research and development there. This prevents the most naked form of IP location abuse, where patents are held by shell companies with no actual employees. But companies with resources can build genuine R&D operations in low-tax jurisdictions while the historical IP that generates most of the current profit was developed in high-tax countries and transferred to the offshore entity at an early stage, when its value was low and before it had become the product of a valuable business. The routine transfer pricing rules for IP still leave enormous room for minimizing the taxable profit recognized where customers actually are.

The Global Minimum Tax

The most significant development in international corporate taxation in decades was the agreement, reached by 136 countries in October 2021 under the OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework on BEPS, on a global minimum corporate tax of 15 percent. The agreement, championed by US Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen, was designed to break the race to the bottom in corporate tax rates by ensuring that no matter where profits were reported, the effective tax rate would be at least 15 percent. Under the "Pillar Two" mechanism, if a subsidiary pays less than 15 percent in a given jurisdiction, the parent company's home country can collect the difference through a "top-up" tax.

The 15 percent rate, which is substantially below most developed-country corporate tax rates, was a compromise that reflected the limited appetite of low-tax jurisdictions to accept more ambitious minimums. Critics from the left, including Zucman, argued that 15 percent was too low to meaningfully constrain profit shifting to the most extreme havens like the Cayman Islands and Bermuda, which have zero corporate tax rates. At 15 percent, significant profit shifting remains financially attractive. But supporters argued that a minimum implemented at meaningful scale, however modest, was preferable to continued indefinite escalation of avoidance mechanisms.

Implementation has been uneven. The EU has implemented Pillar Two through a directive binding on member states. Japan, Canada, South Korea, Australia and the UK have implemented domestic minimum tax legislation. The United States, whose cooperation is essential to the framework's effectiveness, has been unable to implement Pillar Two domestically because the necessary legislation, included in the Build Back Better Act, failed in the Senate. The absence of US implementation creates significant gaps in the global framework and raises questions about whether American multinationals will face top-up taxes in foreign jurisdictions where they book profits below the minimum rate.

Carried Interest and Domestic Tax Preferences

The discussion of corporate tax avoidance often focuses on international structures, but the domestic US tax code contains its own set of preferences that disproportionately benefit wealthy corporations and investors. The carried interest treatment, which allows investment fund managers to characterize a substantial portion of their compensation as capital gains taxed at 20 percent rather than as ordinary income taxed at up to 37 percent, is perhaps the most frequently discussed. Private equity and hedge fund managers who earn hundreds of millions of dollars annually pay capital gains rates on income they receive for their labor, because it is structured as a share of the profits their funds generate.

Depreciation and amortization rules allow capital-intensive businesses to write off investments rapidly, reducing taxable income in early years. Research and development tax credits, stock option deductions and a variety of industry-specific preferences further reduce effective tax rates below the statutory rate. Interest deductibility, which allows businesses to deduct interest payments on debt from taxable income while dividends on equity are not deductible, creates a systematic tax preference for debt financing that contributed to the leveraged buyout boom and the overburdening of acquired companies with debt.

The combined effect of these provisions means that the 21 percent statutory corporate tax rate is a ceiling rather than a floor. Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy analyses of Fortune 500 companies consistently find that large profitable corporations pay average effective tax rates well below the statutory rate, with dozens of companies paying zero or negative effective tax in years when they are profitable. A 2021 ITEP analysis found that 55 of the largest US corporations paid no federal income tax in 2020, a year in which those companies were collectively profitable.

The Political Economy of Tax Reform

Why does the tax code contain so many provisions that primarily benefit large corporations and wealthy individuals? The answer is not mysterious: the political process through which tax legislation is written is heavily influenced by the same interests that benefit from the provisions. The tax committee staffs of the House Ways and Means Committee and Senate Finance Committee are populated by former lobbyists and former members who will become lobbyists. Corporate and financial sector interests spend billions on lobbying specifically targeted at tax legislation. The complexity of tax law creates an information environment in which the specific provisions that generate the largest benefits for the largest donors are invisible to most voters, who focus on headline rates rather than effective rates.

The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, which cut the statutory corporate tax rate from 35 percent to 21 percent, was sold partly on the argument that the high statutory rate was driving profit offshore and that a lower statutory rate with a broader base would raise more revenue by reducing avoidance incentives. The rate cut was genuine; the base broadening was significantly less comprehensive. The international provisions, including the Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income (GILTI) tax on foreign profits, were designed with rates and exemptions that reduced but did not eliminate profit shifting incentives. The effective rate reduction for the corporate sector substantially exceeded revenue projections.

The Build Back Better Act's corporate tax provisions, which would have implemented a minimum corporate book income tax, raised the GILTI rate and partially reversed the TCJA rate cut, passed the House in 2021 but died in the Senate. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 implemented a more modest 15 percent corporate alternative minimum tax on companies with book income exceeding $1 billion, affecting a small number of the largest companies that had been paying near-zero effective rates. It also restored some GILTI rate increases. These were real changes, but well short of the more comprehensive reform that many economists argued was necessary to align the tax system with the principle that economic activity should be taxed where it occurs.

What a More Just System Would Look Like

Economists who study corporate taxation have a reasonably clear picture of what reforms would reduce tax avoidance at both the domestic and international levels, even if political implementation remains formidable.

Formulary apportionment, which would allocate multinational profits to countries based on objective factors like where sales occur, where employees work and where assets are located, would replace the current arm's length transfer pricing system with an approach that cannot be gamed through artificial IP locations or management fees to offshore entities. Under formulary apportionment, a company whose sales are predominantly in the United States would owe taxes primarily in the United States regardless of where its nominal headquarters or IP holding companies are located. The EU has been debating a version of this approach, the Common Consolidated Corporate Tax Base, for decades. It faces fierce opposition from low-tax jurisdictions that benefit from the current system.

A higher global minimum tax, closer to 21 percent as the Biden administration originally proposed rather than the 15 percent eventually agreed, would further reduce the profit shifting incentive. Full US implementation of Pillar Two, which requires domestic legislation that has not passed, would close the most significant gap in the current framework. Ending carried interest treatment for investment fund managers would close a domestic preference with no economic justification other than the political power of those who benefit from it.

Country-by-country public reporting, requiring multinationals to publicly disclose their profits and taxes in each jurisdiction where they operate, would enable public scrutiny and civil society pressure that currently cannot occur because the data is not available. The BEPS project required country-by-country reporting to tax authorities, but in most countries the reports are not public. The EU is implementing public country-by-country reporting for large multinationals; the United States is not. Sunlight, in taxation as elsewhere, is a form of accountability, and the opacity of current corporate tax disclosures allows the scale of avoidance to remain largely invisible to the citizens who bear its costs.
