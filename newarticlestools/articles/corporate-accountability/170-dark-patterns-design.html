Dark Patterns: How Design Is Used to Manipulate You

11 min read  |  Corporate Accountability
91,847 upvotes

You have tried to cancel a subscription and found yourself on a page designed to make cancellation feel dangerous, complicated or temporary. You have attempted to decline data sharing and discovered that the "decline" option requires navigating through multiple screens while "accept" is a single bright button. You have booked a flight and discovered at checkout that travel insurance and a seat upgrade were pre-selected, requiring active unchecking to avoid paying for them. You have tried to close a pop-up and found that the "no thanks" option was written as "No, I don't want to save money" or "No, I prefer to be uninformed," using shame language to pressure compliance.

These are not design accidents or unfortunate user experience failures. They are dark patterns: deliberately designed interfaces that manipulate users into taking actions that serve the company's interests rather than the user's. The term was coined by UX designer Harry Brignull in 2010, who began documenting examples on a website called darkpatterns.org that quickly accumulated hundreds of corporate specimens. The phenomenon he named has since been confirmed, categorized and quantified by academic researchers, and has attracted the attention of regulators on both sides of the Atlantic who have concluded that what began as an industry's competitive advantage has become a widespread form of consumer harm.

The Research Base

Princeton researchers Arunesh Mathur and colleagues conducted one of the most comprehensive surveys of dark patterns in e-commerce in 2019, examining approximately 11,000 shopping websites. They found that approximately 11 percent of sites employed at least one dark pattern, with the prevalence significantly higher among popular and higher-traffic sites. The most common patterns included countdown timers suggesting false urgency, claims that other shoppers were "currently viewing" the same product or that stock was critically limited, hidden subscription sign-ups embedded in one-time purchases and interface design that made opting out of marketing significantly more difficult than opting in.

A 2021 study by researchers at UC Berkeley, UC San Diego and other institutions examined cookie consent interfaces across 2,000 popular websites and found that 90 percent used at least one dark pattern in their consent flows. The most common technique was making the "accept all cookies" option visually prominent and easily accessible while burying the "reject non-essential cookies" option through multiple clicks, confusing language or absent presentation. Under the GDPR's requirement that consent be "freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous," these consent interfaces were not legally valid, but the practical enforcement of that standard has lagged far behind the scale of its violation.

The economic stakes are substantial. Research by the FTC has estimated that dark patterns in subscription sign-ups and cancellation flows generate hundreds of millions of dollars annually in unwanted charges. A study by researchers at Carnegie Mellon found that pre-checked donation boxes on fundraising pages increased average donation amounts significantly but that donors who were deceived by pre-checking reported lower satisfaction and higher likelihood of future non-giving. Dark patterns extract short-term revenue while eroding the trust on which long-term customer relationships depend, a trade-off that is rational from a quarterly earnings perspective even if it is destructive over longer horizons.

A Taxonomy of Manipulation

Researchers and regulators have developed overlapping taxonomies of dark pattern types. The most commonly cited categories illuminate both the variety of techniques and the underlying psychological mechanisms they exploit.

Confirmshaming is the practice of labeling the "decline" option in a way designed to make users feel embarrassed, stupid or irresponsible for declining. "No thanks, I don't want more sales" or "No, I prefer to overpay" are characteristic examples. The technique works because people are motivated by avoiding negative self-perception and are willing to click "accept" rather than identify themselves, even only in their own minds, with the unflattering label attached to the decline option. The manipulation is especially insidious because it is technically transparent: users can read the label, understand that it is manipulative and still feel the emotional pressure it creates.

Roach motel patterns make it easy to get into a situation and very difficult to get out. Amazon's "Prime" subscription enrollment was designed to require one click to join and multiple clicks through multiple confirmation screens to cancel. The asymmetry between enrollment and cancellation is not a design oversight; it is a calculation about how many users will abandon a multi-step cancellation flow versus how many would abandon an equivalent enrollment flow. The FTC filed a complaint against Amazon in 2023 specifically citing the difficulty of cancellation as a deceptive practice, resulting in a $25 million settlement.

Hidden costs appear at the end of checkout flows after users have already invested time in the purchase process and developed a degree of commitment to completing the transaction. Airlines are among the most practiced exploiters of this technique: a flight advertised for $89 may appear at checkout as $149 after mandatory "fees" are added, with the price increasing in stages as users progress through the funnel. The sunk cost psychology of having spent time on the booking encourages completion despite the price being significantly higher than advertised.

Misdirection uses visual hierarchy, language or flow to direct attention away from information users would want to notice. A subscription cancellation page that asks "Are you sure you want to leave?" while prominently displaying a "Stay subscribed" button and burying the "Confirm cancellation" option in small gray text is using visual hierarchy to misdirect attention. Privacy consent interfaces that make the "accept" option a large colorful button while the "manage preferences" option is gray text below the fold are using the same technique in a different context.

Disguised ads present advertising content in formats designed to resemble editorial content, user reviews or organic search results. This pattern is particularly prevalent on search engines, where sponsored results are often formatted to closely resemble organic results with only small and easily overlooked "Ad" labels. App stores have faced criticism for featuring paid placements that appear visually identical to organic editorial recommendations. Social media platforms have evolved sponsored content formats that mimic the appearance of organic user posts to the point where many users cannot reliably distinguish them.

Trick questions use confusing double negatives, ambiguous language or unexpected checkbox conventions to capture consent users did not intend to give. A pre-checked box next to text reading "Uncheck this box if you do not want to receive marketing emails unless you have opted out of receiving promotional materials" requires careful parsing to understand that unchecking means opting into marketing communications, the opposite of what most users would expect from unchecking a box.

The Psychology They Exploit

Dark patterns work because they are designed by teams with sophisticated knowledge of behavioral psychology, A/B testing infrastructure capable of optimizing for conversion at massive scale, and no legal or normative constraint against exploiting cognitive biases. The psychological mechanisms they target are not exotic or unusual; they are fundamental features of human decision-making that research in behavioral economics and cognitive psychology has documented extensively.

Loss aversion, the well-established finding that losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good, underlies urgency patterns like countdown timers, low-stock warnings and "Only 2 left!" notifications. When users believe they will lose something, access to a price, availability of a product, a time-limited offer, they make faster decisions with less deliberation. The urgency is often artificial: countdown timers reset when users return to the page, and "limited" stock claims are made for items with unlimited inventory. But the psychological pressure is real regardless of whether the underlying scarcity is.

Default effects, the well-documented tendency for people to accept default options, underlie pre-checked boxes, pre-selected subscription upgrades and pre-loaded data sharing consent. Research by Thaler and Sunstein on "choice architecture" found that defaults have enormous influence on outcomes across domains from retirement savings to organ donation. Companies exploiting default effects are using a mechanism that behavioral economists designed to help people make better choices as a tool for extracting value from people's tendency not to change defaults.

The endowment effect and sunk cost fallacy underlie the strategic placement of hidden costs at the end of checkout flows. By the time users see the full price, they have invested time and decision-making effort in the purchase. The sunk cost of that investment creates pressure to complete the transaction even when the revealed price exceeds what users would have accepted at the beginning. Companies that use price reveals at checkout know that some percentage of users who would not have started the checkout at the true price will complete it once they have invested in the process.

The Regulatory Response

Regulatory attention to dark patterns has grown significantly as the documentation of harms has mounted. The FTC published a comprehensive report on dark patterns in September 2022, identifying widespread deployment across subscription services, e-commerce and data privacy consent and signaling intent to bring enforcement actions. The report noted that dark patterns are particularly harmful to older users, low-literacy users and users with cognitive disabilities, who may be less able to identify and resist manipulative interface design.

The FTC has brought several dark pattern cases under its unfair and deceptive practices authority. The Amazon case was the most prominent, but similar actions have targeted subscription services in fitness, entertainment and software. California's Automatic Renewal Law requires that subscription terms be presented clearly and that cancellation be as easy as sign-up; the FTC has coordinated with state attorneys general in several enforcement actions using this framework. The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act has been used to pursue dark pattern cases involving children's apps and platforms that used manipulative design to encourage in-app purchases or excessive data collection.

The EU's Digital Services Act, which entered into force in 2024, explicitly prohibits dark patterns on very large online platforms, including interfaces that mislead users about their choices, manipulate users through prominent presentation of certain options over others and make unsubscribing harder than subscribing. The DSA's enforcement mechanism includes fines of up to 6 percent of global annual turnover for violations, a level that creates meaningful financial incentive for compliance rather than mere risk of nominal penalties. Several EU member states have also incorporated dark pattern prohibitions into national consumer protection laws.

The GDPR cookie banners were supposed to give users control over their data. Instead they became the most successful dark pattern deployment in history. Virtually every major website made "accept all" the path of least resistance while making "reject all" require 47 clicks through a taxonomy of advertising partners. Platform Design and Attention Capture

Dark patterns in social media and platform design operate at a different scale and with different implications than those in individual transaction contexts. While e-commerce dark patterns extract money, platform dark patterns extract time and attention, shaping behavior patterns that have implications for mental health, social relationships and political discourse at population scale.

Infinite scroll, introduced by Aza Raskin in 2006 and subsequently adopted by virtually every major social platform, eliminates the natural stopping point that pagination provides. When a feed ends, users make a conscious choice to continue viewing. Infinite scroll removes that choice point, allowing browsing to continue indefinitely without any moment of conscious decision to keep going. Raskin, who spent years publicly expressing regret for the invention, estimated that the global time cost of infinite scroll is approximately 200,000 hours of human attention per day.

Variable reward schedules, the same mechanism that makes slot machines compelling, underlie notification design on most platforms. The unpredictability of whether a given check of notifications will produce something interesting or rewarding creates a stronger behavioral drive than predictable rewards would. Platforms have been designed, either deliberately or through optimization processes that discovered the effect, to deliver notifications in patterns that maximize the frequency of checking rather than the utility of notifications to users. Former Google design ethicist Tristan Harris testified to the US Senate about these mechanisms in 2017, describing how platforms deliberately exploit the same psychological vulnerabilities as gambling machines.

The design of like and follower counts, which display social approval as a public quantified metric, creates social comparison pressure that research has linked to anxiety, depression and reduced self-esteem, particularly among adolescents. Instagram tested hiding like counts in several countries starting in 2019, finding that the change reduced anxiety for some users. Despite this, Instagram's parent Meta did not implement the change universally, having determined that visible like counts drive engagement metrics that matter to the advertising business model. The company's own research, revealed through the Facebook Papers leaked in 2021, showed that Instagram was aware of the platform's negative effects on teenage girls' body image and self-perception and chose engagement optimization over user welfare.

Design Ethics and Its Limits

The existence of dark patterns is frequently treated as a failure of design ethics, implying that the solution is better ethical reasoning by designers. This framing underestimates the structural problem. Individual designers working within companies face enormous pressure to optimize metrics that correlate with business outcomes: conversion rates, subscription retention, engagement minutes, click-through rates. A designer who argues against a dark pattern that demonstrably improves conversion is arguing against their performance metrics, their team's goals and potentially their employment. The structural incentives for dark pattern deployment are strong; the personal costs of resisting them can be significant.

Design ethics training, while valuable, is insufficient when the organizational incentives point in the opposite direction. The companies most sophisticated in their dark pattern deployment are not staffed by people who are unaware that their designs are manipulative; they are staffed by people who are executing on business strategies that have calculated the trade-off between user experience and extraction and chosen extraction. What changes behavior at scale is not individual ethical commitment but legal accountability, financial consequence and competitive dynamics that make manipulation costly rather than profitable.

The emerging regulatory framework is creating those consequences in some contexts. But the breadth and creativity of dark pattern deployment continues to exceed the pace of regulatory response. A/B testing infrastructure allows companies to test hundreds of interface variants at scale and identify the most effective manipulative designs faster than any regulatory process can identify and prohibit specific patterns. The fundamental asymmetry between the optimization capacity of large technology companies and the enforcement capacity of regulatory bodies creates a structural challenge that specific regulations, however well designed, will not fully resolve.

What would actually work is a combination of default user protection, outcome-based rather than pattern-based regulation, mandatory friction parity between enrollment and cancellation flows, independent auditing of consent interface design and, ultimately, a shift in how the digital economy evaluates success. As long as quarterly revenue from unwanted subscriptions and data oversharing is counted as success while the trust costs are externalized to future periods, companies will find ways to manipulate users within whatever specific rules are in force. The design problem is ultimately a business model problem, and the business model problem is ultimately a governance problem about what the digital economy is allowed to value.
