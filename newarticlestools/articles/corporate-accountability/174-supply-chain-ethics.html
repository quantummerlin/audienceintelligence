Supply Chain Ethics: The Hidden Human Cost of What We Buy

12 min read  |  Corporate Accountability
77,394 upvotes

On April 24, 2013, the Rana Plaza building in Savar, Bangladesh collapsed, killing 1,134 garment workers and injuring more than 2,500 others. The building had housed five garment factories producing clothing for Western brands including Primark, Benetton, Mango and others. Cracks had appeared in the building's walls the day before the collapse; a bank on the lower floors evacuated its employees, but garment workers were ordered back to their machines under threat of losing a month's wages. When the building came down, the labels sewn into the clothing being made inside became the most visible evidence in a complex chain of responsibility: a building owner who had added floors without permits, factory owners who had ignored structural warnings, a garment industry pricing structure that made cutting corners on safety financially rational, and brand companies that had outsourced their supply chains to intermediaries while maintaining plausible deniability about working conditions.

Rana Plaza became the defining event in the modern supply chain ethics debate. It forced Western consumers, brands and eventually governments to confront a question that the global outsourcing model had obscured for decades: when a company sells a product made in conditions of near-slavery or in violation of basic safety standards, who is responsible? The company that sells the product and captures most of the margin? The contractor that runs the factory? The government of the country where the factory operates? The consumers who buy the product and would pay more for assurance of safety and dignity but have no reliable way to get it? All of the above? None of them, because the chain of contracts and corporate structures is designed precisely to distribute responsibility diffusely enough that no single actor bears accountability?

The Outsourcing Model and Its Logic

The structure of global supply chains is not an accident or a historical legacy; it was deliberately constructed over several decades as a strategy for reducing labor costs while maintaining brand control and profit margins. The logic is straightforward: if a company can separate the activities where its margin is generated, design, marketing, retail and distribution, from the activities where margin is thin, manufacturing, it can outsource the thin-margin work to contractors in countries where wages and regulatory costs are lowest. The brand company maintains ownership of the product's identity without owning the means of its production.

Nike pioneered this model in athletic footwear in the 1970s and 1980s, contracting manufacturing to South Korean and Taiwanese companies and eventually to their own subsidiaries in Indonesia, Vietnam and China as wages rose in the original supplier countries. The model proved so profitable that it was adopted across industries: electronics, apparel, toys, furniture, food processing, automotive components and hundreds of other sectors. The Apple iPhone, perhaps the most studied supply chain in the world, is assembled primarily at Foxconn's factories in China, with components sourced from hundreds of suppliers across East and Southeast Asia, from a company that employs more people than most cities have residents.

The outsourcing model creates a specific accountability structure: brand companies capture most of the economic value but own none of the production assets and employ none of the workers. When labor violations or safety failures occur, the brand company's standard response, documented across dozens of cases from Nike in the 1990s to H&M and Zara in the 2010s, is a combination of expressed concern, audit review, supplier remediation plans and, when the violations are severe enough, termination of the supplier relationship. Termination sounds like accountability, but its effect is to sever the brand's connection with the violated workers rather than to remediate their situation. Workers in terminated supplier factories lose their jobs; brands find new suppliers.

The Audit Illusion

The primary mechanism through which brands have claimed oversight of their supply chains is social auditing: third-party inspections of supplier facilities conducted by auditing companies against standards set by the brand or by industry bodies like the Fair Labor Association, Business Social Compliance Initiative or SA8000. The scale of the auditing industry is substantial; global spending on supply chain auditing exceeds $50 billion annually. The evidence that auditing actually ensures compliance with labor and safety standards is considerably weaker than the investment would suggest.

Research by Dara O'Rourke at UC Berkeley and by the Worker Rights Consortium has documented systematic limitations of social auditing. Audits are frequently announced in advance, giving factories time to prepare: hire temporary workers to replace child laborers during the inspection window, coach regular workers on what to say to auditors, create false payroll records showing legal wages, temporarily repair safety equipment. Auditors from major firms including Bureau Veritas and SGS have been found to accept bribes from factory managers. Audit checklists focus on observable physical conditions that can be staged rather than on systemic management practices, worker empowerment and remediation culture that are more predictive of sustained compliance.

The Rana Plaza collapse is the most devastating example of audit failure: the factories in the building had been audited by brands that were producing there, and those audits had not identified the structural problems that made the building a death trap. A 2013 investigation by the Institute for Global Labour and Human Rights found that factory audit reports for the Rana Plaza factories had found no major violations in the years preceding the collapse. The factories were rated compliant. They killed 1,134 people.

I managed a clothing factory for twelve years. When an audit was coming, everyone knew. We had a separate set of records for the auditors. We briefed the workers on what to say. The auditors knew we knew, and some of them were grateful for the ease of it. The whole thing was a performance of compliance rather than actual compliance. Forced Labor and Modern Slavery

Beyond poor wages and unsafe conditions, supply chains in multiple sectors have been found to involve forced labor that meets the legal definition of modern slavery: work performed under coercion, debt bondage, threat of violence or confiscation of identity documents. The Global Slavery Index estimates that approximately 40 million people are in conditions of modern slavery globally, with millions more in labor conditions that do not meet the legal threshold but involve significant coercion and unfreedom.

Forced labor in seafood supply chains supplying US retailers and restaurants was documented in a landmark series of investigations by the Associated Press in 2015 and 2016. Thai fishing fleets in the Gulf of Thailand and Indian Ocean were found to be using workers recruited through deceptive labor brokers, transported to vessels from which escape was impossible and subject to physical violence and salary confiscation. The seafood caught on these vessels entered supply chains supplying major US retailers. The AP identified specific shrimp products sold at Walmart, Costco, Whole Foods and major restaurant chains as having supply chains contaminated with forced labor. The retailers expressed concern, launched supplier reviews and most did not meaningfully change their sourcing.

Xinjiang, China has become the most significant current forced labor controversy in supply chains. The Chinese government's program of mass detention and labor transfer of Uyghur and other Turkic Muslim minorities in Xinjiang affects a region that produces approximately 20 percent of the world's cotton, as well as significant shares of global polysilicon (used in solar panels) and tomatoes. Multiple investigations have found that major apparel brands were sourcing cotton from Xinjiang through supply chains involving suppliers implicated in the forced labor program. The US Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act, signed into law in December 2021, created a rebuttable presumption that goods from Xinjiang are produced with forced labor and prohibits their import unless companies can demonstrate otherwise. Demonstrating otherwise has proven extremely difficult given the opacity of multi-tier supply chains and restricted access to Xinjiang.

Child Labor and Its Complexity

Child labor in supply chains is among the most emotive and most poorly understood supply chain issues. Public discussion tends to treat child labor as straightforwardly analogous to adult forced labor, but the reality is more complicated, though no less morally concerning. In the regions where child labor is most prevalent, families often depend on children's economic contribution for survival. School systems are inadequate, inaccessible or culturally inaccessible. The alternative to a child working in a field or factory is not education but a different form of poverty. Removing children from supply chains without addressing the structural poverty that drives child labor can worsen the welfare of the children concerned.

This complexity has been documented in garment supply chain studies. When brands responded to pressure about child labor by terminating contracts with suppliers found to be using child workers, in some documented cases the children shifted to less visible and more dangerous informal work. A more effective approach, documented in programs like the Rugmark Foundation (now GoodWeave) in South Asian carpet manufacturing, combines withdrawal from child labor with investment in schooling, family economic support and community monitoring that addresses the underlying drivers.

Cocoa supply chains in West Africa, where the Ivory Coast and Ghana together produce more than half of the world's cocoa, have been the subject of sustained advocacy and industry commitments on child labor for two decades. The 2001 Harkin-Engel Protocol, signed by major chocolate companies under congressional pressure, committed the industry to eliminating the "worst forms" of child labor in cocoa by 2005. The deadline was extended to 2008, then 2010, then 2020. The 2020 assessment found that approximately 1.56 million children were still engaged in hazardous child labor in Ivory Coast and Ghana cocoa farming, a number that had increased, not decreased, from the baseline level when the commitments were made. The industry commitments produced some program investment and no meaningful reduction in the underlying problem.

Due Diligence Legislation: The New Frontier

The voluntary corporate social responsibility approach to supply chain ethics, comprising codes of conduct, social auditing and supplier engagement programs, has not produced the improvements in labor and human rights conditions that proponents promised when it became the dominant model in the 1990s and 2000s. The evidence of continued and in some cases worsening violations in major supply chain sectors has driven a shift toward mandatory due diligence legislation that requires companies to identify, prevent and remedy human rights and environmental impacts in their supply chains.

France's Loi de Vigilance, enacted in 2017, requires large French companies to establish "vigilance plans" identifying supply chain human rights and environmental risks and to implement measures to prevent and mitigate them. Companies that fail to implement adequate vigilance plans can be held civilly liable for damages resulting from harms they could have prevented. The law's enforcement has been limited by procedural obstacles, but it established the principle that brands have legal responsibility for their supply chain impacts, not just moral responsibility.

Germany's Supply Chain Due Diligence Act, effective from January 2023, requires German companies with more than 3,000 employees (reduced to 1,000 in 2024) to conduct human rights and environmental due diligence across their direct suppliers and to extend that diligence to deeper supply chain tiers when there is substantiated knowledge of risks. Companies face fines of up to 2 percent of global annual turnover for violations. The EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, adopted in 2024, creates an EU-wide mandatory due diligence framework applicable to large companies and extends civil liability for supply chain harms.

These legislative developments represent a genuine paradigm shift. They move from the voluntary CSR model, in which companies decide whether and how to address supply chain harms, to a mandatory due diligence model, in which companies are legally required to know what is happening in their supply chains and to take action. The shift is politically significant because it imposes costs on businesses that benefit from supply chain opacity and creates competitive pressure against companies that take supply chain ethics seriously by raising the floor for all competitors.

Technology, Traceability and Their Limits

A range of technological approaches have been proposed or piloted as solutions to supply chain opacity: blockchain-based product traceability, DNA fiber tracking, satellite monitoring of deforestation and forced labor indicators, and AI-driven risk mapping using satellite, trade and news data. Some of these tools have genuine potential to increase transparency in specific contexts. Fiber tracking using isotope analysis or synthetic DNA markers can verify the geographic origin of cotton or timber. Satellite monitoring can detect forest clearing in commodity supply chains in near real time. Blockchain ledgers can create immutable records of supply chain transactions.

The limitations are significant. Traceability tools can verify where a specific batch of material originated but cannot verify the labor conditions under which it was produced. A blockchain that accurately records that cotton came from a specific gin in Xinjiang provides a record of origin, not of labor conditions. More fundamentally, the supply chain opacity that allows violations to persist is partly informational and partly structural: even when information about violations is available, the financial incentives to act on it are weak when lower-cost alternatives involve similar violations and when the cost of remediation falls on suppliers rather than brands.

The deeper problem is the fundamental economics of the outsourcing model. A garment factory supplying a fast fashion brand under aggressive price pressure has minimal margin with which to pay higher wages, invest in safety equipment, employ workers on formal contracts with legal benefits, and contribute to pension and healthcare schemes. The brands that set those prices capture the margin that would make decent conditions possible. No auditing system, blockchain ledger or due diligence law will resolve the contradiction between supply chain ethics and a business model structured to extract maximum economic value from the cheapest possible production. Resolving that contradiction requires changing what we are willing to pay, what the law requires, and who we hold responsible when the distance between what we buy and how it was made narrows enough for the truth to be visible.
