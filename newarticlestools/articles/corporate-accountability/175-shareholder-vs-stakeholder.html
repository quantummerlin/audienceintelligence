Shareholder vs. Stakeholder Capitalism: Who Should Corporations Serve?

12 min read  |  Corporate Accountability
72,847 upvotes

In August 2019 the Business Roundtable, an association of CEOs of America's largest corporations, issued a statement redefined the purpose of a corporation. For more than two decades the organization had maintained that corporations exist "principally to serve their shareholders." The new statement, signed by 181 chief executives including the heads of Amazon, Apple, JPMorgan Chase, Johnson and Johnson and Walmart, declared that corporations have a "fundamental commitment" to all their stakeholders: customers, employees, suppliers, communities and shareholders. Profit and shareholder returns remained important, but they were now framed as the consequence of serving all stakeholders rather than the primary purpose of corporate existence.

The statement was greeted with a mixture of enthusiasm and skepticism that has shaped discussion of corporate purpose ever since. Advocates for change hailed it as a meaningful inflection point, a signal that the ideology of shareholder primacy that had dominated American business thinking for decades was losing its grip among the very executives who had implemented it. Skeptics noted that the statement was non-binding, contained no accountability mechanism, was issued voluntarily rather than in response to legal obligation and was followed within months by mass layoffs, supply chain labor abuses and lobbying against policies that would have served employees and communities at the expense of shareholders. Harvard Law professor Lucian Bebchuk and colleagues published an analysis finding that the signatories' governance structures and executive compensation arrangements remained unchanged after the statement, suggesting that shareholder primacy continued to govern actual corporate behavior regardless of the rhetorical shift.

This is the central tension in the shareholder versus stakeholder capitalism debate: the gap between how corporations describe themselves and how they actually operate when the interests of different stakeholders conflict. Understanding that gap requires understanding the intellectual history of shareholder primacy, the evidence about its consequences and the genuine difficulty of constructing a workable alternative.

Friedman's Doctrine and Its Influence

The intellectual foundation of shareholder primacy is Milton Friedman's 1970 New York Times Magazine essay, "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits." Friedman's argument was characteristically direct and deliberately provocative: corporate executives are employees of shareholders, and spending corporate resources on social purposes not required by law is spending shareholders' money without their consent, which is a form of theft. If shareholders want to support social causes, they can do so with their own money. If executives believe social spending serves long-term shareholder interests, they may pursue it on those grounds. But the claim that corporations have independent social responsibilities beyond profit is, in Friedman's view, a "subversive doctrine" that undermines the foundations of a free society.

Friedman's argument was not simply that corporations should maximize short-term profit; it was that the appropriate governance structure for corporations is accountability to shareholders, with social objectives pursued through democratic legislation rather than corporate discretion. His concern was partly about agency: executives spending other people's money on causes of their own choosing are exercising power without accountability. His concern was also about the proper division of social responsibility: markets allocate resources efficiently, governments address social needs through democratic processes, and conflating these functions undermines both.

The essay's influence was enormous and permeated business education, corporate governance reform and executive compensation design over the following decades. Business schools taught shareholder primacy as both descriptive of how corporations work and normative about how they should. The wave of hostile takeovers and leveraged buyouts in the 1980s was partly fueled by the argument that managers who did not maximize shareholder value were breaching their duty to shareholders and that market discipline through takeovers would correct the problem. Executive compensation shifted heavily toward stock and stock options, aligning executive incentives directly with share price. Corporate strategy became increasingly focused on earnings per share, return on equity and total shareholder return as the dominant metrics of success.

The Legal Reality: Shareholder Primacy Is Not Required by Law

One of the most important and least-known facts about shareholder primacy is that it is not, in most contexts, a legal requirement. The claim that corporate law requires managers to maximize shareholder value is pervasive in business culture and is frequently invoked as justification for decisions that harm employees, communities or the environment. It is largely false.

The landmark case most often cited for shareholder primacy is Dodge v. Ford Motor Co., a 1919 Michigan Supreme Court decision in which Henry Ford was required by the court to pay dividends to minority shareholders rather than using the profit to benefit workers and lower car prices. The case has been cited for a century as establishing that corporations must be run for shareholders. Legal scholar Lynn Stout spent much of her career arguing that the citation was both legally inaccurate, Dodge was a closely held company case with limited precedential value for publicly traded corporations, and practically misleading because corporate law's "business judgment rule" gives directors enormous discretion to consider non-shareholder interests as long as they can articulate a plausible business rationale.

Delaware corporate law, which governs most large US corporations because of Delaware's dominant position in corporate incorporation, explicitly allows directors to consider employee, customer, community and supplier interests as part of their business judgment. The Model Business Corporation Act, adopted by most states as a template, contains constituency statutes allowing directors to consider non-shareholder interests. The fiduciary duty of directors runs to the corporation, not specifically to shareholders, and acting in the corporation's long-term interest frequently requires considering stakeholder relationships that affect the corporation's value over time.

What corporate law does require is that directors not act in their own interests at shareholders' expense, and that decisions be made in good faith. It does not require that every decision optimize short-term shareholder returns at the expense of other considerations. The legal foundation for shareholder primacy is considerably weaker than its ideological dominance would suggest, which matters because the doctrine's hold on corporate behavior is partly attributable to the mistaken belief that the law requires it.

The Consequences of Shareholder Primacy

The empirical consequences of several decades of shareholder primacy in American corporate governance are, at this point, reasonably well documented. The picture is not uniformly negative, but neither is it the efficient-markets success story that Friedman's framework promised.

Share buybacks illustrate the dynamic clearly. Under shareholder primacy, returning cash to shareholders through buybacks rather than investing in employees, research, infrastructure or communities is the rational response when management cannot identify investments with returns above the cost of capital. Share buybacks by S&P 500 companies exceeded $1 trillion annually in the years before the 2020 pandemic. From 2003 to 2012, the 449 S&P 500 companies that existed throughout the period spent 54 percent of their earnings on buybacks and 37 percent on dividends, leaving approximately 9 percent for investment in employees, equipment and research. Boeing spent $43.4 billion on buybacks between 2013 and 2020 while cutting corners on the 737 MAX's development; the resulting crashes killed 346 people and grounded the entire fleet, costing the company and its customers an estimated $20 billion.

Wage stagnation for production and non-supervisory workers over the same period that corporate profits and executive compensation soared is not entirely attributable to shareholder primacy, since technological change and globalization are also contributing factors, but the corporate governance model that prioritizes capital returns over labor compensation is a structural contributor. When the Trade-off between higher wages and higher share returns is explicit in quarterly earnings presentations, and when executive compensation is tied to share price, the systematic choice of shareholders over workers is built into the incentive structure.

Short-termism, the tendency to make decisions that boost near-term earnings at the expense of long-term value creation, is another documented consequence. Research by McKinsey Global Institute and others has found that companies focused on long-term value creation significantly outperform short-term-oriented companies on revenue growth, earnings growth and share returns over periods of five years or more. But the quarterly earnings cycle, analyst coverage focused on near-term earnings surprises and executive compensation with relatively short performance windows create powerful incentives for short-term decisions that damage long-term value. Research and development investment as a share of corporate revenue has declined in publicly traded companies while increasing in private ones, consistent with the hypothesis that short-term earnings pressure discourages R&D that will not pay off within the earnings reporting horizon.

Stakeholder Capitalism: The Alternatives

The stakeholder capitalism framework, associated intellectually with R. Edward Freeman's 1984 "Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach," holds that corporations are embedded in networks of relationships with employees, customers, suppliers, communities and shareholders, and that managing these relationships well is both ethically required and commercially advantageous. Freeman's argument was originally analytical rather than normative: managers who ignore stakeholder interests will face consequences from those stakeholders that ultimately harm shareholder value. But it has been interpreted as an alternative normative framework in which corporations have genuine obligations to multiple parties rather than to shareholders alone.

Institutional alternatives to the standard shareholder-primacy public corporation structure have demonstrated that different governance models are viable. German codetermination law requires that supervisory boards of large companies include worker representatives equal in number to shareholder representatives, giving employees a formal role in major corporate decisions including executive compensation, capital allocation and strategic direction. Research on codetermination has found mixed but generally positive effects on long-term investment, wage levels and innovation, without the shareholder value destruction that American finance theory would predict from "inserting" non-shareholder interests into corporate governance.

Benefit corporations (B Corps) are a legal structure available in most US states and several other countries that explicitly requires directors to consider stakeholder interests alongside shareholder returns, providing legal cover for non-shareholder-maximizing decisions and creating a third-party certification framework through B Lab's assessment process. As of 2023, more than 7,000 companies are certified B Corps globally, including Patagonia, Ben and Jerry's, Danone North America and Eileen Fisher. The evidence on whether B Corp certification produces meaningfully different behavior from comparable conventional companies is mixed; certification may more frequently reflect self-selection of companies already operating with stakeholder values than the conversion of shareholder-primacy companies to stakeholder orientation.

Patagonia's 2022 ownership restructuring, in which founder Yvon Chouinard transferred ownership to a nonprofit trust and a new LLC with a legal purpose of using company profits for environmental causes, represents a more radical departure from conventional shareholder ownership. By removing the company from the possibility of acquisition by shareholders who would prioritize financial returns over the environmental mission, Chouinard addressed the fundamental vulnerability of mission-driven businesses: that financial pressure or ownership change can undermine the values that defined the organization's purpose. The structure is not replicable for most companies, but it illustrates that the connection between corporate ownership and profit maximization is not architecturally necessary.

The ESG Controversy

The rise of Environmental, Social and Governance investing, discussed in the algorithmic accountability article in the context of financial sector greenwashing, represents another attempted reconciliation of shareholder and stakeholder interests: the claim that companies that perform well on ESG dimensions produce better financial returns and lower risk for investors. If true, this would dissolve the tension between shareholder and stakeholder interests, making stakeholder management the path to superior shareholder returns.

The evidence for this claim is contested. Some meta-analyses of ESG-performance studies find positive correlations between ESG scores and financial performance. Critics argue that the correlation, where it exists, reflects survivorship bias, the correlation of ESG scores with general management quality, and reverse causation: profitable companies have more resources for ESG activities rather than ESG activities producing profitability. More fundamentally, the ESG-as-investment-returns argument conflates two different claims: that good stakeholder management is financially valuable over the long run, which may be true, and that current ESG scores measure stakeholder management quality well enough to be reliable predictors of returns, which is dubious given the methodological weaknesses in ESG rating discussed elsewhere.

The political backlash against ESG investing in the United States, which emerged in 2022 and intensified through 2023, complicated the landscape further. Several Republican-governed states passed legislation prohibiting state pension funds from considering ESG factors in investment decisions, framing ESG as ideological interference in financial markets. Major financial institutions including BlackRock, which had been among the most prominent advocates for stakeholder capitalism through CEO Larry Fink's annual letters, moderated their ESG rhetoric in response to political pressure. The backlash illustrated that the political sustainability of stakeholder capitalism as a corporate governance framework depends on its ability to demonstrate financial returns, not just articulate values.

What Actually Changes Corporate Behavior

The evidence on what actually changes corporate behavior toward stakeholders is more useful than the theoretical debate about corporate purpose. Several factors appear to produce genuine, sustained changes in how corporations treat employees, communities and the environment, as opposed to rhetorical shifts that do not alter underlying incentive structures.

Binding legal requirements change behavior at scale. The Clean Air Act reduced corporate air pollution more than any voluntary corporate sustainability initiative. Minimum wage laws raise worker pay more than stakeholder capitalism statements. Mandatory paid family leave, where it exists, provides worker benefits that shareholder primacy systematically denies. The evidence from decades of voluntary versus mandatory approaches to corporate social responsibility consistently finds that voluntary approaches produce modest improvements among early adopters while mandatory approaches produce broader change across the corporate population, including laggards who would not change voluntarily.

Genuine market consequences for poor stakeholder treatment, as opposed to reputational consequences that fade quickly, change behavior. The real cost of high employee turnover, the measurable impact of poor community relationships on operating licenses and regulatory treatment, the long-term brand damage from supply chain scandals that remain in public memory, and the growing evidence that short-termism destroys financial value over longer periods all create financial incentives for stakeholder management that do not require ideological conversion.

Governance structures that give non-shareholder constituencies direct voice in corporate decisions, as German codetermination does for workers, produce sustained differences in how trade-offs are made rather than relying on the voluntary decisions of shareholder-accountable executives. The Business Roundtable's 2019 statement changed no governance structures, installed no worker representatives on boards and created no accountability mechanism for stakeholder commitments. The gap between its rhetoric and subsequent corporate behavior was therefore predictable, and it was predicted.

The debate about corporate purpose will not be resolved by CEO statements or academic frameworks. It will be resolved, gradually and unevenly, by the legal and governance structures that determine what corporations are required to do, what they are financially rewarded for and whose interests have formal voice in their decisions. That is a political process, not a philosophical one, and its outcome will reflect the distribution of power among the stakeholders competing to define what the corporation owes them.
