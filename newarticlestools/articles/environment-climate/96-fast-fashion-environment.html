Fast Fashion's Environmental Toll: The Industry That Ate the Planet

In the Atacama Desert in northern Chile, there is a mountain of clothing. It is not a metaphor. It is a literal accumulation of discarded garments, stretching across the landscape in multicolored dunes, some of the piles standing several stories tall. The desert's extreme aridity makes it difficult for organic material to decompose, which means the clothes accumulate rather than break down. They arrive from secondhand markets in nearby Iquique, dumped when the quantity of unsold donations exceeds what the local market can absorb. What the dump represents, in visual terms that the global fashion industry rarely wants to provide, is the physical reality of a system that produces far more clothing than the world needs or can absorb.

The fashion industry produces approximately 100 billion garments per year for a global population of roughly 8 billion people, or about 12.5 items of clothing per person on earth annually. Production has roughly doubled since 2000. The average number of times a garment is worn before it is discarded has declined approximately 36 percent over the same period. The combination of more production and less use per item means the total volume of clothing that passes through the global system has grown dramatically. Most of it ends up in landfill or incinerated within a year, often without ever being worn. The Atacama dump is not an aberration. It is a destination in a supply chain whose logic leads almost inevitably to waste.

The environmental consequences of this system operate at multiple points in the supply chain, from the inputs that produce raw materials to the emissions released when discarded clothing decomposes or burns. Understanding the full scope requires following the garment from field or factory to landfill, examining what happens at each stage and why the cumulative effect is so large.

Cotton's Hidden Costs

Cotton is the most widely used natural fiber in clothing, accounting for roughly a quarter of global fiber production. It is often presented as more sustainable than synthetic alternatives because it is natural and biodegradable. This framing is misleading. Conventional cotton production is among the most resource-intensive and chemically dependent forms of agriculture in existence. Cotton covers about 2.5 percent of the world's cultivated land but accounts for approximately 16 percent of global pesticide use, more than any other single crop. Many of the pesticides used in cotton production are classified as hazardous to human health, and their effects on agricultural workers, local water systems and soil ecology in producing regions are substantial.

Water consumption is equally alarming. Producing one kilogram of cotton fiber requires approximately 10,000 to 20,000 liters of water, depending on the region and irrigation method. The environmental and human cost of this water use has been made dramatically visible by the fate of the Aral Sea, once the fourth largest lake in the world, which was largely drained by the 1960s Soviet decision to divert its tributary rivers for cotton irrigation in what is now Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan. The sea has shrunk to roughly 10 percent of its former volume, a regional ecological catastrophe driven substantially by cotton agriculture.

Organic cotton addresses some of these problems, using no synthetic pesticides and typically consuming less water. But organic cotton currently represents less than 1 percent of global cotton production, and even organic cotton requires significant land and water. The most environmentally honest answer on cotton is that all cotton has significant environmental costs, organic cotton somewhat less so, and that the sheer volume of clothing production means that even significant per-garment improvements leave the aggregate impact very large.

Synthetic Fibers and Microplastic Pollution

Polyester, nylon, acrylic and other synthetic fibers now account for roughly 60 percent of global textile fiber production, having surpassed natural fibers in the early 2000s. Synthetics are preferred by fast fashion producers because they are cheap, durable, versatile and easy to produce in the colors and textures that trend cycles demand. They are also, in environmental terms, a significant and underappreciated source of harm.

The most documented problem with synthetic clothing is microplastic pollution. When synthetic garments are washed in a washing machine, they shed tiny plastic fibers, typically between 0.1 and 5 millimeters in length, that pass through wastewater treatment systems and end up in rivers, lakes and oceans. A single synthetic fleece jacket can shed more than 1 million microfibers per wash, according to research from UC Santa Barbara. Studies have found microplastics in virtually every marine environment sampled, from beach sand to deep sea sediments to the gut contents of marine animals at every level of the food chain, including fish consumed by humans. A 2019 study found microplastics in human stool samples from every participant, confirming that the contamination has reached the human food supply.

Synthetic textiles are estimated to account for roughly 35 percent of primary microplastic pollution in the world's oceans, making them a major contributor to a pollution type whose long-term biological effects are still being studied. The effects on marine organisms range from physical blockage to chemical toxicity, since plastics absorb persistent organic pollutants from the surrounding water and concentrate them in animals that ingest them. The effects on humans from dietary microplastic exposure are not yet fully characterized, but researchers have found microplastics in human blood, lungs and placenta, raising serious questions about health effects that will take decades to fully understand.

The Dyeing and Treatment Problem

Between raw fiber and finished garment, most clothing undergoes dyeing and chemical treatment processes that represent one of the most significant environmental impacts in the supply chain. The textile dyeing industry is estimated to be the second largest source of water pollution globally after agriculture, using roughly 200 tons of water for every ton of fabric dyed. In regions with limited environmental regulation, dye effluent is frequently discharged directly into waterways, turning rivers and groundwater the colors of the season's fashion trends and introducing compounds that are toxic to aquatic life and to the communities that depend on those water sources.

The Citarum River in Indonesia, which flows through one of the world's largest textile manufacturing regions, has been described as the world's most polluted river. Its waters regularly run in colors corresponding to current fashion palettes as dye waste enters from upstream factories. Testing of the river has found the presence of mercury, arsenic, lead and numerous synthetic dye compounds. Local communities that once relied on the river for drinking water, fishing and bathing have been severely affected. Similar conditions exist in textile manufacturing regions in Bangladesh, India, China and Cambodia, where the concentration of production and the limited regulatory enforcement create conditions in which the environmental costs of cheap clothing are externalized onto local communities and ecosystems.

PFAS compounds, sometimes called "forever chemicals" for their persistence in the environment and human body, are widely used in clothing to provide water-resistant and stain-resistant properties. They have been found in groundwater near textile manufacturing facilities, in the blood of workers who handle treated textiles and in consumer products at levels that accumulate with repeated exposure. The European Union has moved to restrict PFAS in textiles, but as of the early 2020s, they remain in widespread use globally in performance and workwear categories.

Labor and the Externalized Human Cost

The environmental story of fast fashion cannot be fully told without the labor story, because the conditions that make cheap clothing environmentally harmful are the same conditions that make it possible to externalize costs onto workers and communities. When a country allows textile factories to dump dye waste in rivers because the economic pressure to attract manufacturing jobs makes environmental enforcement politically difficult, the environmental harm and the labor harm are products of the same dynamic: a global supply chain structured to concentrate benefits in consuming countries while concentrating costs in producing ones.

The 2013 collapse of the Rana Plaza building in Dhaka, Bangladesh, which killed 1,134 garment workers producing clothing for major Western fashion brands, is the most famous illustration of what the race to the bottom in garment production costs means for the people doing the producing. Workers had been instructed to continue working in a building with visible cracks in its walls, under the threat of losing pay, because production schedules required it. The disaster produced significant industry attention to supply chain labor conditions and led to the Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh, a binding agreement between brands and unions. But subsequent reporting has consistently found that labor conditions in garment supply chains remain far below the standards that the brands publicly commit to, and that monitoring and enforcement are inadequate to close the gap between commitment and reality.

The Overproduction Engine

The scale of waste in the fast fashion model is not primarily a result of consumer behavior. It is the result of a production model designed to overproduce and to engineer rapid obsolescence. The traditional fashion calendar involved two seasons per year, spring/summer and fall/winter. Fast fashion companies introduced the concept of micro-seasons, with some brands refreshing their offerings weekly or even more frequently. Shein, the Chinese ultra-fast fashion platform that became one of the world's largest clothing retailers in the early 2020s, has been documented adding as many as 6,000 new styles per day to its platform. This rate of new product introduction is not designed to meet existing demand. It is designed to create demand by constant novelty, the psychological mechanism of new arrivals triggering purchases that would not have occurred in a more stable product environment.

The resulting overproduction creates vast quantities of unsold inventory. High-end brands have historically dealt with unsold inventory by burning it to maintain scarcity and brand value, a practice that generated significant controversy when Burberry disclosed in 2018 that it had incinerated approximately £28.6 million worth of unsold goods. Fast fashion brands deal with unsold inventory by selling it at extreme discount to secondary markets in the Global South, where it arrives in quantities so large that the local markets, as the Atacama dump illustrates, cannot absorb it. In either case, the clothing ends up destroyed, serving no useful purpose after consuming the water, energy, chemicals and labor that produced it.

What Secondhand Markets and Repair Culture Can and Cannot Do

The secondhand clothing market has grown dramatically, driven by platforms like ThredUp, Poshmark and Depop and by increased consumer interest in more sustainable consumption. ThredUp's annual resale report has projected continued strong growth in secondhand clothing over the coming decade, suggesting that resale could represent a significant market share of overall apparel by the late 2020s. This is genuinely good news for environmental impact, at least partially. A garment that is worn ten times generates lower per-wear emissions than one worn twice. The secondhand market extends garment life, which reduces the production demand that would otherwise fill that market segment.

But secondhand markets have limits as a solution to the fast fashion problem. They work best for higher-quality garments that retain their value and usability across multiple owners. The $7 polyester top from Shein is not well-suited to resale because it was not built to last the additional use cycles that secondhand requires. Secondhand markets also have what researchers call a rebound effect: making clothing cheaper through secondhand channels can increase the total quantity consumed, offsetting some of the per-item environmental benefit. And the scale of the secondhand market, even with its impressive growth, remains small relative to the volume of new clothing production. Shifting consumption from new to secondhand addresses the symptom without addressing the production volume that is the root cause.

Repair culture, the practice of maintaining and extending the life of clothing through mending, alterations and rehabilitation, is even more environmentally beneficial than secondhand but remains genuinely countercultural. The economics of repair, particularly for inexpensive garments, often don't pencil out when repair labor costs more than replacement. The cultural norms around visible mending, darning and altering have weakened substantially over the past several decades as clothing has become cheap enough that the economics of repair have rarely made sense. Reviving a repair culture requires both policy changes, such as VAT reductions on repair services as the European Union has experimented with, and cultural changes in how people think about their relationships to the objects they own.

Regulatory Responses and Their Trajectory

The European Union has been the most active regulatory jurisdiction in addressing the environmental impacts of the textile industry. The EU's Strategy for Sustainable and Circular Textiles, adopted in 2022, includes proposals for mandatory minimum recycled content requirements, durability standards, mandatory product passports providing supply chain information and the extension of producer responsibility schemes to textiles. The proposed Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation would allow the EU to set specific environmental performance requirements for product categories including textiles, potentially establishing minimum standards for durability, repairability and recyclability. These are significant policy interventions that, if implemented fully, would meaningfully change the incentive structure the fashion industry operates within.

The United States has no comparable federal regulatory framework for fashion sustainability, though several state-level initiatives are underway. California's Fashion Sustainability and Social Accountability Act, which passed the state Senate in 2022 before failing in the Assembly, would have required large fashion companies to map and disclose their supply chains and set targets for reducing environmental impacts. Similar legislation has been introduced in New York. The federal government has not pursued significant regulatory action on fashion sustainability, leaving the field primarily to voluntary commitments and market forces.

The most significant lever available in the regulatory toolbox is likely extended producer responsibility, requiring companies that produce clothing to be financially responsible for its end-of-life management. France implemented mandatory EPR for textiles in 2007, requiring brands to either take back used clothing or fund a producer responsibility organization that handles collection and recycling. This has created incentives for brands to design for recyclability that purely market-based incentives do not provide. Other countries are at various stages of developing similar programs. Whether these regulatory developments will be enough, fast enough, to materially reduce the environmental impact of a global industry that is continuing to grow is genuinely uncertain. What is certain is that voluntary commitments alone have not achieved it.
