Climate Anxiety Is Real and It's Not Irrational

There is a word coined by philosopher Glenn Albrecht: solastalgia. He defined it as the distress caused by environmental change in one's home environment. It was originally developed to describe the grief felt by communities watching landscapes they loved change or disappear, mining towns whose surroundings were altered by extraction, coastal communities watching the water creep closer, people who grew up in places that no longer exist in the form they knew. The word has spread because it names something that a growing number of people feel every time they read a climate report, every time a summer breaks another heat record, every time a wildfire burns through a forest they hiked as a child.

Climate anxiety, which researchers sometimes call eco-anxiety or climate grief, is not a fringe phenomenon and it is not a sign of psychological fragility. A 2021 survey published in The Lancet surveyed 10,000 young people between ages 16 and 25 across ten countries and found that 59 percent were very or extremely worried about climate change, 45 percent said their feelings about climate change negatively affected their daily lives and functioning and 68 percent agreed that governments were betraying them and future generations. A 2020 American Psychological Association survey found that more than two-thirds of American adults reported at least some level of climate-related anxiety. The American Psychological Association now recognizes eco-anxiety as a significant mental health concern and has developed clinical guidance on addressing it.

What distinguishes climate anxiety from other anxiety disorders is that it is a response to a real threat, not an exaggerated fear of something unlikely. The scientific consensus on climate change is overwhelming, the projections for near-term impacts are serious, and many of those impacts are already unfolding in ways that are empirically verifiable. This creates a therapeutic paradox: the standard cognitive-behavioral approaches to anxiety, which involve examining whether the feared outcome is realistically likely and recalibrating catastrophic thinking, are not fully applicable when the feared outcome is, in fact, realistically likely and backed by scientific consensus. You cannot simply reassure someone out of climate anxiety the way you can reassure someone out of the fear that an airplane will crash, because the scientific basis of the concern is fundamentally different.

What Climate Anxiety Actually Feels Like

Climate anxiety manifests differently in different people, and it is worth describing its phenomenology carefully rather than lumping it with either generic anxiety disorder or political activism. For some people it presents primarily as grief, a pervasive mourning for things already lost and things expected to be lost. People who grew up near coral reefs are watching them bleach and die. People who lived near glaciers are watching them retreat to their lifetimes. People who knew certain species of birds from childhood are observing their local extinctions. This grief is anticipatory as well as retrospective, extending across time in ways that make it feel larger than ordinary losses.

For others it presents primarily as anxiety about the future, a chronic low-grade dread about what the coming decades will bring. Young people in particular, whose anticipated lifespans extend furthest into a warming future, often report a specific kind of existential uncertainty about whether it makes sense to plan for a life, to have children, to invest in a future whose contours seem dangerously unclear. A 2021 study found that 39 percent of young people surveyed said they were hesitant to have children because of climate change. This is not an irrational hesitation. It is a rational response to genuine uncertainty about the world those children would inhabit.

A third form involves what researchers call climate grief or ecological grief, which presents as depressive rather than anxious, a sense of loss and meaninglessness in the face of something too large and too systemic to respond to adequately as an individual. This form is often accompanied by a specific kind of helplessness, the gap between the scale of what would need to change and the scale of what any individual can do, which produces a psychological experience that has features of learned helplessness and in some cases can slide toward depression.

"I'm 26 and I think about climate change every single day. Not in a productive 'what can I do about it' way most of the time. In a background hum of dread that colors everything. Every hot summer day feels ominous. Every wildfire news story feels personal. Every time someone says 'enjoy this while you can' about a beautiful landscape, I want to cry. My therapist says this isn't disordered thinking. She says it's a reasonable response to the actual situation. That's both validating and not helpful." u/dread_the_forecast — r/ClimateAnxiety · ▲ 44,819

The Generational Dimension

Climate anxiety is distributed unequally across generations, and understanding that distribution is important for understanding its psychological and political implications. Younger people feel it more acutely than older people, and this is not simply a matter of temperament or political orientation. It is a matter of temporal exposure. A 70-year-old who is aware of climate change can reasonably expect, with some sorrow, that the worst of its projected impacts will unfold largely after their own lifetime. A 25-year-old making decisions about career, housing, family and life trajectory does not have this consolation. The climate projections for 2050 and 2070 are not an abstraction for someone who expects to be alive in 2050 and 2070. They are the expected conditions of their actual life.

This generational difference in exposure creates a genuine tension in intergenerational conversations about climate, particularly within families. When a young person expresses distress about climate change and an older relative responds with reassurance that "things will work out" or "humans are adaptable," the mismatch is not just a difference of opinion. It is a difference in how directly each person will experience what happens next, and that difference is real in ways that make dismissive reassurance feel not just unhelpful but dishonest. Many young people report that their climate anxiety is compounded by feeling that the people with the most power to have changed course over the past several decades did not do so, and that they have inherited both the problem and the bill without having been party to the decisions that created it.

Research on intergenerational equity and climate has found that younger cohorts have consistently expressed higher concern about climate change than older cohorts in surveys across multiple countries and over many years. This is not primarily a lifecycle effect in which people become less concerned as they age. It is a cohort effect in which people who came of age knowing climate change as a present and growing reality carry that knowledge differently than people who came of age when it was primarily a future concern. The psychological consequences of growing up with this knowledge as background reality rather than alarming new information are still being studied, but the available evidence suggests they are significant.

Climate Grief and Ecological Loss

The ecological losses associated with climate change and the broader environmental crisis are not limited to changes in temperature and weather patterns. They include species extinction, habitat destruction, the degradation of landscapes that people have meaningful connections to and the disappearance of natural phenomena that have shaped cultures, spiritual practices and human identities for millennia. The grief associated with these losses is real and legitimate, and it deserves to be named as grief rather than categorized as anxiety or political concern.

Environmental philosopher Timothy Morton coined the term "dark ecology" to describe the disorientation of recognizing that the web of life in which humans are embedded is in radical transformation. Aldo Leopold, writing in the 1940s, described something similar when he wrote about the "penalty of an ecological education" being that you live in a world of wounds. This framing, in which environmental awareness comes at a psychological cost, is not defeatist. It is honest about the emotional reality of paying close attention to what is happening to the living world.

Indigenous communities and communities with deep land-based cultural traditions often experience this loss with particular intensity. The cultural and spiritual dimensions of ecological relationships, connections between specific species and specific peoples, relationships between landscapes and cultural practices and between seasonal rhythms and community life, are being severed at rates and in ways that existing categories of grief and loss do not fully capture. Albrecht's term "solastalgia" was originally developed to describe these community-level ecological losses, and the concept has been expanded by researchers working with indigenous communities experiencing climate-driven displacement from ancestral lands.

When Anxiety Becomes Paralyzing

Climate anxiety exists on a spectrum. At one end is productive concern that motivates engagement, action and community connection. At the other end is debilitating anxiety that prevents functioning, generates chronic despair and leads to withdrawal from both personal life and civic engagement. The clinical and psychological question is not whether climate anxiety is legitimate, it clearly is, but what distinguishes the versions of it that motivate from the versions that paralyze.

Research on psychological responses to large-scale threats generally suggests that perceived efficacy, the sense that one's actions can make a meaningful difference, is a key moderator between awareness of a threat and adaptive response to it. People who feel that they can do something meaningful are more likely to engage constructively with the problem than people who feel powerless. This has an important implication for climate communication: messaging that focuses heavily on the magnitude of the problem without connecting it to meaningful individual or collective action may increase concern without increasing efficacy, producing the worst psychological outcome, high anxiety combined with low motivation to engage.

Doom-scrolling climate news, consuming an unrelenting stream of climate disaster stories without any counterbalancing engagement with solutions, community or action, is a reliable way to increase anxiety without generating any corresponding benefit. The information consumed does not translate into behavior change, does not provide community support and does not offer any sense of efficacy. It simply loads the nervous system with threat information and leaves it there. Therapists working with climate-anxious clients often find that helping clients establish intentional relationships with climate information, including when, how much and in what context they engage with it, is one of the most useful interventions available.

"I had to stop reading climate news for six months because I was having panic attacks. When I came back to it, I was deliberate about only reading it while I was in an activist context, like at a meeting or before taking action. That shift was huge. The information was the same but the context changed everything. I wasn't just absorbing bad news anymore. I was using information to do something." u/climate_action_recovered — r/ClimateAnxiety · ▲ 29,481

The Therapeutic Response to an Unprecedented Problem

The clinical community has been actively developing approaches to climate anxiety that take both the legitimacy of the concern and the need for functional wellbeing seriously. The Climate Psychology Alliance and similar organizations have been training therapists in what they call "climate-aware" or "ecologically-informed" practice, which means being able to work with climate anxiety without either dismissing the underlying concern as disordered thinking or being so aligned with the urgency that the therapeutic space becomes a space of shared despair rather than constructive engagement.

Several therapeutic approaches have shown particular relevance. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, or ACT, is well-suited to climate anxiety because it works with the client's relationship to difficult thoughts and feelings rather than attempting to eliminate them. ACT helps people identify what matters to them, accept the presence of painful feelings without being controlled by them and commit to actions consistent with their values even in the presence of uncertainty. For someone experiencing climate grief, this might look like being able to hold the grief, the uncertainty and the limitation of individual action while still living in accordance with values related to care for the natural world and connection to community.

Group approaches are particularly valuable for climate anxiety because one of its most corrosive features is the sense of isolation it can produce, the feeling that one is alone in seeing the situation clearly while the people around them continue as if nothing fundamental is changing. Climate Cafes, Good Grief Network groups and similar peer support structures provide spaces where climate feelings can be named and witnessed without judgment, and where the connection to others who share the concern provides both validation and community. Research on grief more broadly has consistently found that community and witnessed grief are central to healthy processing, and climate grief is no exception.

The Political Dimension and Its Complications

Climate anxiety does not exist in a political vacuum, and one of its complications is the way in which the political environment around climate shapes both the experience of anxiety and the available responses to it. In countries and communities where climate denial or minimization is politically dominant, people experiencing climate anxiety may face the additional burden of having their concern dismissed, mocked or treated as politically motivated rather than scientifically grounded. This dismissal is itself a form of psychological harm, the invalidation of a legitimate concern by people who have the cultural or political authority to define what counts as a real problem.

The political dimension also complicates therapeutic work. A therapist who works to reduce a client's climate anxiety without any engagement with the legitimacy of the concern risks implicitly treating a rational response to a real threat as a disordered response to be corrected. But a therapist who fully endorses the most alarming possible reading of climate projections, without helping the client develop a functional relationship to uncertainty and action, risks reinforcing the despair that prevents engagement. The therapeutic goal is not to change the client's assessment of climate change but to help them live and function in the context of that assessment, which is a meaningfully different aim.

The distinction between climate concern and climate anxiety is itself politically loaded. People who want to minimize attention to climate change have an interest in pathologizing concern as anxiety, treating it as a mental health problem rather than a political position. People who are deeply concerned about climate change sometimes resist the framing of their concern as anxiety because it seems to individualize what is fundamentally a collective and political problem. Both of these concerns are legitimate and both should be held simultaneously: climate concern is rational and politically important, and some people experience it at intensities that interfere with their functioning and deserve clinical support.

Living Meaningfully Inside a Climate Crisis

The question that climate anxiety ultimately forces is not whether to feel it, which is not fully within individual control, but how to live meaningfully and engage effectively within the reality of it. The people who appear to manage climate awareness without being paralyzed by it share certain characteristics. They maintain their connection to the natural world as a source of solace and meaning rather than cutting themselves off from it to avoid grief. They find communities of shared concern that provide both emotional support and collective action. They take actions that are within their sphere of influence while holding without despair the recognition that individual action alone cannot solve a systemic problem. And they maintain hope not as optimism about outcomes but as commitment to the possibility of meaningful engagement regardless of outcome.

Joanna Macy, whose "Work That Reconnects" framework has been influential in climate psychology circles, writes about what she calls "active hope," which she distinguishes from passive hope that things will work out. Active hope is not an expectation but a practice, a commitment to working toward the world we care about without guaranteeing the results. This reframe moves hope from the domain of prediction to the domain of values and action, making it available to people who cannot honestly maintain optimism about climate outcomes but who can honestly maintain commitment to the communities, species and landscapes they love.

The ecological and climate crises of the 21st century are unprecedented in human history in their scope, their speed and the extent to which they require restructuring the basic assumptions about human relationship to the natural world. The psychological responses to this reality, including grief, anxiety and despair, are not signs of weakness or disorder. They are signs of honest engagement with what is actually happening. The task is not to stop feeling them but to find ways of feeling them that are sustainable enough to support a life of meaning and engagement, which is, in the end, the only adequate response to a problem this large.
