The Greenwashing Playbook: How Companies Sell Sustainability They Don't Have

The word "sustainable" appears on more product packaging today than perhaps any other adjective in retail history. It is on water bottles and sneakers, coffee cups and fashion lines, airline loyalty programs and fast food wrappers. Some of the products bearing this label represent genuine efforts to reduce environmental harm. Many do not. The gap between the claim and the reality has a name: greenwashing. And understanding how it works is one of the more practically useful things an environmentally conscious consumer can do, not because individual consumer choices are the most important lever in addressing the climate crisis, but because understanding how you're being manipulated is always worth knowing.

The term greenwashing was coined by environmentalist Jay Westerveld in 1986, in a context that remains instructive. He was staying at a hotel that had placed cards in each room asking guests to reuse their towels to "save the environment." The hotel was, at the same time, undergoing a major expansion that would increase its environmental footprint substantially. The message to guests was not about environmental responsibility. It was about reducing laundry costs while creating the impression of environmental concern. The structure of that original example, a visible, feel-good environmental gesture that obscures the larger reality of business-as-usual, remains the template for most greenwashing that followed.

A 2021 study by the European Commission examined websites claiming environmental credentials across a range of product categories and found that 42 percent of the claims were exaggerated, false or deceptive. The European Union subsequently moved to ban unsubstantiated environmental claims on products by 2026, with the Green Claims Directive requiring companies to substantiate environmental claims with scientific evidence before making them. The US Federal Trade Commission has maintained "Green Guides" since 1992 that establish standards for environmental marketing claims, though enforcement has been inconsistent and the guides have not kept pace with the sophistication of contemporary greenwashing techniques.

Carbon Offsets: The Math That Rarely Works

Of all the greenwashing mechanisms in corporate use, carbon offsets have arguably the largest gap between promise and delivery. The concept has a straightforward logic: if your company emits a ton of carbon dioxide, you can pay someone else to remove or prevent a ton of carbon dioxide from elsewhere in the world, and your net contribution to atmospheric carbon remains zero. You are "carbon neutral." This logic, if the offset program actually works as described, is sound enough. The problem is that most carbon offset programs don't work as described, and the verification systems that are supposed to ensure they do have been repeatedly exposed as inadequate.

An investigation by The Guardian, Die Zeit and SourceMaterial published in 2023 examined Verra, the largest certifier of carbon offsets in the world, and found that more than 90 percent of the rainforest offset credits it had certified were likely "phantom credits" that did not represent genuine carbon reductions. The investigation found that independent analysts who examined Verra-certified projects concluded that the vast majority of the forest preservation credited was in forests that were not actually at risk of deforestation. If the forest wasn't going to be cut down anyway, preventing its imaginary deforestation doesn't prevent any actual carbon emissions.

This is the additionality problem. A legitimate carbon offset must represent carbon reduction that would not have occurred without the offset payment, meaning the payment must be the difference between the project happening and not happening. In practice, many offset projects involve activities that would have happened regardless of offset funding, forest preservation in areas with low deforestation pressure, renewable energy projects that were economically viable anyway and efficiency improvements that were already planned. The offset market has strong incentives to certify projects regardless of additionality, because more certified projects mean more revenue for certifiers and more offsets for companies to purchase, but no incentive strong enough to maintain the rigorous additionality verification that genuine credits require.

"I worked at a company that was very proud of being 'carbon neutral.' I eventually looked into what that meant. It meant they were buying credits from a rainforest preservation project that, when I did some digging, had been certified to protect forest that satellite imagery showed was not being threatened. We were paying for a problem that didn't exist to claim we were solving it. Management was not interested in this information." u/former_sustainability_director — r/environment · ▲ 37,814

The Recyclable Packaging Scam

The chasing arrows symbol on plastic products has been one of the most effective pieces of consumer misdirection in the history of packaging. The symbol and the number inside it indicate the type of plastic resin the product is made from, which is useful information for recycling facilities. It does not mean the product is recyclable, and it has been widely understood as implying exactly that. In practice, only plastics coded 1 (PET, typically water and soda bottles) and 2 (HDPE, typically milk jugs and some shampoo bottles) are accepted by most municipal recycling programs in the United States. Plastics coded 3 through 7, which carry the same arrows symbol, are recycled by roughly 9 percent of Americans due to limited facility capacity and market constraints.

The overall recycling rate for plastic in the United States is around 5 to 6 percent, according to research by Beyond Plastics and the Last Beach Cleanup. This means that when companies market their products as recyclable and urge consumers to "please recycle this packaging," roughly 94 percent of that packaging is going to landfill or incinerator regardless. The consumer has done what they were told. The infrastructure to handle the material simply doesn't exist at sufficient scale, and the economics of recycled plastic, now competing with cheap virgin plastic made from inexpensive fossil fuels, have made the situation worse rather than better since China restricted its imports of mixed plastics in 2018.

Companies have known this for decades. A 2020 investigation by NPR found internal documents from the plastics industry showing that industry leaders knew as early as the 1970s that plastic recycling was not economically viable at scale. They promoted recycling anyway, through public campaigns, through lobbying against plastic regulations and through placing the recycling symbol on products they knew were unlikely to be recycled, because the message that plastic was recyclable reduced public pressure for restrictions on plastic production. The recycling symbol was, in substantial part, a public relations strategy rather than an environmental one.

Vague Environmental Claims and What They Hide

The most common form of greenwashing involves claims so vague they cannot be evaluated. Words and phrases like "eco-friendly," "green," "natural," "sustainable," "responsible" and "environmentally conscious" appear on thousands of products and carry no legally defined meaning in most contexts. They communicate a positive environmental signal without making any specific, falsifiable claim. A product can be called "eco-friendly" without any evidence that it is better for the environment than alternatives, and the manufacturer faces essentially no legal risk from the claim as long as they don't make it more specific.

The FTC's Green Guides specifically warn against unqualified environmental claims like these, noting that they imply an overall environmental benefit that rarely exists. But enforcement of these guidelines is limited, and the guides themselves acknowledge that even well-intentioned environmental claims can mislead if they're not qualified with information about what specifically makes the product better and better than what. The consumer who buys "sustainable" sneakers has no way to know whether that claim refers to the use of recycled materials in the insole, reduced water use in manufacturing, lower-carbon shipping or simply the company's stated commitment to being better in the future.

The "natural" label is particularly problematic in the food and personal care product space. "Natural" on a food label has no legally defined meaning in the United States. The FDA has declined to define it despite petitions from consumer groups. Products labeled natural can contain high fructose corn syrup, genetically modified ingredients, artificial preservatives and various synthetic additives, all of which consumers reasonably interpret "natural" to exclude. A 2015 Consumer Reports survey found that 59 percent of consumers trusted "natural" food labels, making it one of the most effective deceptive marketing claims in common use.

Fast Fashion's Green Collections

The fashion industry is responsible for roughly 10 percent of annual global carbon dioxide emissions, more than aviation and shipping combined, according to widely cited estimates. It is also one of the most active sectors in green marketing, with virtually every major fast fashion brand now maintaining a "conscious," "sustainable" or "responsible" collection alongside their main lines. These collections receive prominent marketing attention and environmental framing, but they typically represent a small fraction of total production, and their environmental credentials are often largely cosmetic.

H&M, which operates what it calls its Conscious Collection, was fined 50 million Swedish kronor by Sweden's Consumer Agency in 2022 for misleading environmental claims about its products. The company had provided environmental scorecards for products that turned out to contain errors and exaggerations. A separate investigation by Changing Markets Foundation found that 96 percent of the claims H&M had made under its Higg Index sustainability scoring system were unsubstantiated or misleading. The same investigation found similar problems with claims from Zara, ASOS, M&S and other major fashion brands.

The fundamental problem with sustainable fashion lines from fast fashion companies is that they exist within a business model predicated on producing and selling as many items as possible at the lowest possible price. The environmental problem with fast fashion is not primarily the materials used in any individual item. It is the rate of production, the planned obsolescence that encourages consumers to discard clothing quickly, the overproduction that results in large quantities of unsold clothing being incinerated, and the supply chain labor and chemical use involved in producing hundreds of millions of garments annually. A cotton t-shirt made with "organic" cotton but sold for $8 as part of a business model that encourages consumers to buy twelve shirts a year is not a sustainable product.

"I got really into sustainable fashion for a while. I bought from brands that had organic certifications and ethical supply chain claims. Then I started doing actual research on what those certifications meant. Some were meaningful. Most were essentially self-certified or certified by organizations the companies paid for. I'm not saying nobody is doing it right. I'm saying the signal-to-noise ratio in 'sustainable fashion' marketing is terrible." u/researched_my_values — r/sustainability · ▲ 26,814

Net Zero: Accounting for a Stable Climate

The corporate commitment to "net zero" has proliferated across industries since the Paris Agreement established 1.5 degrees Celsius warming as a critical threshold. As of 2023, more than 90 percent of Fortune 500 companies have made some form of net zero commitment. The problem is that "net zero" has no consistent definition, and the quality of commitments using that label varies from genuinely ambitious to functionally meaningless.

The NewClimate Institute and Carbon Market Watch analyzed the net zero commitments of 25 major corporations in 2022 and found that the combined "net zero" commitments would, in practice, deliver only a 40 percent reduction in emissions by 2030 rather than the near-100 percent reduction implied by the label. Most of the gap was attributable to companies' continued reliance on carbon offsets to claim neutrality for ongoing emissions, to the exclusion of supply chain emissions from scope 3 accounting and to commitments with distant target years lacking near-term milestones.

The Science Based Targets initiative, or SBTi, has developed standards for corporate net zero commitments that require companies to reduce absolute emissions in line with Paris Agreement pathways before using offsets for residual hard-to-abate emissions. Commitments aligned with SBTi standards are meaningfully more rigorous than unverified net zero claims. But as of 2024, most corporate net zero commitments, including many prominently marketed ones, are not aligned with SBTi standards and have not been independently verified. The consumer or investor looking at a company's sustainability page has no reliable way to distinguish a genuine commitment from a marketing exercise without doing the kind of detailed analysis that most people don't have the time or technical background to perform.

How Greenwashing Harms Genuine Progress

Beyond the direct harm of misleading consumers, greenwashing does systemic damage to the project of actually addressing environmental problems. It creates the impression that major companies are taking environmental problems seriously and making meaningful progress, which reduces the pressure for the structural changes that genuine progress would require. If consumers believe their purchases are sustainable, they are less likely to demand regulatory change. If investors believe companies are managing climate risk responsibly, they are less likely to demand the transparency and accountability that genuine management would require. Greenwashing functions as a pressure valve, releasing concern without releasing emissions.

It also undermines genuinely sustainable businesses. When consumers cannot reliably distinguish genuine environmental performance from marketing performance, and when the cost of genuine environmental improvement is higher than the cost of a marketing campaign, the market rewards the latter. Companies that actually invest in supply chain improvements, emissions reductions and genuine product redesign compete against companies that invest in marketing claims and certification schemes, at higher cost, without necessarily capturing the consumer preference that would justify the investment. The result is a market that fails to allocate capital toward genuine environmental improvement because the signal connecting environmental performance to consumer preference has been corrupted by misleading claims.

What Genuine Sustainability Looks Like and How to Find It

Separating genuine environmental performance from greenwashing requires some specific knowledge about what to look for, and the honest admission that perfect information is not available to consumers operating in reasonable time constraints. But some heuristics help. Companies with genuine environmental commitments tend to provide specific, quantified claims with third-party verification rather than vague qualitative ones. They disclose actual emissions data including scope 3 supply chain emissions, not just direct operational emissions. Their sustainability reports include information about what they haven't achieved as well as what they have, because a sustainability report that reads as entirely positive news is almost certainly not providing a complete picture. And their environmental commitments include near-term milestones rather than only distant target dates.

Certifications vary significantly in quality. B Corp certification, which requires assessment of a company's overall social and environmental performance, governance and accountability, is more rigorous than most product-level certifications. Certified Organic has legal backing in the US through the USDA, though it applies to agricultural practices rather than overall environmental performance. Fair Trade certification addresses labor practices more than environmental ones. Carbon neutral certifications vary enormously in quality, with those based on Gold Standard or Verra certification of offsets being more rigorous than self-certified claims, though as the Guardian investigation showed, even third-party certified offsets can fail to deliver genuine reductions.

The most honest advice for environmentally motivated consumers is that individual purchasing decisions, while meaningful to a degree, are not the primary lever for the changes that environmental problems require. The companies with the largest environmental footprints, fossil fuel companies, agriculture conglomerates, shipping companies and industrial manufacturers, are not primarily consumer-facing in ways that individual boycotts or purchases can meaningfully influence. The most significant environmental decisions are made in regulatory, financial and political arenas. Understanding greenwashing matters not just for making better purchases but for developing a clearer picture of which companies deserve regulatory scrutiny and political accountability, which is ultimately where the largest changes will come from.
