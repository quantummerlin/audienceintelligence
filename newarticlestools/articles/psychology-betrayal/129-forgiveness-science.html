What the Science of Forgiveness Actually Says

61,293 upvotes · by u/still_processing · 12 min read
▲ 61,293 people found this relatable

Forgiveness has been the subject of sustained scientific research since the late 1980s, when psychologist Robert Enright and his colleagues began developing empirical tools for studying it. In the three decades since, the literature has grown to encompass hundreds of studies, several well-validated intervention programs and a reasonably clear set of findings about what forgiveness does to the person who forgives, what it requires and what it doesn't require. The popular understanding of forgiveness lags considerably behind this research, which is unfortunate because the misconceptions about forgiveness cause real harm to real people who are trying to navigate the aftermath of serious harm.

The most consequential misconception is the equation of forgiveness with reconciliation. In the popular understanding, to forgive someone is to restore the relationship, to treat what happened as no longer relevant, to essentially return to the state before the harm occurred. This understanding makes forgiveness feel threatening to survivors of serious harm, because it seems to require them to expose themselves again to someone who hurt them, to act as though what happened was not as serious as it was or to provide the person who harmed them with an endorsement they may not deserve. The research on forgiveness does not support this understanding. The scientific consensus is that forgiveness and reconciliation are distinct processes that can occur together but don't have to.

What Forgiveness Is and What It Isn't

The most widely used research definition of forgiveness, developed by Enright and later refined by Everett Worthington and others, defines it as a motivational transformation in which a person who has been genuinely wronged chooses to reduce or eliminate the negative feelings, thoughts and behaviors directed toward the offender and to increase or develop compassion, generosity or even love toward them. Several things in this definition are worth unpacking.

Forgiveness requires that the person was genuinely wronged. It is not a reframing of the harm as not-harm. You cannot forgive someone for something that wasn't actually wrong. This matters because one of the ways forgiveness is misused in relationships is as pressure on victims to reframe legitimate grievances as misunderstandings, overreactions or things that "weren't that bad." The research definition of forgiveness explicitly builds in the acknowledgment that something harmful and unjust actually happened.

Forgiveness is a choice, which means it is not automatic, not required and not produced by time alone. The common formulation "time heals all wounds" confuses temporal distance with forgiveness. Distance from a harmful event may reduce the acuteness of negative feelings without involving any motivational transformation toward the person who caused the harm. People can be non-forgiving decades after an event, and people can forgive within weeks. The timeline is not the determinant.

Forgiveness does not require reconciliation. This is perhaps the most important clarification in the research literature. Reconciliation involves restoring a relationship, and it requires behavioral changes from the person who caused harm, ongoing trustworthiness, appropriate accountability and the practical conditions for a safe relationship. None of these are required for forgiveness. A person can fully forgive someone they never speak to again, someone who is dead, someone whose behavior has not changed and someone who has never apologized. Forgiveness occurs inside the forgiver, not in the relationship between the parties.

"I forgave my father while he was in prison and I had no contact with him. The forgiveness had nothing to do with him. It wasn't for him. It was for the version of me that was still angry every day, still carrying something that was poisoning my present. I released it. That's forgiveness. I haven't spoken to him since and I don't plan to. Forgiveness and reconciliation are different things." — r/raisedbynarcissists, 57,293 upvotes

What the Research Finds About Forgiveness and Health

The health benefits of forgiveness are among the most replicated findings in positive psychology, though the effect sizes are generally modest and the mechanisms are not fully understood. Meta-analyses consistently find that greater trait forgiveness (the general tendency to forgive across situations) is associated with lower rates of depression, anxiety and hostility; better cardiovascular health markers; lower cortisol levels under stress; and better sleep quality. Longitudinal studies have found that increases in forgiveness over time are associated with subsequent improvements in mental health outcomes, even controlling for prior mental health levels.

The physiological research is particularly interesting. Studies by Charlotte van Oyen Witvliet at Hope College found that asking participants to imagine forgiving versus ruminating about a person who had wronged them produced different physiological responses: rumination produced elevated heart rate, blood pressure and corrugator (frowning muscle) activity that persisted after the imagery task ended, while forgiveness imagery produced faster physiological recovery. The state of unforgiveness, characterized by sustained anger, resentment and grudge-holding, appears to maintain a chronic stress response that has cumulative physiological costs.

The mechanism connecting forgiveness to health is generally understood through the stress response pathway. Sustained unforgiveness involves recurrent activation of the threat and anger systems: thinking about the person who harmed you, rehearsing the wrong, imagining confrontations, experiencing the associated physiological arousal repeatedly. This chronic low-level stress activation has known negative health consequences over time. Forgiveness, by reducing the frequency and intensity of these activations, may reduce the cumulative physiological burden.

Worthington's REACH Model and the Evidence for Interventions

Everett Worthington's REACH model is probably the most extensively tested forgiveness intervention in the literature. The acronym stands for Recall the hurt (acknowledging what happened and that it was genuinely harmful), Empathize with the offender (developing some understanding of why they might have done what they did, not excusing it but contextualizing it), offer the Altruistic gift of forgiveness (framing forgiveness as a gift given to oneself and, in a sense, to the offender, rather than something earned by the offender), Commit to forgive (making an explicit decision and often writing it down), and Hold onto forgiveness when doubt returns (expecting that the feelings will not instantly and permanently change and having a plan for when the anger and hurt resurface).

The REACH model has been tested in randomized controlled trials across multiple populations, including survivors of childhood incest, adult victims of intimate partner violence, people dealing with infidelity and people struggling with self-forgiveness, among others. Meta-analyses of these trials find consistent, modest to moderate effect sizes in favor of forgiveness interventions over waitlist controls or alternative treatments, with effects on both forgiveness levels and psychological wellbeing outcomes. The effect sizes are not enormous and the follow-up periods in most studies are limited, but the evidence supports the conclusion that forgiveness is a learnable and teachable process rather than simply something that happens or doesn't.

The Pressure to Forgive and Its Harms

Despite the research finding benefits associated with forgiveness, the research also documents harm caused by forgiveness pressure: the social, cultural or religious expectation that victims should forgive, should forgive quickly and should demonstrate their forgiveness by maintaining or restoring the relationship with the person who harmed them. This pressure is particularly intense in religious communities, where forgiveness is often framed as a moral and spiritual obligation rather than a personal process, and in family contexts, where pressure to maintain family harmony can override appropriate accountability.

Forgiveness pressure harms survivors in several specific ways. It communicates that their anger and grief are inappropriate rather than understandable responses to being wronged. It shifts focus from the behavior of the person who caused harm to the psychological state of the person harmed, making the victim's forgiveness the primary concern rather than the perpetrator's accountability. It is frequently weaponized by people who caused harm to avoid accountability: "you said you forgave me, so why are you still bringing this up" is a way of using forgiveness to silence legitimate ongoing concerns. And it can pressure survivors to maintain relationships with people who remain harmful, using forgiveness language to override the protective instinct to maintain distance from those who have hurt them.

"Every Sunday, the message was clear: you must forgive. After my uncle assaulted me, my mother told me I had to forgive him or I was sinning. At the age of thirteen. I spent twenty years thinking that my inability to forgive was a moral failing. My therapist eventually helped me understand that what I'd been told was forgiveness was actually being pressured to protect my abuser while being told it was for my own spiritual good." — r/religioustrauma, 63,847 upvotes

The research response to the forgiveness pressure problem distinguishes carefully between intrinsic and extrinsic forgiveness motivation. Forgiveness that is self-motivated, emerging from the person's own internal process rather than external pressure, is associated with the psychological benefits documented in the literature. Forgiveness that is externally pressured or compliant, undertaken to satisfy social expectations rather than as an authentic internal choice, does not show the same health benefits and may in some contexts be associated with worse outcomes by preventing the full processing of legitimate anger and grief.

Self-Forgiveness: The Neglected Dimension

The forgiveness literature has increasingly attended to self-forgiveness, which is both conceptually distinct from forgiving others and practically important for recovery from trauma and moral injury. Self-forgiveness becomes particularly important in contexts where survivors of harm experience guilt about their response to the harm, their perceived failure to prevent it, their subsequent behavior or their difficulty in forgiving others. It is also important for people who have caused harm and are attempting to change.

Research by Hall and Fincham found that genuine self-forgiveness requires the same elements as forgiving others: full acknowledgment of what was done, taking genuine responsibility without minimizing, genuine remorse and willingness to make amends where possible, and then a decision to release the self-condemnation that stands in the way of constructive change. The counterfeit version of self-forgiveness, which involves excusing oneself rather than genuinely confronting what was done, does not produce the psychological benefits of genuine self-forgiveness and is associated with reduced likelihood of behavioral change.

Moral injury, which has been studied extensively in military contexts but applies more broadly, refers to the psychological damage done by participating in or witnessing acts that violate deeply held moral beliefs. Self-forgiveness is central to recovery from moral injury, because the injury is fundamentally to the person's self-concept as a moral agent. Research on moral injury treatment consistently finds that interventions addressing self-condemnation and working toward some form of self-forgiveness are among the most important components of recovery.

What the Research Doesn't Say

Several things that the forgiveness research clearly does not support are worth stating explicitly, because they are frequently invoked in popular discourse. The research does not support the claim that you must forgive to heal. People recover from serious harm without forgiving, and the research shows health benefits associated with forgiveness on average across populations, not a requirement for individual healing. Some people heal well without forgiveness. Others find forgiveness central to their recovery. Both are valid.

The research does not support the claim that forgiveness requires an apology. As noted above, forgiveness occurs inside the forgiver and does not require anything from the person forgiven. The research on whether apology facilitates forgiveness shows that it does, on average, but the absence of apology does not make forgiveness impossible. Many survivors of abuse by people who never apologized or never will have fully forgiven them, for their own reasons and in their own time.

The research also does not support the claim that forgiveness is always the right response. This is perhaps the most important corrective to popular usage. Forgiveness is a personal and psychological process with documented benefits for the person who forgives. It is not a moral requirement, a social obligation or an indicator of psychological health. People who are in ongoing harm situations, who need their anger to sustain the motivation required to protect themselves, who are not ready to forgive and who have reasons they understand for not forgiving are not failing at recovery. They are navigating their own experience in their own way, which is ultimately what recovery requires.
