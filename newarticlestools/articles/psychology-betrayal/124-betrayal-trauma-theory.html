Betrayal Trauma: When the Person Who Hurt You Is Someone You Need

In 1994, psychologist Jennifer Freyd published a paper proposing a theory that would reshape how clinicians and researchers think about childhood trauma and its long-term consequences. She called it betrayal trauma theory, and its central insight was deceptively simple: the psychological consequences of traumatic events depend not only on what happened but on who did it. Trauma inflicted by a stranger produces a different kind of damage than trauma inflicted by someone the victim depends on and loves, and the difference is not simply a matter of degree. It involves a fundamentally different set of survival adaptations that explain patterns of memory, behavior and symptom presentation that had previously been poorly understood.

The theory emerged partly from Freyd's own experience, which she has written about publicly, and partly from her observation that standard trauma models couldn't adequately explain the behavior of adults who had been abused as children by caregivers. Why did so many people not remember their abuse until adulthood? Why did victims frequently maintain close relationships with their abusers? Why did they so often blame themselves rather than the people who had harmed them? Standard fear-conditioning models of trauma couldn't account for these patterns, but betrayal trauma theory could.

The Core Insight: Dependency Changes Everything

The foundational claim of betrayal trauma theory is that children who are abused by caregivers face a fundamentally different adaptive problem than children who are threatened by strangers or environmental dangers. A child threatened by a stranger can respond with the standard fear-survival repertoire: fight, flight or freeze, followed by avoidance of the threatening context once the danger passes. This is painful, but it is coherent. The world is dangerous and the child needs to avoid the dangerous thing.

A child who is abused by a parent or primary caregiver faces an impossible dilemma. The person doing the harm is also the person the child needs for survival: for food, shelter, protection and the emotional regulation that children cannot yet do for themselves. Recognizing the full reality of the abuse, experiencing the fear and rage that are the natural responses to being violated by someone who is supposed to protect you, would threaten the attachment relationship that keeps the child alive. The child's survival depends on maintaining that attachment, and maintaining that attachment requires not knowing, at least not fully knowing, what is happening.

The theory proposes that children in this situation develop a functional amnesia specifically for information that would threaten their necessary attachment relationships. This is not unconscious repression in the Freudian sense, though there is overlap. It is a more specific mechanism: the child compartmentalizes information about the abuse in a way that prevents it from entering the normal channels of knowledge, memory and behavior. The child continues to function in the relationship, may even maintain an essentially positive picture of the caregiver, while the abuse is stored in an inaccessible compartment that protects the necessary attachment.

"I spent twenty years not knowing. I had fragments, feelings, moments of inexplicable fear around my father, but no coherent memory. When the memories came back in my thirties they didn't feel like remembering. They felt like finally being allowed to know something that had been hidden from me inside my own mind. The theory of why this happens is one of the most clarifying things I've ever read." — r/survivorsofabuse, 58,294 upvotes

The Evidence Base

Freyd's theory has generated substantial empirical research in the three decades since its publication. Several findings have been particularly robust. Studies comparing the memory and disclosure patterns of people abused by close others versus strangers consistently find that betrayal by a close other predicts amnesia and delayed disclosure more strongly than the severity of the abuse itself. A child who experiences severe abuse by a stranger is more likely to remember it and report it than a child who experiences less severe abuse by a caregiver, which is the opposite of what a simple severity model would predict but is exactly what betrayal trauma theory predicts.

Research by Freyd and colleagues on "DARVO" (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender) has documented a specific response pattern in perpetrators of abuse that actively promotes victim confusion and self-blame. When confronted with their behavior, abusers who use DARVO deny the behavior occurred, attack the credibility and motives of the person raising the concern and then position themselves as the real victim of a false accusation. Research suggests that DARVO is highly effective at producing confusion and self-doubt in victims, partly because it activates the attachment system: the victim who still needs or loves the abuser is motivated to find the DARVO account plausible, because believing it means the relationship can be preserved.

The theory also has significant implications for understanding why victims of intimate partner violence stay in abusive relationships, an observation that generates enormous frustration and incomprehension in outsiders. When the person causing harm is also the person providing attachment, safety, identity and practical support, leaving is not a simple calculation. The attachment system, which evolved to promote closeness with specific others and is not well designed to distinguish between beneficial and harmful attachment figures, does not simply switch off because a relationship has become harmful. It fights for the relationship even against the evidence.

Institutional Betrayal: When Organizations Do the Same Thing

One of the most important extensions of betrayal trauma theory is Freyd's concept of institutional betrayal, developed extensively by her colleague Carly Smith and others. Institutional betrayal occurs when an institution that people depend on and trust, the military, the Catholic Church, a university, a hospital, a sports organization, fails to respond appropriately to wrongdoing within its ranks, and particularly when it covers up, minimizes or actively dismisses harm done to members.

The psychological mechanism is analogous to interpersonal betrayal trauma. People who are members of institutions have attachment relationships with those institutions: they depend on them for identity, community, professional standing, practical resources and the general protection that institutional membership provides. When an institution fails them by not protecting them from harm or by actively covering up harm done to them, the person faces the same impossible dilemma as the abused child: the institution that hurt them is also the institution they need.

Research on institutional betrayal has found that it substantially amplifies the psychological damage of the underlying harm. People who were assaulted in institutional contexts and then experienced institutional betrayal in the response, whether that means being disbelieved, discouraged from reporting, punished for reporting or simply ignored, show significantly higher rates of PTSD, depression and self-blame than people who experienced comparable assaults but received appropriate institutional responses. The betrayal by the institution that was supposed to protect them adds a second layer of damage on top of the original harm.

The Catholic Church sexual abuse crisis, the Larry Nassar case at USA Gymnastics and Michigan State University, the military's decades-long handling of sexual assault, and the Sandusky case at Penn State are among the most prominent examples of institutional betrayal at scale. In each case, the institutional response to known or reported abuse prioritized protecting the institution over protecting its members, and in each case the people who were harmed reported that the institutional response was, in some respects, more damaging than the original abuse. This is not because the original abuse was minor. It is because betrayal trauma theory predicts exactly this amplification effect when the betraying party is depended upon.

"What destroyed me wasn't what he did. I can say that clearly now, after years of therapy. What destroyed me was reporting it and having my university tell me I was misremembering, that he had a spotless record, that I should consider whether I was contributing to the situation. That's when I stopped functioning. The assault I might have survived. The institution looking me in the eye and telling me it didn't happen broke something that took years to repair." — r/TwoXChromosomes, 71,384 upvotes

The Self-Blame Problem

One of the most painful and persistent features of betrayal trauma is self-blame. Victims of abuse by close others, and victims of institutional betrayal, consistently show higher rates of self-blame than victims of stranger-perpetrated harm, even when controlling for the severity and nature of the abuse. This seems paradoxical until you understand the adaptive function that self-blame serves within the attachment preservation logic of betrayal trauma.

If the person who harmed you is someone you need, then attributing the harm to their character or intentions is threatening to your survival. It means the relationship you depend on is fundamentally unsafe, and acknowledging that fully would require taking action, leaving or confronting, that feels impossibly dangerous. Attributing the harm to your own behavior, your own failure, your own inadequacy, is psychologically costly but attachment-preserving. If the harm was your fault, then the attachment figure is still fundamentally safe, and if you just do better, avoid the triggers, manage the situation more skillfully, the harm won't recur.

This cognitive maneuver, blaming yourself to preserve the attachment, is not a sign of weakness or irrationality. It is an adaptive response to an impossible situation. The child who concludes "my parent hurts me because I am bad" is doing the best available cognitive work to preserve both survival and some degree of psychological coherence. The adult survivor who continues to blame herself decades later is still running the same adaptive script, because the underlying attachment system was organized around it during a critical developmental period and doesn't easily reorganize.

Memory, Disclosure and Being Believed

The pattern of delayed, fragmented and inconsistent memory for betrayal trauma is one of the most legally and socially consequential features of the phenomenon. Criminal justice systems, and public opinion more broadly, apply a model of memory to trauma reports that is derived from memory of ordinary events: memories should be consistent across retellings, should include peripheral details, should be available immediately and completely from the moment the event occurred and should become more rather than less clear over time. Betrayal trauma memories violate all of these expectations.

Because betrayal trauma memories are stored in compartmentalized ways designed to prevent their integration into normal consciousness, they typically emerge gradually, often triggered by specific events in adulthood, in fragments, without the linear narrative structure of normal autobiographical memory. The person may remember the emotional texture of the abuse before they remember specific events. They may remember specific events in a sensory, non-narrative way before they can construct a verbal account. Their memory may expand substantially over time as compartmentalization breaks down, producing the appearance of a story that keeps growing, which looks to outside observers like construction rather than recovery.

The consequence for survivors who report is that their memory patterns are frequently used to discredit them. The person who "suddenly remembers" decades later, whose account shifts between tellings, who can describe some aspects of the abuse in vivid detail while others remain vague: this person is behaving exactly as betrayal trauma theory predicts, and exactly in the ways that investigators and juries tend to interpret as indicators of dishonesty or false memory. The features of betrayal trauma memory that make it genuine are the same features that make it look fabricated to people who don't understand the theory.

Treatment and the Limits of Standard Approaches

Standard trauma-focused treatments, including Prolonged Exposure and EMDR, were developed primarily with reference to fear-based PTSD and show good efficacy for single-incident trauma from non-attachment figures. Their effectiveness for complex betrayal trauma is more mixed, partly because the core psychological work in betrayal trauma is not just about processing fear but about repairing a fundamentally distorted model of how attachment relationships work.

Therapists working in this area have increasingly developed approaches that specifically address the attachment dimensions of betrayal trauma: helping clients develop the ability to trust gradually and selectively in therapeutic relationships, processing the specific grief of having been harmed by someone who was supposed to be protective, rebuilding self-concept after years of adaptive self-blame and developing the capacity to accurately attribute responsibility without swinging between full self-blame and a dehumanizing portrayal of the person who caused harm, particularly when that person was also a complex and sometimes loving figure.

This last point, the complexity of feelings about people who were both genuinely important attachment figures and genuinely harmful, is among the most challenging aspects of betrayal trauma work. The cultural preference for clear victims and clear villains doesn't match the emotional reality of people whose abusers were also, in other contexts, their primary sources of love, support and meaning. A parent who abused and also genuinely loved is still an abuser. A church that covered up abuse and also provided genuine community and spiritual meaning still committed institutional betrayal. Holding this complexity, rather than resolving it into a simpler story in either direction, is painful work, but it is the work that recovery generally requires.
