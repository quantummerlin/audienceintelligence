Domestic Violence and the Systems That Fail Survivors

13 min read • Safety & Justice
▲ 96,492 found this important

On average, a victim of domestic violence leaves their abuser seven times before leaving for good. This statistic, widely cited in domestic violence advocacy and education, is often presented as evidence of the psychological complexity of abusive relationships, the trauma bonding that keeps people in situations that are visibly harming them. That interpretation is partly accurate. What it routinely omits is the other half of the explanation: the systems designed to help survivors consistently fail them in documented, predictable ways, and the barriers erected by inadequate shelter capacity, legal system re-traumatization, economic dependence, immigration status vulnerability, child custody entanglement and housing instability make "just leaving" a much more complex and dangerous undertaking than the phrase implies. The systems problem is at least as significant as the psychological one, and it receives far less attention in popular accounts of domestic violence.

Approximately 10 million Americans experience domestic violence annually, according to the National Domestic Violence Hotline, though this figure substantially undercounts the actual prevalence since most incidents are never reported to any authority. The Centers for Disease Control's National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey found that roughly one in four women and one in nine men experience severe intimate partner physical violence, sexual violence or stalking in their lifetime. Domestic violence accounts for 15 percent of all violent crime in the United States, is a factor in approximately half of all female homicide victims (killed by current or former intimate partners), and is a significant predictor of child abuse, child welfare system involvement and children's later violent behavior. The economic cost has been estimated at over $8 billion annually in healthcare and lost productivity, not counting criminal justice costs or the intergenerational transmission of trauma.

The number of times I watched the system make things worse for survivors — mandatory arrest policies that arrested the victim too, shelter rules that separated mothers from their teenage sons, immigration status used as a threat by abusers — it was relentless. We were supposed to be helping and we were creating obstacles." r/socialwork • 27,493 upvotes

The Violence Against Women Act, first passed in 1994, marked a significant federal commitment to funding domestic violence services and reforming the legal system's response. VAWA has been reauthorized several times, with each reauthorization expanding coverage to additional populations and strengthening certain protections. The 2022 reauthorization closed the "boyfriend loophole" that had excluded current or former dating partners from firearms restrictions applicable to spouses or cohabitants. These are real improvements. They also represent the outer limit of what a legislative framework can accomplish when the implementation infrastructure is chronically underfunded and when the cultural and systemic barriers to effective response remain largely intact.

The Shelter Crisis

Emergency shelter is the most fundamental resource for survivors leaving dangerous situations, and its chronic inadequacy is one of the most documented failures in the domestic violence response system. The National Network to End Domestic Violence's annual census found that on a single day in 2022, domestic violence programs served 67,597 adults and children. On that same day, 11,186 requests for services were unmet due to lack of resources, primarily housing. Most emergency shelters have strict limits on length of stay, typically 30 to 90 days, which is rarely sufficient time to secure stable housing in housing markets where low-income rental units have waiting lists of months to years. Transitional housing programs, which provide longer-term support, serve far fewer people than emergency shelters and have even longer waiting lists.

The intersection of domestic violence and housing instability is particularly severe in high-cost urban housing markets. A survivor leaving an abusive relationship often has little or no credit history in their own name, may have employment gaps caused by abuse-related disruption, may have an eviction record from situations the abuser caused, and may be competing for rental units in markets where landlords routinely screen out applicants with any negative housing history. The practical consequence is that leaving an abuser frequently means homelessness, which many survivors accurately weigh against the known risks of staying. Research by Tiffany Anderson and colleagues at the Urban Institute found that housing instability was one of the primary reasons survivors returned to abusive relationships, and that stable long-term housing was among the most significant predictors of not returning.

Law Enforcement Response and Its Complications

The mandatory arrest policies adopted by most states in response to advocacy in the 1980s and 1990s were intended to solve the problem of police discretion being used to ignore domestic violence calls. Before mandatory arrest, the standard police response to domestic violence calls was often to separate the parties, tell the abuser to "cool off" and leave without making an arrest, even when there was clear evidence of assault. The mandatory arrest model required officers to arrest someone whenever there was probable cause to believe a domestic assault had occurred, removing discretion from individual officers and signaling that the state took domestic violence seriously as a crime rather than as a private family matter.

The consequences were more complicated than advocates anticipated. Research by Lawrence Sherman and colleagues found that mandatory arrest reduced recidivism among employed, attached men but increased it among unemployed, marginally housed men, suggesting that the deterrence mechanism worked differently across populations. More significantly, mandatory arrest policies led to a substantial increase in dual arrests, situations in which both partners were arrested because officers could not or chose not to determine who was the primary aggressor. Research by Evan Stark and others found that dual arrest rates were particularly high in jurisdictions with mandatory arrest policies, and that dual arrest disproportionately affected women, Black women in particular, including women who had defended themselves against their abusers. The criminalization of self-defense in domestic violence contexts is one of the more troubling unintended consequences of well-intentioned reform.

"My client had bruises all over her arms from blocking punches and scratch marks on her hands from fighting back. He had a scratch on his cheek. They both got arrested under mandatory arrest. She lost her job because of the arrest record. He moved in with his girlfriend. The system took a bad situation and made her life functionally worse in ways that will follow her for years." r/legaladvice • 31,847 upvotes

Prosecutorial no-drop policies, which require prosecutors to pursue domestic violence charges regardless of whether the victim wishes to cooperate or testify, were similarly intended to remove the burden of prosecution from survivors who might be intimidated or manipulated into recanting. They have produced similar complications. A substantial body of research finds that prosecution without victim cooperation has limited deterrence effect on abusers while potentially placing survivors at greater risk when abusers are not convicted but are enraged by the prosecution attempt. Deborah Epstein's research at Georgetown Law found that survivor-centered prosecution approaches, which maintain victim choice while providing robust support, produced better safety outcomes than no-drop policies in several jurisdictions.

Economic Abuse and the Control Framework

The conceptual shift from "domestic violence" to "intimate partner violence" to Evan Stark's concept of "coercive control" represents an important evolution in understanding what abusive relationships actually involve. Physical violence is the most visible manifestation of domestic abuse, and the legal system is primarily designed to respond to physical violence as discrete criminal events. But research consistently finds that the most predictive features of dangerous relationships are not frequency or severity of physical violence but patterns of control: surveillance and monitoring of movements and communications, economic control including sabotaging employment and controlling access to money, isolation from family and friends, threats involving children or immigration status and a pervasive dynamic of domination that structures the entire relationship.

Economic abuse, in which an abuser controls all financial resources, prevents the victim from working or sabotages their employment, and uses financial dependency as a tool of control, affects an estimated 98 percent of domestic violence survivors according to research by the Center for Financial Security. Survivors leaving relationships often have no access to bank accounts, credit cards or cash, may have tax debt or credit damage caused by the abuser and may face immediate financial crisis regardless of available shelter. Economic empowerment programs, which help survivors develop financial literacy, access legal assistance to address financial damage caused by abuse and build economic independence, are among the better-supported interventions but remain significantly underfunded relative to crisis services.

Immigration Status as a Tool of Abuse

For survivors with insecure immigration status, the threat of deportation is one of the most powerful tools of abuser control, and it intersects with the legal system in ways that have become more dangerous during periods of heightened immigration enforcement. Abusers routinely threaten to report undocumented partners to immigration authorities, confiscate identity documents, withhold information about immigration status and make credible threats about what deportation would mean for US-born children. VAWA provisions for undocumented survivors, including the U visa for crime victims who cooperate with law enforcement and self-petition protections allowing abused spouses of citizens and permanent residents to seek status independently, were significant advances. But access to these protections requires navigating a complex immigration bureaucracy that few survivors without legal assistance can manage, and immigration enforcement activities near domestic violence shelters and courthouses have been documented to deter survivors from seeking help.

What Evidence-Based Response Looks Like

The research on domestic violence intervention has accumulated enough to identify what works, what is ineffective and what causes unintended harm. Danger assessment tools, particularly Jacquelyn Campbell's Danger Assessment instrument developed at Johns Hopkins, reliably identify survivors at highest risk of being killed by their partners and allow for targeted intervention with the most dangerous situations. Lethality Assessment Programs, which use danger assessment at the point of police contact to connect high-risk survivors immediately with advocacy services, have shown reductions in subsequent violence in multiple evaluations. Coordinated community response models, in which police, prosecutors, advocates, healthcare providers and housing services operate from a shared framework and share information (with consent), produce better outcomes than siloed institutional responses.

Batterer intervention programs, which became widespread in the 1980s and 1990s as an alternative to incarceration, have a weaker evidence base than originally hoped. The Duluth Model, the most widely used curriculum, has been subjected to rigorous evaluation with mixed results. Meta-analyses by Julia Babcock and colleagues found small to negligible effects of BIPs on recidivism compared with control conditions. More recent cognitive-behavioral programs show somewhat stronger effects than the Duluth curriculum for some populations, but the evidence that any current batterer intervention approach significantly reduces recidivism for the most dangerous abusers is limited. This does not mean BIPs should be abandoned, but it does mean that the criminal justice system's reliance on BIPs as the primary consequence for domestic violence offenders is based on more optimism than evidence.

The fundamental insight from decades of research and advocacy is that domestic violence is a complex problem rooted in gender inequality, economic inequality, cultural norms about intimate relationships and the specific psychology of coercive control, and that no single intervention addresses all of these simultaneously. The most effective responses combine immediate safety planning with survivors, access to legal and economic resources, housing stability, accountability mechanisms for abusers that are genuinely calibrated to safety rather than to system convenience, and the long-term cultural work of changing the norms that make intimate partner violence so prevalent. This combination is expensive, requires sustained cross-system coordination and does not produce the visible results that political systems tend to reward. It also has the best evidence of actually keeping people alive.
