The Criminalization of Poverty

13 min read • Safety & Justice
▲ 99,847 found this eye-opening

In August 2014, the Department of Justice investigation into the Ferguson, Missouri police department produced a report that described in detail a municipal justice system that had been deliberately structured to extract revenue from its poorest residents. The Ferguson Municipal Court issued approximately 1,500 warrants per year for a city of 21,000 people, mostly for minor traffic violations and ordinance infractions. Failure to pay a $150 fine would generate late fees, additional fines for failure to appear, and eventually a warrant. A warrant meant arrest. Arrest meant jail time, which meant job loss, which meant inability to pay the original fine and all subsequent fees. The DOJ report documented individual cases in which a $151 parking violation had cascaded, through the accumulation of fees, interest and additional charges over several years, into thousands of dollars in debt and multiple arrests. The people subjected to this system were disproportionately Black, and the revenue they generated funded approximately a quarter of Ferguson's municipal budget.

Ferguson was not an anomaly. It was a particularly well-documented example of a phenomenon present across the United States: the use of the criminal justice system to extract money from poor people through a cascading structure of fines, fees, surcharges and consequences for non-payment that systematically transforms minor civil infractions into criminal justice entanglement. The Brennan Center for Justice's research found that state and local governments assessed approximately $15 billion in criminal court fees annually, not including fines, and that these fees had expanded dramatically since the 1990s as states sought to offset criminal justice costs by charging them back to people who passed through the system. The fees accumulated by people who could not pay them generated debt that could result in driver's license suspension, probation revocation, additional incarceration and the permanent criminal records that foreclosed employment, housing and educational opportunities.

"I got a $300 fine for driving with expired tags. I couldn't pay it, so my license got suspended. I got pulled over six months later driving to work — which I had to do because there's no public transit where I live — and got a $500 fine for driving on a suspended license. I couldn't pay that either. This went on for three years. I now have $4,000 in fines and fees on a $300 original ticket. My license is still suspended." r/povertyfinance • 47,293 upvotes

The Supreme Court's 1983 decision in Bearden v. Georgia established that the Constitution prohibits imprisoning someone solely because they cannot afford to pay a fine, requiring courts to consider alternatives to incarceration for indigent defendants. Despite this ruling, what effectively functions as debtors' prison operates routinely in many jurisdictions through the criminalization of probation violations triggered by inability to pay supervision fees, the issuance of arrest warrants for failure to appear at hearings people cannot attend because they are working, and the incarceration of people who fail drug tests while on probation for drug offenses they are too poor to treat. The gap between the constitutional prohibition and operational reality reflects the inadequacy of public defender resources, the limited oversight of local court practices and the structural incentives that make fine and fee revenue attractive to jurisdictions facing budget pressure.

Cash Bail and Pretrial Detention

The cash bail system represents one of the most direct mechanisms through which the justice system imposes different consequences on identical legal situations based purely on financial means. A defendant who can post bail continues their life at liberty while awaiting trial. A defendant who cannot afford bail, even a modest amount of a few hundred dollars, may spend weeks, months or in extreme cases years incarcerated before their case is adjudicated. This pretrial detention has consequences that compound independently of the case outcome: job loss, housing disruption, family separation, disruption of medical care and the generation of strong incentives to plead guilty to charges one might contest, since pleading guilty to time served means immediate release while insisting on trial means continued incarceration for an uncertain duration.

The Arnold Foundation's research on pretrial detention, which used data from millions of cases to develop actuarial risk assessment tools, found that pretrial detention predicted worse case outcomes, worse subsequent criminal justice outcomes and significant collateral harm compared with community supervision at the same assessed risk level, suggesting that the pretrial detention produced by inability to post bail was itself criminogenic rather than simply a neutral waiting period. Research by Laura and John Stevenson at Columbia Law School found that defendants detained pretrial were substantially more likely to be convicted and sentenced to incarceration than comparably situated defendants who were released, a finding suggesting that pretrial detention itself produces conviction and incarceration regardless of underlying guilt, through its effects on defense preparation, plea bargaining dynamics and jury perception.

Homelessness and Anti-Homeless Laws

The criminalization of homelessness through anti-camping ordinances, sit-lie laws, anti-panhandling statutes and the aggressive enforcement of quality-of-life infractions has accelerated in many cities alongside the homelessness crisis it purports to address. The National Law Center on Homelessness and Poverty found in its 2019 survey of 187 cities that 72 percent had laws prohibiting camping in public, 76 percent prohibited sitting or lying down in public places and 58 percent prohibited loitering. Enforcement of these laws generates fines that homeless people cannot pay, warrants for failure to pay, arrests for outstanding warrants and criminal records that make obtaining housing more difficult, deepening the very condition the enforcement is nominally addressing.

The Supreme Court's 2024 decision in City of Grants Pass v. Johnson upheld cities' authority to enforce anti-camping ordinances against homeless people even when shelter beds are unavailable, overturning a Ninth Circuit ruling that such enforcement when no alternative existed constituted cruel and unusual punishment. The practical consequence of the decision is that cities can enforce laws prohibiting homeless people from sleeping in public regardless of whether any legal alternative to sleeping in public exists, creating a situation where people are subject to criminal enforcement for a condition they cannot legally remedy. Critics noted that the decision was likely to increase criminal justice system involvement among homeless people without any corresponding increase in housing availability.

"We did an analysis in our city of the cost per year of one chronically homeless person cycling through emergency services, jail and the ER. It was $87,000 per year on average. A permanent supportive housing unit in our market costs $35,000 per year including services. We're choosing the more expensive, less humane option because of where the budget lines fall across different departments." r/socialwork • 33,847 upvotes

The economic analysis of homeless criminalization consistently reaches the same conclusion: enforcement is more expensive than housing-based solutions and produces worse outcomes. A RAND Corporation analysis found that Housing First programs cost significantly less than the combination of shelter, emergency services and incarceration that characterized the prior trajectory of the same individuals. A 2017 University of California San Francisco study found that homeless people in San Francisco cost the city an average of $87,480 per year in emergency services before housing placement, compared with $20,000 per year in housing costs after placement, with improved health outcomes and reduced service utilization. These analyses are not contested. The political challenge is that the costs of homeless criminalization are distributed across multiple budget lines (police, courts, jails, emergency departments) while the savings from housing investment require upfront spending from a single housing budget, making the fiscal case for housing politically harder to make than the numbers warrant.

Predatory Financial Practices and the Law

The criminalization of poverty extends beyond the justice system into the financial system, where predatory products specifically targeting people in financial distress have been enabled by regulatory choices and constrained by others. Payday lending, which provides short-term loans at annualized interest rates often exceeding 400 percent, is legal in most states and specifically targets people who lack access to conventional credit. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's research found that the majority of payday loan fees are paid by borrowers who roll over their loans repeatedly, meaning that the product's revenue model depends on borrowers being trapped in debt rather than successfully using the product for a short-term bridge. Several states that have capped payday loan interest rates have seen their residents use the product less and show no worse financial outcomes, suggesting that the product generates more harm than benefit for most users.

Criminal justice fines and fees are assessed without regard to ability to pay in most jurisdictions, despite clear evidence that many defendants cannot pay them. The driver's license suspension for unpaid fines, used in over 40 states, is particularly destructive in areas without public transit where driving is necessary for employment: it transforms an unpaid debt into an employment barrier, an income loss that makes paying the original debt less rather than more likely, and a criminal justice risk through the criminalization of driving while suspended. Research by the Fines and Fees Justice Center has found that license suspension for non-driving reasons affects an estimated 11 million Americans and disproportionately affects Black, Hispanic and low-income drivers. Several states have repealed these suspensions after fiscal and equity analyses found that they generated more cost than revenue while significantly harming the people subjected to them.

The Evidence on What Works

The research on effective alternatives to poverty criminalization converges on several findings. Income-based or day fines, in which fine amounts are indexed to daily income, are the norm in Finland and other Nordic countries and produce comparable deterrence to fixed fines while avoiding the regressive incapacitation effects of fines that represent manageable costs for wealthier defendants and catastrophic costs for poor ones. Ability-to-pay hearings that genuinely assess defendants' financial circumstances and adjust or waive fines accordingly, as required by Bearden but rarely implemented in practice, reduce warrant generation without reducing compliance among those who can pay. Pretrial diversion programs that offer supervision and services as an alternative to detention for lower-risk defendants consistently show similar or better public safety outcomes at substantially lower cost. Decriminalization of homelessness combined with genuine investment in housing-based solutions reduces long-run costs while improving outcomes for the people involved.

The common thread in all of these approaches is that they treat poverty as a circumstance to be accommodated rather than a behavior to be punished, and that this accommodation produces better outcomes for both the individuals affected and the communities they live in. The failure to implement these approaches at scale is not primarily an evidence problem. It is a political economy problem in which the people most harmed by poverty criminalization are the least politically powerful and the jurisdictions most dependent on fine and fee revenue are the most resistant to reform. Structural change in this area, as in most areas where systematic harm falls on those with the least political voice, requires the kind of organized political power that has historically preceded legislative reform rather than following from it.
