The Criminalization of Mental Illness

13 min read • Safety & Justice
▲ 93,847 found this important

Los Angeles County Jail, Cook County Jail in Chicago and Rikers Island in New York City are three of the largest mental health treatment facilities in the United States. This is not a metaphor or an activist exaggeration. It is the clinical reality, documented by the Treatment Advocacy Center and confirmed by the facilities themselves, that these jails house more people with serious mental illness at any given moment than most psychiatric hospitals in the country. Approximately 20 percent of people in jails and 15 percent of people in state prisons have a serious mental illness, according to the Bureau of Justice Statistics, compared with roughly 5 percent in the general population. People with serious mental illness are arrested at rates far exceeding their population prevalence, cycle through jails repeatedly for low-level offenses, and frequently do not receive adequate treatment during incarceration, emerging in worse psychiatric condition than they entered and with the additional burdens of a criminal record, disrupted housing and severed treatment relationships.

How the United States arrived at this situation is a story of well-intentioned policy failure compounded by inadequate funding over several decades. The community mental health movement of the 1960s produced the Community Mental Health Centers Construction Act of 1963, which promised to replace the large state psychiatric hospitals where most seriously mentally ill people were then housed with a network of community-based treatment centers that would provide comprehensive care in the least restrictive setting. The state psychiatric hospitals were indeed emptied, largely between the 1960s and the 1980s, in a process called deinstitutionalization that reduced the state psychiatric hospital population from approximately 560,000 in 1955 to fewer than 50,000 by the late 1990s. The community mental health centers that were supposed to absorb this population were never built at the promised scale, never received the promised funding and never developed the intensive community support services that the most seriously ill people required. The result was a population of people with serious mental illness who had no institutional home, inadequate community support and strong legal protections against involuntary treatment that were intended to protect against psychiatric abuse but in practice made it very difficult to provide care to people too ill to recognize their own illness or to seek treatment voluntarily.

The criminalization that followed deinstitutionalization was not accidental. The expansion of homelessness in the 1980s, driven by the combination of deinstitutionalization, the elimination of affordable housing through federal policy changes and the crack cocaine epidemic, produced visible public disorder in urban spaces that created political pressure for enforcement responses. People in psychiatric crisis who were camping in parks, shouting on street corners or engaging in erratic behavior became targets of quality-of-life enforcement strategies, particularly in cities implementing broken windows policing theories that prioritized visible disorder as the leading indicator of neighborhood decline. Police were the first responders to psychiatric crises not because they were best equipped to address them but because they were available around the clock, had arrest authority and were the response mechanism that cities had invested in.

The Encounter Risk

The consequences of routing psychiatric crises through law enforcement include a specific and measurable risk of lethal violence. Research by the Treatment Advocacy Center found that people with untreated serious mental illness are 16 times more likely to be killed during a police encounter than other civilians approached or stopped by officers. This figure reflects the reality that police training for mental health crisis response has historically been minimal, that the tools available to police, verbal commands, physical force and firearms, are poorly suited to psychiatric crisis intervention, and that the behaviors produced by acute psychosis or severe bipolar mania, non-compliance, apparent aggression, extreme agitation and apparent inability to understand commands, are exactly the behaviors that police training treats as indicators of dangerous resistance.

The killing of people in psychiatric crisis during police encounters includes some of the most discussed and devastating examples of police use of force in recent years. Daniel Prude in Rochester, New York, was restrained face-down on the pavement while in a psychiatric crisis in March 2020 and died as a result. He had been taken to a hospital hours earlier and released. His brother had called 911 for mental health help. The encounter lasted minutes. Cases like Prude's have driven momentum for alternative response models that do not route psychiatric crises through armed police as the default first response.

Alternative Response Models

The CAHOOTS program in Eugene, Oregon, launched in 1989 through a partnership between the city and a nonprofit called White Bird Clinic, is the longest-running and most extensively studied alternative mental health crisis response model in the United States. CAHOOTS dispatches teams of medics and crisis workers rather than police to calls involving mental health crisis, intoxication, welfare checks and non-criminal disputes. The program handles approximately 24 percent of Eugene's total call volume, and police backup is requested in fewer than 1 percent of CAHOOTS calls. A 2021 analysis found that CAHOOTS handled 24,000 calls in 2019 at a cost of approximately $2.1 million, compared with an estimated $8.9 million cost for the same call volume handled by police. The program has operated for over 30 years without a reported fatal outcome.

The CAHOOTS model has inspired dozens of similar programs across the United States since 2020, including Denver's STAR program, which in its first six months handled 748 calls with no police backup required and no arrests. New York City's B-HEARD program, launched in 2021, deployed mental health teams to high-volume precincts and found that they were able to resolve the majority of calls without police involvement. The evidence base for these alternative response models is building rapidly, though the programs vary substantially in design, scope and the types of calls they accept, making direct comparison difficult.

Mental Health Courts and Diversion

Mental health courts, which provide diversion from the conventional criminal court process to treatment-focused supervision for defendants with serious mental illness, have expanded substantially since the first was established in Broward County, Florida in 1997. There are now over 400 mental health courts operating in the United States. Research on their effectiveness shows generally positive results: participants have lower recidivism rates, better housing stability and better medication adherence than comparable defendants who go through conventional court processing. A 2015 meta-analysis by Sarteschi, Vaughn and Kim found that mental health court participation significantly reduced criminal recidivism compared with control conditions.

The limitations of mental health courts are also documented. They typically serve only defendants who have already been charged, meaning they intervene after incarceration exposure rather than preventing it. They often accept only non-violent misdemeanor cases, excluding people with the most serious mental illnesses who are most likely to cycle through the system. Successful completion typically requires sustained engagement with treatment, housing and support services that may not be available at the scale required. And the voluntary nature of participation, combined with the coercive context in which the choice is made, raises questions about whether agreement to participate constitutes genuinely free and informed consent.

The Housing-Mental Health Nexus

The relationship between mental illness, homelessness and criminalization is circular and self-reinforcing in ways that cannot be addressed by any single intervention. Serious mental illness increases risk of homelessness. Homelessness worsens mental illness through the chronic stress, inadequate nutrition, sleep disruption, victimization exposure and treatment disruption it produces. Homelessness increases criminal justice contact through the criminalization of the behaviors that homeless people engage in necessarily, such as sleeping in public, and opportunistically, such as trespassing and petty theft. Criminal justice contact disrupts treatment, worsens housing prospects and increases the probability of subsequent contact. Breaking this cycle requires simultaneous intervention on multiple points, and the evidence on what works most reliably points toward Housing First.

Housing First, developed by Sam Tsemberis at Pathways to Housing in New York in the early 1990s, provides permanent housing to homeless people with mental illness without preconditions of sobriety or treatment compliance. The conventional "treatment first" approach required people to demonstrate readiness for housing by completing treatment programs, a requirement that many seriously mentally ill people could not meet. Tsemberis's insight was that housing is a prerequisite for treatment engagement rather than a reward for it, and that providing housing unconditionally produced better treatment engagement, housing stability and quality of life outcomes than requiring treatment compliance before housing was offered. Dozens of randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental evaluations conducted across North America and Europe have consistently supported this finding, making Housing First one of the most well-replicated intervention models in the homelessness and mental health research literature. The barrier to wider implementation is not evidence but funding and political will.

The Anosognosia Problem

One of the most clinically complex and politically contested issues in mental health policy is anosognosia, the neurological symptom present in a significant minority of people with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder in which the illness itself impairs the person's ability to recognize that they are ill. People with anosognosia are not refusing treatment out of preference or stubbornness. They are experiencing a symptom of their illness that prevents them from understanding that they need treatment. The result is that people who are severely ill and whose quality of life would be dramatically improved by medication and support cannot be connected to that treatment on a voluntary basis because they do not believe they need it, and the legal protections against involuntary treatment that were established to prevent psychiatric abuse prevent treatment of people who are suffering severely and whose families and communities are desperate for help.

Assisted outpatient treatment laws, often called Kendra's Law after Kendra Webdale who was killed in 1999 by a man with schizophrenia who had refused treatment, allow courts to order people with serious mental illness to comply with outpatient treatment as a condition of remaining in the community. Evaluations of Kendra's Law in New York and similar laws in other states have shown reductions in hospitalization, incarceration and homelessness among participants. The law is controversial among disability rights advocates, who argue that it substitutes coercive compliance for genuine treatment relationships and that the evidence of effectiveness is based on intensive services that work regardless of the legal coercion element. This debate is genuinely difficult and does not resolve cleanly, because it involves a real tension between the right to autonomy and the reality of a condition that impairs the capacity for autonomous decision-making in its most severe form.

The honest conclusion is that the criminalization of mental illness is both a policy failure and a resource failure, and that meaningful change requires both. Decriminalization through alternative response models, diversion programs and Housing First addresses the policy failure of routing psychiatric crises through law enforcement and jails. But these approaches require substantial investment in mental health infrastructure, housing and community support services that has not been forthcoming at anything like the scale required. The political formula that produced the current situation, deinstitutionalize without funding community alternatives, punish the resulting disorder with enforcement rather than care, and call it justice, is as clearly counterproductive as anything the American policy record contains. The question is whether the political conditions for doing something different have finally arrived, or whether the pattern of recognizing the problem and failing to fund the solution will continue.
