Restorative Justice: What It Is and What the Evidence Shows

12 min read • Safety & Justice
▲ 81,293 found this informative

In 1974, a probation officer named Mark Yantzi in Kitchener, Ontario made an unusual request to a judge. Two young men had vandalized 22 properties in their community while drunk, causing several thousand dollars of damage. Yantzi asked whether the judge would be willing to have the offenders meet with their victims directly rather than simply pay fines and move on. The judge was skeptical but agreed, and the resulting meetings, in which the offenders confronted the actual human impact of what they had done and negotiated restitution directly with those they had harmed, were transformative enough that they became recognized as one of the founding practices of the contemporary restorative justice movement. The story has since been told and retold, not because it is the first recorded instance of anything resembling victim-offender mediation, a practice with deep roots in Indigenous justice traditions around the world, but because it happened to occur at a moment when researchers, practitioners and reformers were looking for alternatives to a criminal justice system whose failures were increasingly apparent.

Restorative justice is not a single program or a single philosophy. It is a framework for responding to harm that centers the needs of those directly affected, requires accountability from those who caused harm, and aims at the repair of relationships and community rather than the punishment of individuals. Its core practices include victim-offender mediation, in which a trained facilitator guides a dialogue between the person harmed and the person who caused the harm; family group conferencing, which expands the circle to include supporters of both parties; circle processes, drawing on Indigenous traditions of communal decision-making; and community conferencing, which addresses harms with broader community impact. What these practices share is the premise that the criminal justice system's substitution of the state for the victim as the aggrieved party, while necessary for many purposes, systematically excludes the people most directly affected from the process of determining what justice means in their specific situation.

"I participated in a restorative justice process after my car was broken into and my grandmother's watch was stolen. I was initially skeptical but the prosecutor's office asked if I wanted to try it. Meeting the kid who did it — he was 17, had stolen to fund a drug habit he'd developed after his mom died — didn't excuse what he did. But I understood it, and I felt more resolved afterward than I ever would have from watching him get convicted." r/TrueOffMyChest • 34,847 upvotes

The philosophical foundations of restorative justice draw on several traditions simultaneously. Howard Zehr, whose "Changing Lenses" (1990) is the foundational text of the modern restorative justice movement, contrasts a "retributive lens," in which crime is a violation of the state's law and justice requires determining guilt and imposing proportionate punishment, with a "restorative lens," in which crime is a violation of people and relationships and justice requires repairing the harm done to the extent possible. Zehr is careful to say that retributive justice addresses real needs, including the need for accountability and proportionality, but argues that it consistently fails to address the needs of victims, who typically want to be heard, to understand why they were targeted, to have their losses acknowledged and addressed, and to be reassured they will not be harmed again, needs that a process centered on establishing guilt and setting punishment terms does not reliably meet.

What the Research Shows

The evidence base for restorative justice has grown substantially since the movement's early years, when it rested primarily on practitioner experience and case studies. The most comprehensive systematic review, conducted by Lawrence Sherman and Heather Strang and published as "Restorative Justice: The Evidence" in 2007, analyzed all rigorous evaluations of RJ programs available at that time and reached several consistent conclusions. Restorative justice substantially reduced reoffending compared with conventional criminal justice processing in most studies, with effects largest for violent offenses, which is counterintuitive for many people who assume RJ is most appropriate for minor offenses. Victim satisfaction was consistently higher in restorative processes than in conventional court processes. Offenders reported that RJ felt more fair than court proceedings and were more likely to complete their agreements than court-ordered sanctions. Post-traumatic stress symptoms were reduced in victims who participated in RJ conferences compared with those who went through conventional court processes.

A 2020 meta-analysis by Strang and colleagues updating this evidence base found 36 experimental or quasi-experimental studies of RJ, with consistent positive effects on reoffending across different offense types, locations and program designs. The effect sizes were moderate rather than dramatic, meaning that RJ is not a magic solution but a significantly better alternative than the conventional processing it replaces across multiple outcomes. Notably, the evidence of harm reduction for victims, in terms of both psychological outcomes and reduced fear, is among the strongest findings in the literature and distinguishes RJ from many other criminal justice interventions that measure success primarily in terms of recidivism.

Victim Choice and the Voluntariness Question

A central ethical requirement of restorative justice is that victim participation must be entirely voluntary, a requirement that some critics argue is not adequately enforced in practice when referrals come through prosecutors or courts that have their own institutional interests in case processing. The voluntariness concern is real and important. Victims who feel pressured into restorative processes, or who participate without adequate preparation and support, may experience those processes as additional trauma rather than as healing. The research on what constitutes adequate preparation and support is less developed than the research on RJ outcomes overall, and the quality of implementation varies considerably across programs.

The voluntariness requirement also raises questions about what happens when victims do not wish to participate. Restorative processes that require victim participation cannot be used without it, which means a significant proportion of cases that might benefit from RJ cannot use it in its standard forms. Several jurisdictions have developed offender-facing RJ processes that do not require direct victim involvement, using surrogate victims or community representatives to allow offenders to confront the impact of their actions without requiring the specific victim's participation. The evidence on these approaches is more limited but generally supportive.

"The thing critics miss about restorative justice is that it puts the victim at the center in a way the conventional system never does. The conventional system treats the victim as a witness in the state's case. RJ treats them as the person whose needs and voice matter most. For many victims, that difference alone is transformative regardless of what the offender does or doesn't do." r/CriminalJusticeReform • 16,293 upvotes

RJ in Schools

Perhaps the most widespread contemporary application of restorative practices is in educational settings, where restorative approaches to discipline have been adopted by school districts as alternatives to suspension and expulsion. The school discipline crisis that drove this adoption is well-documented: zero-tolerance policies adopted in the 1990s produced dramatic increases in suspension and expulsion rates, with Black students suspended at three times the rate of white students according to Department of Education data, a disparity that has been documented consistently across districts, states and school types and that correlates strongly with the "school-to-prison pipeline," the pathway through which school disciplinary exclusion increases the likelihood of later criminal justice involvement.

Restorative practices in schools replace punitive responses to conflict and misbehavior with community-building circles, peer mediation and resolution processes that address the underlying dynamics of the conflict rather than simply removing the student. Research on school-based RJ is more mixed than research on criminal justice applications, partly because implementation quality varies enormously and partly because schools serving the highest-need students often have the least capacity to implement RJ well. A randomized controlled trial of RJ in Pittsburgh public schools found reduced racial disparities in discipline but did not find improvements in academic outcomes. Evaluations in Australia and the UK have shown more consistently positive results. The field consensus is that restorative practices can reduce punitive discipline and its racial disparities, but that implementation quality, particularly the investment in training and facilitation capacity, is the primary determinant of whether outcomes are positive or neutral.

The Limits of Restorative Justice

Honest engagement with the evidence requires acknowledging what restorative justice does not do well and what critics have identified as genuine limitations. The evidence on RJ for the most serious violent offenses, including homicide and severe sexual assault, is limited and the ethical complexity is high. Some survivors of serious violence find the idea of any process that centers their offender's rehabilitation or transformation offensive rather than just, and their perspective deserves respect. The research suggesting that RJ produces better outcomes for violent offenses than for property offenses applies primarily to less severe violence than homicide or severe assault, and the extension to the most serious cases requires caution.

The feminist critique of restorative justice in domestic violence contexts is particularly important. Several scholars and advocates have argued that RJ processes in intimate partner violence contexts can produce pressure on victims to reconcile with abusers, that the voluntariness of victim participation is difficult to ensure when power imbalances persist outside the process, and that the community accountability mechanisms RJ relies on may not exist in the same way for intimate partner violence as for community-harm offenses. Kathleen Daly's research in Australia found that sexual assault cases going through RJ produced variable outcomes and that victim experience ranged from genuinely healing to re-traumatizing depending heavily on facilitation quality and preparation. The mainstream domestic violence advocacy community has been consistently cautious about RJ in DV contexts, and that caution is grounded in real concerns rather than reflexive conservatism about non-punitive approaches.

Why It Remains Marginal

Given the evidence base, the question of why restorative justice remains marginal in the United States criminal justice system rather than a central component of it deserves a structural answer. Several factors are relevant. The criminal justice system is organized around legal categories, procedural requirements and institutional roles that do not easily accommodate RJ processes. Prosecutors' offices measure success in conviction rates and sentence lengths, not in victim satisfaction or recidivism reduction. Defense attorneys are trained to minimize punishment rather than to facilitate accountability processes. Courts are overburdened and structured for rapid processing rather than the slower, more intensive engagement RJ requires. Police and corrections unions have institutional interests in systems that employ their members at scale.

The political economy of crime also matters. Tough-on-crime political positioning has been electorally profitable in American politics for decades, and RJ does not produce the visible, satisfying punishments that the political rhetoric of justice as retribution requires. The victims' rights movement, which has been enormously influential in criminal justice reform, has a complex relationship with RJ: while the movement correctly identified that conventional criminal justice failed victims, it often expressed that failure through demands for harsher punishment rather than for processes that actually centered victim needs and voices. Changing this political environment requires the same kind of sustained organizing, credible messenger communication and long-term investment that narrative change on any entrenched political question requires, and the organizations and communities most experienced with RJ have been building that infrastructure, though against formidable institutional inertia.
