The Prison Abolition Debate

13 min read • Safety & Justice
▲ 88,493 found this challenging

The United States incarcerates approximately 2 million people, the highest absolute number and one of the highest rates of incarceration of any country on earth, at roughly 630 per 100,000 residents. This is five times the incarceration rate of the United Kingdom, seven times that of France and thirteen times that of Japan. The US prison population grew by approximately 700 percent between 1970 and 2008 as a result of policy choices, mandatory minimum sentencing, the War on Drugs, truth-in-sentencing requirements and the overall political shift toward punitive criminal justice that characterized the 1980s and 1990s. The annual cost of incarceration exceeds $80 billion. The recidivism rate, measuring return to prison within five years of release, exceeds 75 percent according to Bureau of Justice Statistics data. Mass incarceration falls on Black Americans at a rate that produces, as Michelle Alexander argued in "The New Jim Crow" (2010), something approaching a new caste system: at current rates, one in three Black men born today can expect to be imprisoned at some point in their lives.

Prison abolition as a political and intellectual project argues that this system is not merely in need of reform but is fundamentally unreformable because its harms are structural rather than incidental. Angela Davis, whose "Are Prisons Obsolete?" (2003) is the most widely read abolitionist text, argues that prisons do not effectively address crime because they do not address the conditions that produce it, that they produce new harms including violence, trauma, family separation and lifelong disenfranchisement, and that the very existence of an enormous carceral infrastructure creates political and economic pressures toward its continued use and expansion. Davis traces the prison's origins to the convict leasing system that replaced chattel slavery after Emancipation, arguing that incarceration has functioned from its modern inception as a mechanism for controlling and exploiting Black labor rather than as a neutral response to criminal behavior.

"The strongest abolitionist argument isn't 'prisons are bad for the people inside them,' though they are. It's 'prisons don't actually make the people outside them safer, cost an enormous amount, and we've tried reforming them for fifty years without meaningfully changing their fundamental character.' That's an empirical argument about effectiveness and it deserves an empirical response." r/CriminalJusticeReform • 22,847 upvotes

The abolitionist position is often caricatured as calling for the immediate release of all incarcerated people and the elimination of all consequences for violence. This caricature is worth correcting. Most serious abolitionists articulate a long-term vision of a society in which the social conditions producing crime, poverty, lack of opportunity, housing instability, inadequate mental health care and substance use disorder treatment, racism and trauma are addressed sufficiently that incarceration is no longer necessary at anything like its current scale. The immediate practical program is less the elimination of prisons than the sustained divestment from carceral systems and investment in community-based alternatives, what advocates call "non-reformist reforms": changes that reduce the prison system's reach and power without strengthening or legitimizing it as currently constituted.

The Reformist Response

Prison reformers, who range from conservative advocates of cost-saving alternatives to incarceration to liberal advocates of racial justice in sentencing, argue that abolition is an aspirational vision that does not provide an operational roadmap for the near-term reduction of harm to the millions of people currently in the system and the millions more affected by crime. The reform position holds that while abolitionist critiques of mass incarceration are largely accurate, the appropriate response is to use available political leverage to reduce incarceration rates, improve conditions, reduce recidivism through evidence-based programming and ensure that the criminal legal system operates with reduced racial bias and greater procedural fairness.

The evidence that reform within the existing system can reduce incarceration while maintaining public safety is reasonably strong. New York State reduced its prison population by approximately 40 percent between 1999 and 2020 without a corresponding increase in crime rates. California's Realignment Act of 2011, which shifted responsibility for non-violent, non-serious, non-sex offenders from state prisons to county jails and community supervision, reduced the state prison population by roughly 25,000 people. Drug court programs, which divert drug offenders to treatment rather than incarceration, have shown reduced recidivism compared with traditional prosecution in multiple evaluations. The bipartisan First Step Act of 2018, while limited in scope, reduced sentences for some drug offenders and resulted in the early release of thousands of people. These reforms are real, incremental and not nothing.

The Question of Violent Crime

The most difficult challenge for prison abolition is the question of what to do with people who commit serious violence. The abolitionist response typically involves several components: preventing violence through investment in communities, mental health care, conflict resolution infrastructure and economic opportunity; using restorative and transformative justice processes to address violence when it does occur; providing community-based accountability mechanisms rather than carceral ones; and, where incarceration remains necessary for the protection of others, making it as humane, rehabilitative and brief as possible. The philosophical argument is that even people who have committed terrible acts retain their humanity and that indefinite incarceration in punitive conditions reflects vengeance rather than justice or safety.

The practical problem is that current alternatives to incarceration for serious violence are underdeveloped relative to the scale of need. Restorative justice processes have strong evidence of effectiveness for non-violent and less serious offenses, and some evidence of effectiveness for serious crime, but the research on transformative justice processes for the most serious violent crime is limited. The community-based mental health and social services that abolitionists propose as alternatives to incarceration currently reach only a fraction of the people who need them and are chronically underfunded. Critics of abolition argue that proposing the elimination of incarceration while the alternatives are not yet built is asking communities most affected by violence to bear the cost of a social experiment that benefits the people most insulated from that violence. This critique is taken seriously by thoughtful abolitionists like Mariame Kaba, who acknowledges that abolitionist work requires simultaneously building alternatives and reducing reliance on incarceration, and that the absence of fully developed alternatives is itself a consequence of the decision to invest in incarceration rather than in community infrastructure.

"Every time I hear 'what about the violent offenders' as a reason abolition is impossible, I think about how less than 20% of people in state prison are there for violent offenses. We've somehow built a narrative where abolition is only credible if it can solve every conceivable case, while the current system gets a pass for failing at 75% recidivism." r/PrisonReform • 19,293 upvotes

The claim that less than 20 percent of state prisoners are serving time for violent offenses requires some clarification. The Bureau of Justice Statistics reports that approximately 55 percent of state prison inmates are serving time for violent offenses, with the remainder serving for property, drug or public order offenses. The confusion partly arises from different measurements of arrest rates, prison admission rates and standing prison populations, as violent offenses carry longer sentences and therefore comprise a larger share of the standing population than of new admissions. Neither figure supports complacency about the system's current functioning, but the precise composition of the prison population matters for evaluating which populations realistic alternatives could serve and which present the hardest cases for abolitionist approaches.

Nordic Models and What They Actually Show

Scandinavian prison systems are frequently invoked in American reform and abolition debates as evidence that radically different approaches to incarceration are possible. Norway's Halden Prison, often described as the most humane prison in the world, features individual rooms with flat-screen televisions, a music studio, a jogging trail and extensive programming. Norway's recidivism rate of approximately 20 percent is dramatically lower than the US rate of over 75 percent. Finnish prisons are so normalized that incarcerated people wear their own clothes, have access to the internet for legitimate purposes and work real jobs. These systems are real and their outcomes are genuinely better than American outcomes on most measures.

The relevant caution is that Nordic countries differ from the United States in social welfare provision, income inequality, racial demography, gun prevalence, drug policy, poverty rates, housing stability and dozens of other factors that affect both crime rates and institutional outcomes. It is genuinely difficult to isolate the effect of prison conditions from the effect of the broader social context in which those prisons operate. A more useful framing than "why can't we have Norwegian prisons" is "what specific changes in conditions, programming and sentence length are associated with reduced recidivism in the research literature, and which of those changes are achievable in the current American political context?" The evidence on that question, though sparser than advocates on all sides would prefer, points toward education in prison (a 2013 RAND Corporation meta-analysis found correctional education reduced recidivism by 43 percent compared with no education), vocational training, cognitive-behavioral therapy, housing and employment support immediately post-release and shorter sentences for most non-violent offenses.

The Political Economy of Mass Incarceration

Loïc Wacquant's analysis of mass incarceration as a response to the social dislocations produced by neoliberal economic policy offers a structural framework that bridges reform and abolition perspectives. Wacquant argues in "Punishing the Poor" (2009) that the expansion of the penal state in the United States from the 1970s onward was the carceral complement to the retraction of the welfare state: as government investment in housing, healthcare, education and income support for the poor was cut, the prison expanded to manage the social disorders produced by concentrated poverty and urban disinvestment. On this account, the prison is not a failed response to crime but a successful response to a different problem: the management of surplus labor and the containment of the political consequences of extreme inequality. Reducing incarceration without addressing the underlying inequality would, on this view, simply produce other mechanisms of control rather than genuine liberation.

This analysis does not resolve the reform versus abolition debate, but it clarifies why incrementalist reform has limits: reforms that reduce incarceration without changing the underlying economic and social conditions that feed the system will tend to be re-absorbed by the system over time, as has happened repeatedly in American criminal justice history. The political consequences of this analysis are demanding, requiring simultaneous attention to criminal justice reform, economic policy, housing policy, healthcare access and racial justice in ways that are difficult to coordinate within a political system that rewards incremental and legible victories over structural transformation. The honest conclusion is that both reformers and abolitionists are responding to real features of the problem, and that the tension between them is not primarily intellectual but political, reflecting different assessments of what is achievable and at what pace in the current political environment.
