Gun Violence as a Public Health Crisis

13 min read • Safety & Justice
▲ 104,293 found this important

In 2020, gun violence became the leading cause of death for children and teenagers in the United States, surpassing motor vehicle accidents for the first time since researchers began tracking the statistic. Approximately 45,000 Americans die from gun violence annually, a figure that encompasses roughly 24,000 suicides, 19,000 homicides and about 1,500 deaths from accidents and undetermined causes. Approximately 85,000 more survive gunshot wounds with injuries that range from minor to permanently disabling. The United States has more guns per capita than any other country in the world, roughly 120 guns per 100 residents according to the Small Arms Survey, and its rate of gun deaths is roughly four times higher than peer nations after adjusting for population. These are not contested figures. They are agreed upon by researchers across the ideological spectrum. What is contested is what, if anything, should be done about them, and that contest has made gun violence research politically extraordinary in ways that have directly hindered the scientific understanding required for effective intervention.

The Dickey Amendment, passed by Congress in 1996 and named after Representative Jay Dickey of Arkansas, stipulated that none of the funds made available for injury prevention and control at the CDC could be used to advocate for or promote gun control. The amendment came in response to CDC-funded research by Arthur Kellermann and colleagues in the early 1990s that found homes with guns had higher rates of homicide and suicide. The gun lobby interpreted the research as advocacy for gun control, and successfully pressured Congress to both pass the amendment and cut $2.6 million from the CDC's budget, precisely the amount it had spent on gun research. The practical effect was a near-complete cessation of CDC gun violence research for over twenty years. The amendment was clarified in 2018 to indicate that research itself was not prohibited, only advocacy, and Congress appropriated $25 million for gun violence research in 2019 for the first time since 1996. But two decades of research infrastructure, data collection and scientific attention were lost, making the evidence base for gun violence interventions substantially weaker than for comparable public health problems.

"The Dickey Amendment story is one of the most successful suppressions of public health research in American history. We spent 20 years essentially not studying the leading preventable cause of death for young people. Jay Dickey himself said before he died that it was one of his greatest regrets and that he was wrong." r/medicine • 29,847 upvotes

Jay Dickey did indeed reverse his position. In a 2012 op-ed co-authored with Mark Rosenberg, the CDC official who had overseen the defunded research, Dickey wrote that the nation was "virtually defenseless" against gun violence because of the research gap his amendment had created, and called for Congress to fund research. Dickey died in 2017 without seeing the funding restored, and the 2019 appropriation, while a significant step, represents a fraction of what peer public health problems receive. The National Institutes of Health spent approximately $22 million on gun violence research in 2019, compared with roughly $600 million on sepsis research and $240 million on cholera research, conditions that kill far fewer Americans annually.

The Public Health Framework

The public health approach to gun violence differs from the political debate about gun rights in a crucial respect: it treats gun violence as a preventable injury problem rather than primarily as a civil liberties question, and applies the same epidemiological methods used for motor vehicle accidents, infectious disease or occupational hazards. This reframing has been productive in other injury contexts. Motor vehicle deaths in the United States peaked at approximately 54,000 in 1972 and had declined to roughly 38,000 by 2019, despite a substantially larger driving population and far more miles driven. This dramatic improvement did not come primarily from restricting who could drive. It came from a comprehensive public health approach that identified every point in the chain from crash to death and intervened: seat belts, airbags, rumble strips, graduated licensing for teenagers, drunk driving laws, roadway design, emergency response systems and vehicle safety ratings. No single intervention was sufficient. The aggregate effect of many targeted interventions was substantial.

Public health researchers apply this model to gun violence by decomposing the problem into its components. Suicide accounts for more than half of gun deaths, and gun suicide is substantially more lethal than most other suicide methods: approximately 85 percent of gun suicide attempts result in death, compared with roughly 5 percent for drug overdose attempts. Research consistently finds that the choice of method in suicidal crisis is largely a function of what is available, and that temporary removal of firearms during periods of suicidal crisis (a practice called "lethal means counseling") significantly reduces suicide completion rates without producing equivalent substitution to other methods. This is a targeted, evidence-based intervention that does not require restricting gun ownership in general, only time-limited access during identifiable crises. Emergency Risk Protection Order laws, often called "red flag" laws, provide a legal mechanism for temporarily removing firearms from people in crisis; research on Connecticut's law, enacted in 1999, estimated it prevented one suicide for every ten to eleven gun removals.

The Heterogeneity of Gun Violence

One of the most important insights of the public health approach is that "gun violence" is not a single phenomenon requiring a single intervention. It encompasses suicide, domestic violence homicide, community gun violence in urban neighborhoods, mass shootings, accidents and police shootings, and these different categories have different risk factors, different geographic distributions, different victim demographics and different effective interventions. Policy debates that treat all gun violence as a single problem requiring a single legislative response consistently produce unsatisfying results because the intervention that would most reduce suicide (reducing access during crisis) differs substantially from the intervention that would most reduce urban community violence (a mix of street outreach, economic investment and focused deterrence policing) and both differ from the interventions most relevant to mass shootings (which remain rare enough that research is limited) or domestic violence homicide (which requires a response integrated with the domestic violence intervention system).

Community violence intervention programs represent one of the better-studied approaches to urban gun homicide. Programs like Cure Violence, which recruits credible messengers from the communities most affected by gun violence to interrupt retaliatory cycles and connect high-risk individuals with services, have shown significant effects in several cities. A Johns Hopkins evaluation of Cure Violence in Baltimore found a 56 percent reduction in shootings in target areas. Chicago's READI program, which combines street outreach with transitional employment for the highest-risk individuals, showed a 79 percent reduction in shooting victimization and perpetration among program participants in a 2021 evaluation. These programs are substantially underfunded relative to their demonstrated effectiveness and the scale of the problem they address.

"What strikes me about the public health research on gun violence is how much actionable evidence exists that gets completely ignored in the political debate. We know what reduces gun suicide. We know what reduces urban shootings in the short term. We have solid evidence on domestic violence and guns. The debate we're actually having is almost entirely disconnected from the research." r/PublicHealth • 21,493 upvotes

Suicide and the Method Substitution Debate

The question of whether reducing access to highly lethal suicide methods reduces overall suicide rates or merely shifts method choice is one of the most empirically investigated in suicidology. The evidence consistently supports what researchers call "means restriction" as an effective suicide prevention strategy, with minimal substitution. Studies of bridge barriers, coal gas detoxification in the UK, pesticide restriction in Sri Lanka and analgesic tablet pack size limits in the UK all found reductions in suicide rates rather than equivalent substitution to other methods. The mechanism appears to be that suicidal crises are typically time-limited and impulsive, and that access to a highly lethal method during the brief window of maximum crisis increases completion rates substantially.

In the United States context, research by Matthew Miller and colleagues at Harvard found that states with higher gun ownership rates have higher rates of firearm suicide but not higher rates of overall suicide, implying that gun access increases the lethality of suicidal behavior in high-gun states rather than simply substituting for other methods that would occur regardless. This finding has direct policy implications: reducing firearm access during suicidal crises, whether through red flag laws, voluntary storage programs, healthcare provider conversations or other means, should reduce suicide rates. The pushback from gun rights advocates that suicidal people will simply find other methods is not supported by the empirical evidence on method substitution.

The Constitutional and Political Context

The public health framework for gun violence operates within a constitutional and political context that constrains available interventions in ways that do not apply to motor vehicles or tobacco. The Supreme Court's 2008 Heller decision established an individual right to keep a handgun in the home for self-defense, and the 2022 Bruen decision established a "text, history and tradition" standard for evaluating gun regulations that has been interpreted by lower courts in ways that have struck down several regulations previously considered constitutional. The range of gun regulations that survive current constitutional scrutiny is substantially narrower than it was before Bruen, and the litigation landscape is actively developing in ways that create substantial uncertainty about which interventions remain legally available.

Within the political system, gun policy has long been characterized by what political scientists call a preference-policy gap: public opinion on many specific gun policies substantially outpaces legislative action. Polling consistently shows majority or supermajority support for universal background checks (approximately 88 percent in most polls), red flag laws (around 70 percent) and safe storage requirements (above 75 percent). Federal legislation in these areas has been either absent or minimal, primarily because of the structure of the Senate, where rural states with high gun ownership and strong gun rights sentiment have disproportionate representation, and because of the intensity differential between gun rights advocates and gun control advocates, with the former more likely to vote on the single issue of gun policy. The Bipartisan Safer Communities Act of 2022 was the most significant federal gun legislation in nearly thirty years, closing the "boyfriend loophole" in domestic violence firearms restrictions and providing additional funding for state crisis intervention programs, but advocates note that it addressed only a narrow set of the available evidence-based interventions.

The International Comparison Problem

Comparisons between the United States and peer nations on gun violence are simultaneously illuminating and limited as policy guides. Australia's 1996 National Firearms Agreement, enacted after the Port Arthur massacre in which 35 people were killed, combined a mandatory buyback of semi-automatic weapons with comprehensive licensing and registration requirements. Research suggests it significantly reduced mass shootings and may have contributed to firearm homicide and suicide rate reductions, though the effects are debated because Australian gun violence rates were already declining before 1996. Australia is frequently cited in American gun policy debates, but direct comparison is complicated by constitutional differences, cultural differences in gun ownership norms, geographic differences and the fact that Australia's buyback targeted a specific weapon type that was not yet as widely distributed as comparable weapons in the United States.

The most honest international comparison is not "why can't the US be more like Australia" but rather "what do peer nations do, what evidence do we have about effectiveness, and which of those interventions are legally and politically accessible in the American context?" The answer to that question is more tractable than the all-or-nothing framing of most political debate suggests. Universal background checks, red flag laws, safe storage requirements, funding for community violence intervention and lethal means counseling in healthcare settings are all supported by evidence, are legally viable under current constitutional interpretation, command majority public support and would reduce gun deaths across multiple categories. The gap between what the evidence supports and what the political system produces is not primarily a knowledge problem. It is a political power problem, and the public health researchers who study gun violence are appropriately clear about that distinction.
