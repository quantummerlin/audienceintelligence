Voting Rights and Suppression: The Ongoing Battle for the Ballot

13 min read  |  Law & Governance
78,294 upvotes

On August 6, 1965, President Lyndon Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act into law. The ceremony took place in the President's Room of the Capitol, the same room where Abraham Lincoln had signed a bill freeing enslaved soldiers in 1861. Johnson told the assembled members of Congress and civil rights leaders that the law was "a triumph for freedom as huge as any victory that has ever been won on any battlefield." He was not overstating the case. The Voting Rights Act dismantled the apparatus of disenfranchisement that had effectively barred most Black Southerners from political participation for nearly a century despite their nominal constitutional right to vote under the Fifteenth Amendment.

On June 25, 2013, the Supreme Court gutted the Voting Rights Act's most effective enforcement mechanism. In Shelby County v. Holder, the Court struck down the coverage formula of Section 4(b), which determined which states and jurisdictions were required to obtain federal "preclearance" before changing their voting laws. The majority opinion by Chief Justice John Roberts argued that the formula was based on outdated data and that conditions had changed sufficiently since 1965 to render it unconstitutional. Within hours of the ruling, Texas announced it would implement a voter ID law that had previously been blocked by the Justice Department under preclearance. Other states that had been covered by preclearance quickly moved to implement restrictions that would have been challenged or blocked under the old regime.

The distance between those two moments, from Johnson's ceremony to Roberts' opinion, is a compressed version of the arc of American voting rights: periodic expansion followed by systematic contraction, with each round of restriction finding new legal and administrative forms to accomplish what previous generations did through more explicit exclusion. Understanding the current state of voting rights in America requires understanding that arc, the specific mechanisms being used today, the evidence about their effects and the genuine disagreements about how to interpret that evidence.

The Original Exclusions

The Constitution as ratified in 1788 left voting qualifications almost entirely to the states. The result was a patchwork of restriction: most states limited voting to property-owning white men, reflecting the prevailing view that only those with a tangible stake in the community should have a voice in governing it. Women were universally excluded. Free Black men could vote in several northern states in the early republic but were progressively stripped of that right as the nineteenth century wore on. The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in 1870, prohibited states from denying the right to vote on the basis of race, color or previous condition of servitude. The Nineteenth Amendment, ratified in 1920, extended the franchise to women. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment, ratified in 1964, banned poll taxes in federal elections. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment, ratified in 1971, lowered the voting age to eighteen.

Each of these expansions was met with resistance and, in several cases, with new mechanisms for restriction. The most elaborate and sustained system of post-Constitutional restriction was the one that emerged in Southern states after Reconstruction, combining poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, white primaries, registration obstacles, violence and terror to effectively re-disenfranchise Black voters despite the Fifteenth Amendment's nominal prohibition. By 1940, only 3 percent of Black Southerners of voting age were registered to vote. In Mississippi, which had the largest Black population of any state, only 0.4 percent of Black adults were registered.

The civil rights movement dismantled this system through a combination of organized resistance, federal litigation and, ultimately, federal legislation. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 addressed some barriers. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 addressed the rest, deploying federal examiners to register voters in covered jurisdictions and creating the preclearance mechanism that required jurisdictions with a history of discrimination to obtain federal approval before changing their voting laws. The results were dramatic and rapid: Black voter registration in the South went from roughly 29 percent in 1965 to 52 percent by 1967. By the 1970s, the basic infrastructure of formal legal exclusion had been dismantled.

Modern Suppression: New Forms, Familiar Functions

The tools of modern voting restriction operate differently from poll taxes and literacy tests. They are administratively neutral on their face, applying the same rules to all voters regardless of race. Their discriminatory effect, where it exists, operates through the interaction of facially neutral rules with socioeconomic conditions that are themselves the product of historical discrimination. Critics argue that this structural discrimination is no less real for being laundered through facially neutral administration. Defenders argue that facially neutral rules cannot constitute suppression, that concerns about voter fraud justify reasonable administrative requirements and that expanding vote-by-mail and same-day registration creates new vulnerabilities. The debate is genuine in some respects and cynical in others, and sorting out which arguments are which requires looking at the evidence.

Strict voter ID laws, which require voters to present specific government-issued photo identification at the polls, are the most studied and contested element of contemporary voting restriction. As of 2023, 35 states have some form of voter ID requirement, with varying stringency. Several studies have found that strict photo ID requirements reduce turnout among the specific groups least likely to possess the required ID: elderly people, low-income people, students and minority voters. A 2017 study by political scientists Zoltan Hajnal, Nazita Lajevardi and Lindsay Nielson found that strict voter ID laws significantly reduced minority voter turnout in general elections. Subsequent studies have produced mixed results, with some finding large effects and others finding smaller or insignificant effects, generating a genuine methodological debate about how to measure voter suppression.

The fraud justification for voter ID requirements does not hold up well empirically. The Brennan Center for Justice has found that documented cases of in-person voter impersonation, the only form of fraud that photo ID requirements would prevent, are extraordinarily rare. An investigation by the News21 journalism project found 10 cases of alleged in-person voter fraud in 2,068 alleged election fraud cases examined from 2000 to 2012, a period spanning more than 600 million ballots cast in general elections. The Heritage Foundation, which maintains an election fraud database and is not sympathetic to arguments against voter ID, has documented 1,334 proven instances of election fraud from 1982 to 2023, across approximately 10 billion votes cast in federal, state and local elections during that period. The ratio is not suggestive of a widespread fraud problem that requires significant barriers to voting access.

Polling Place Closures and Wait Times

One of the most documented effects of the Shelby County decision has been the closure of polling locations in formerly covered jurisdictions. The Leadership Conference Education Fund documented the closure of more than 1,600 polling places in states previously covered by preclearance between 2012 and 2018. Texas closed 750 polling locations. Arizona closed 320. Georgia closed 214. Many of these closures were concentrated in counties with large minority populations, producing the specific pattern that preclearance was designed to detect and prevent.

The mechanism connecting polling place closures to reduced participation is intuitive and well-documented. When there are fewer polling places, lines are longer. When lines are longer, people who cannot afford to wait, those with hourly jobs, childcare responsibilities, or limited transportation, are less likely to vote. Research by University of Wisconsin-Madison political scientist Barry Burden and colleagues found that polling place consolidation significantly reduced voter turnout, with the effect concentrated among lower-income and minority voters. A 2020 study found that each additional hour of waiting time reduced turnout by approximately 1 to 2 percentage points in the affected precincts.

Georgia's 2018 gubernatorial election between Stacey Abrams and Brian Kemp became a national symbol of this dynamic. Kemp, who was simultaneously the Republican candidate for governor and the state's Secretary of State with oversight over election administration, had overseen the closure of hundreds of polling places, the use of an "exact match" voter registration policy that had placed approximately 340,000 registrations in pending status and a purge of approximately 1.4 million voters from the rolls between 2012 and 2018. Kemp won by approximately 55,000 votes in an election with an estimated 1.4 million eligible voters who were not registered. Whether the registration and access obstacles swung the outcome cannot be established with certainty; that they created barriers disproportionately affecting minority voters is documented.

Voter Roll Purges

States are required by federal law to maintain accurate voter rolls, which includes removing people who have died, moved or become ineligible. Reasonable roll maintenance is uncontroversially legitimate. The controversy is about how roll maintenance is conducted, how frequently, using what criteria and with what notice to affected voters.

Aggressive purge programs have repeatedly removed eligible voters from rolls, sometimes without adequate notice and often using unreliable matching criteria. The Interstate Crosscheck program, operated by Kansas Secretary of State Kris Kobach and used by 29 states to identify voters allegedly registered in multiple states, generated a list of approximately 7.2 million potential double registrants. Investigative reporting by journalist Greg Palast found that Crosscheck's matching criteria, which matched first and last names while ignoring middle names, suffixes and dates of birth, produced an error rate that would have flagged millions of legitimate voters for purging. Research by political scientists found that the program's mismatches disproportionately fell on voters with names common in communities of color.

Ohio's "use it or lose it" purge system, which removed voters from the rolls if they had not voted in a two-year period and did not respond to a mailed notice, was challenged in Husted v. A. Philip Randolph Institute. The Supreme Court upheld Ohio's purge process in a 5-4 decision in 2018, finding that it did not violate the National Voter Registration Act. The dissent argued that triggering purge proceedings based on inactivity, rather than evidence of ineligibility, was precisely what the NVRA was designed to prevent. Research found that Ohio's purge process removed registrations from voters in Democratic-leaning urban precincts at significantly higher rates than from voters in Republican-leaning suburban and rural precincts.

Gerrymandering and Political Representation

Voting rights encompasses not only who can cast a ballot but whether that ballot has meaningful effect. Partisan gerrymandering, the practice of drawing legislative district lines to maximize one party's electoral advantage, can effectively nullify majority preferences in state and federal elections by packing opposition voters into a small number of lopsided districts while spreading majority-party voters efficiently across many competitive ones.

The mathematical effects of aggressive partisan gerrymandering can be dramatic. In North Carolina's 2012 congressional elections, Democratic candidates won approximately 51 percent of the aggregate congressional vote but won only 4 of 13 congressional seats. Republicans won 49 percent of the total vote and won 9 seats. The district map, drawn by Republican-controlled legislature, had been designed by Republican consultant Tom Hofeller using sophisticated computational tools to produce that outcome. Similar patterns existed in Pennsylvania, Ohio, Michigan and Wisconsin following the 2010 round of redistricting, in which Republicans had won control of most state legislatures and used that control to entrench their congressional and legislative majorities through favorable district lines.

The Supreme Court addressed partisan gerrymandering in Rucho v. Common Cause in 2019, holding in a 5-4 decision that federal courts could not review partisan gerrymandering claims because they presented "political questions" beyond judicial competence. Chief Justice Roberts' majority opinion acknowledged that extreme partisan gerrymandering was "unjust" and "incompatible with democratic principles" but concluded that there were no judicially manageable standards for courts to use in reviewing it. The dissent by Justice Elena Kagan argued that the majority was "gerrymand[ering] itself" by refusing to police a practice that directly undermined democratic accountability.

State courts have proven more willing to intervene. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court struck down that state's congressional gerrymander in 2018 under the state constitution's free and equal elections clause. The North Carolina Supreme Court, after a change in its partisan composition, reversed its initial approval of that state's legislative maps. Several states have created independent redistricting commissions to remove partisan control of the mapmaking process, with Arizona, California, Colorado and Michigan among those that have adopted commissions through ballot initiative. The evidence on whether commissions produce fairer maps than legislatures is generally positive, though the design details matter considerably.

Felon Disenfranchisement

The United States is an international outlier in its treatment of voting rights for people with criminal convictions. Most democracies limit disenfranchisement to the period of incarceration, if they impose it at all. In the United States, 48 states and the federal government disenfranchise people during incarceration, most disenfranchise people on parole, many disenfranchise people on probation and twelve states impose post-sentence disenfranchisement that can be permanent absent gubernatorial pardon or clemency.

The racial arithmetic of felon disenfranchisement follows directly from the racial arithmetic of mass incarceration. Because Black Americans are incarcerated at roughly five times the rate of white Americans, they are disenfranchised at roughly five times the rate. The Sentencing Project estimated that approximately 5.2 million Americans were disenfranchised in 2022, including approximately 1 in 13 Black adults nationally. In several states with the most restrictive policies, including Mississippi, Alabama, Tennessee and Kentucky, more than 1 in 5 Black adults were disenfranchised. These figures represent a substantial reduction in Black political voice in states where political outcomes are often decided by margins smaller than the disenfranchised population.

Florida's Amendment 4, passed by 64 percent of voters in 2018, restored voting rights to most people who had completed their sentences, potentially re-enfranchising up to 1.4 million Floridians. The Florida legislature subsequently passed a law, signed by Governor Ron DeSantis, conditioning restoration on the payment of all court fines and fees. For the approximately 775,000 Floridians who still owed fees and fines, this requirement effectively created a new poll tax. A federal district court blocked the fees requirement, but the Eleventh Circuit reversed that ruling on appeal. The Supreme Court declined to intervene. The fees requirement remained in force for the 2020 election.

The Access-Integrity Debate

The political debate over voting rights has increasingly been framed around a supposed tension between "access" (making voting easier and more widely available) and "integrity" (protecting elections from fraud). The framing is partly genuine and partly manufactured. There are legitimate administrative questions about how to balance convenience with verification and how to maintain accurate voter rolls without purging legitimate voters. The disagreement about how to answer those questions reflects genuine value differences about the relative costs of wrongly excluding a legitimate voter versus accepting a fraudulent one.

But the framing is also partly a rhetorical construction designed to make restrictions seem like reasonable administrative choices rather than partisan tactics. The evidence that specific restrictions, particularly strict photo ID requirements and aggressive purges, are targeted at groups that vote disproportionately for one party is substantial enough to raise serious questions about whether "election integrity" is the actual motivation. Research by political scientist Christopher Warshaw and colleagues found that voter ID laws were adopted in states with Republican legislatures at dramatically higher rates than in states with Democratic legislatures, controlling for other factors, a pattern more consistent with partisan motivation than with response to actual fraud levels.

The international comparative picture is again useful. Most comparable democracies achieve both high voter participation and high election integrity without the restrictions that have become politically contentious in the United States. Germany, Canada, Australia and the United Kingdom all have comprehensive automatic voter registration, accessible polling infrastructure, robust mail voting and no evidence of meaningful in-person voter fraud. These countries treat the goal of maximizing participation as fully compatible with election integrity, not in tension with it, because they understand that democratic legitimacy requires both.

The Path Forward

The John Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act, which would restore and update the preclearance framework struck down in Shelby County, has passed the House twice and failed to overcome Senate filibuster each time. The Freedom to Vote Act, which would establish national standards for voter registration, early voting and vote-by-mail, has similarly stalled. Without federal legislative action, voting rules will continue to be made primarily at the state level, where the incentives for partisan manipulation are direct and the accountability mechanisms for those who restrict access are limited.

State-level progress has been more varied. Approximately 20 states have implemented automatic voter registration, which adds eligible citizens to the rolls when they interact with government agencies unless they opt out. Research consistently shows that automatic registration increases both registration rates and turnout with no evidence of increased fraud. Vote-by-mail, which was dramatically expanded during the COVID-19 pandemic, proved both administratively feasible and secure, with fraud rates even lower than in-person voting; several states that expanded it have kept expanded access in place.

The deeper challenge is that voting rights in America have always been contested terrain, and the contest has never been purely about administrative efficiency or genuine fraud prevention. It has been about who gets to participate in democratic self-governance and who can be systematically excluded from it. The forms of exclusion change with each era, adapting to constitutional constraints and public norms while preserving the core function of limiting the political power of groups that threaten the interests of those who write the rules. Recognizing that continuity is not a counsel of despair; it is a prerequisite for understanding what is actually at stake in the technical-sounding debates about voter ID requirements, polling place hours and roll maintenance procedures. These are not administrative details. They are the current form of an ancient argument about who belongs in the demos.
