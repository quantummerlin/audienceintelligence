Policing Reform: What the Evidence Actually Says

12 min read  |  Law & Governance
77,493 upvotes

On May 25, 2020, a Minneapolis police officer knelt on George Floyd's neck for nine minutes and twenty-nine seconds while three colleagues stood watch. Floyd's death, recorded by bystanders and broadcast around the world, ignited protests in all fifty states and dozens of countries, producing what many observers called the largest civil rights uprising in American history. Within weeks the Minneapolis city council had voted to disband its police department, "defund the police" had become a household phrase, and the question of how policing should work had moved from academic criminology journals to the center of national political life.

Three years later the landscape looks considerably more complicated. Minneapolis did not actually disband its police department. Most major American cities that reduced police budgets in 2020 had reversed those cuts by 2022. Homicide rates rose sharply in 2020, generating fierce debate about whether reduced policing was responsible. And the political salience of crime helped Republicans make significant gains in the 2022 midterms, leading many Democratic politicians to distance themselves from the "defund" framing they had briefly embraced.

What got lost in the political turbulence was a genuine reckoning with what the research on policing actually shows. There is, in fact, a substantial and sophisticated body of evidence about what police do, what reduces crime, what harms community trust and what builds it, and how American policing differs from policing in comparable countries. That evidence is more nuanced than either the "defund" camp or the "back the blue" camp typically acknowledges, and it points toward a set of reforms that are politically uncomfortable but empirically defensible.

What Police Actually Do

The popular image of police work centers on crime fighting: chasing suspects, solving murders, arresting violent offenders. That image, reinforced by decades of television drama, does not match what most officers spend most of their time doing. Studies using systematic observation or detailed call analysis consistently find that somewhere between 80 and 90 percent of calls for service involve no criminal matter at all. Officers respond to traffic accidents, mental health crises, noise complaints, neighbor disputes, welfare checks on elderly residents, requests for directions, assistance with locked vehicles, and hundreds of other situations that have nothing to do with crime.

Peter Moskos, a sociologist and former Baltimore police officer, describes this gap as the "cop paradox": we hire officers to fight crime, train them to fight crime, and evaluate them based on crime statistics, but the overwhelming majority of their actual work involves tasks that no crime-fighting training prepares them for. The mismatch matters because those non-criminal calls frequently involve people in acute distress, including individuals experiencing mental health crises, domestic conflicts and substance use emergencies, and how those calls are handled significantly affects community well-being even when no arrests are made.

The mental health dimension is particularly striking. A 2019 Treatment Advocacy Center analysis estimated that people with untreated mental illness are sixteen times more likely to be killed during a police encounter than other civilians. Police are often the first, and sometimes the only, responders to mental health crises, not because they are well-suited to that role but because no alternative infrastructure exists. In city after city, the same individuals cycle repeatedly through police contact, emergency rooms, jails and streets, without receiving sustained treatment, because the mental health system is underfunded and the criminal justice system has become the default response to social distress.

The Stop-and-Frisk Laboratory

New York City's stop-and-frisk program offers one of the most thoroughly studied natural experiments in policing history. Beginning in the late 1990s and accelerating under Mayor Michael Bloomberg, the NYPD conducted hundreds of thousands of pedestrian stops annually, most of them concentrated in Black and Latino neighborhoods. At its peak in 2011 the department made 685,724 stops. Approximately 88 percent of those stopped were innocent. Approximately 85 percent were Black or Latino.

City officials argued that the program was responsible for New York's dramatic crime decline. The evidence does not support that claim. Criminologists Jeffrey Fagan and Bernard Harcourt analyzed the stop-and-frisk data extensively and found no meaningful relationship between stop intensity and crime rates across precincts. New York's crime decline began years before the program's expansion and continued after it was dramatically curtailed following a 2013 federal court ruling that the program constituted a pattern of unconstitutional stops. Between 2011 and 2019, stops fell by 98 percent while crime continued its downward trend.

What the program did produce was documented harm. People who were stopped, searched and released reported elevated levels of anxiety, hypervigilance and distrust of police. Research by psychologist Kevin Drakulich found that repeated non-criminal police contact predicted reduced willingness to cooperate with police investigations, meaning the program likely undermined the community information sharing that effective crime-solving depends on. The stops also consumed officer time that might have been directed toward higher-yield activities.

Hot Spots Policing: What Works

While aggressive stop-and-frisk programs have weak crime-reduction evidence and strong community harm evidence, a different approach has accumulated a more impressive research record. "Hot spots" policing rests on an observation first documented systematically by criminologist Lawrence Sherman in Minneapolis in 1989: crime is extraordinarily concentrated in space. In most cities, roughly half of all crime occurs at roughly 3 to 5 percent of addresses. The distribution is so skewed that a relatively small number of specific locations, particular bars, intersections, blocks and corners, account for a disproportionate fraction of total criminal activity.

If crime is concentrated, the logic goes, then concentrating police presence at those locations should produce meaningful reductions. The research largely supports this intuition. A 2014 Campbell Collaboration systematic review of 65 rigorous hot spots studies found that focused patrol reduced crime significantly at target locations, with most studies showing displacement effects smaller than the concentration effects, meaning crime did not simply move around the corner when officers showed up. A 2016 study by David Weisburd and colleagues found that hot spots patrol in Sacramento reduced Part I crimes by approximately 15 percent in target areas.

The key finding, however, is that how police behave at hot spots matters as much as whether they show up. Sherman and his colleagues documented what they call a "backfire" effect: when officers conduct highly aggressive enforcement at hot spots, including mass arrests and confrontational stops, crime sometimes increases because community hostility and reduced cooperation cancel out any deterrent effect. The most effective hot spots interventions combine a visible but non-confrontational presence with genuine problem-solving efforts to address the underlying conditions, lighting, abandoned properties, alcohol outlets, that generate crime at those locations.

Procedural Justice and Legitimacy

One of the most important and underappreciated concepts in policing research is legitimacy: whether people perceive police authority as rightful and worth complying with. Tom Tyler, a legal psychologist at Yale, has spent decades studying what drives legitimacy perceptions, and his findings consistently point in a direction that many law enforcement leaders find counterintuitive. People's willingness to comply with law and cooperate with police depends less on fear of punishment than on whether they believe they are treated fairly.

Tyler's "procedural justice" framework identifies four core elements that drive fairness perceptions: voice (whether people have the opportunity to explain their situation), neutrality (whether decisions seem based on consistent principles rather than personal bias), respect (whether officers treat people with dignity regardless of outcome) and trustworthiness (whether officers seem to have people's interests at heart rather than just their own authority). When encounters meet these criteria, people comply with officers' requests and cooperate with investigations even when outcomes go against them. When encounters fail these criteria, even technically lawful enforcement can produce resentment, reduced cooperation and increased hostility.

The policy implications are significant. Agencies that have invested in procedural justice training, developing officers' ability to explain decisions, listen actively and maintain respectful demeanor under stress, have seen measurable improvements in community perceptions and in some cases crime reductions. A randomized controlled trial in Louisville, Kentucky found that procedural justice training reduced civilian complaints against officers by 13 percent and use of force incidents by 28 percent over two years. The same training produced no reduction in crime clearance rates, suggesting that respectful policing does not come at the cost of effectiveness.

Use of Force: Scale, Patterns and Accountability

The United States lacks a comprehensive, mandatory, nationally standardized database of police use of force. This is not an accident of administrative oversight; it reflects decades of resistance from law enforcement organizations to systematic external accountability. What we know comes from a patchwork of sources: the FBI's voluntary Use of Force collection (which as of 2021 covered only about 40 percent of departments), investigative journalism projects like The Washington Post's Fatal Force database, and state-level reporting requirements that vary enormously in scope and quality.

The Post database, which has tracked every fatal police shooting since 2015, consistently records approximately 1,000 people shot and killed by police annually. The United States rate is dramatically higher than comparable wealthy democracies: roughly 30 to 40 times the rate in the United Kingdom, Germany or Australia. Some of this gap reflects genuine structural differences, including far higher rates of civilian gun ownership in the United States, which creates objectively higher risk for officers in some encounters. But the gap persists even in analyses that control for gun prevalence, and it is largest for unarmed individuals, where the structural argument is weakest.

Racial disparities in police use of force are well-documented but contested in their interpretation. Black Americans are killed by police at approximately 2.5 times the rate of white Americans. A widely publicized 2019 study by Roland Fryer of Harvard found no racial bias in the decision to shoot when conditioning on the full set of encounter characteristics, a finding that was quickly criticized by other researchers, including Knox, Lowe and Mummolo, who argued that Fryer's conditioning strategy introduced selection bias. A subsequent study by Knox and colleagues using a different method found clear evidence of racial bias in both lethal and non-lethal force. The methodological debate is genuine and unresolved, but the pattern of disparate outcomes is not.

Qualified Immunity and the Accountability Gap

When police officers violate citizens' constitutional rights, the primary federal remedy is a civil rights lawsuit under 42 U.S.C. Section 1983, a Reconstruction-era statute. In practice, that remedy is largely inaccessible because of a judicial doctrine called qualified immunity. The doctrine, which the Supreme Court began developing in the 1960s and dramatically expanded in 1982's Harlow v. Fitzgerald, shields government officials from civil liability unless they violated a "clearly established" right, meaning a prior court decision addressing nearly identical facts.

The practical effect is circular and profound. Officers receive immunity because no prior court has ruled their specific conduct unconstitutional. But without lawsuits reaching adjudication, courts cannot issue the rulings that would make rights "clearly established." The result is a doctrine that, as Justice Sonia Sotomayor argued in a 2015 dissent, "renders the protections of the Fourth Amendment hollow." Courts have found qualified immunity to apply in cases involving an officer who shot a ten-year-old while attempting to shoot a non-threatening dog, officers who entered the wrong house and handcuffed a man in his underwear at gunpoint while he lay in the snow, and an officer who pepper-sprayed a non-resisting high school student.

Critics of qualified immunity span the ideological spectrum. The Cato Institute, a libertarian think tank, has campaigned for the doctrine's abolition on constitutional grounds, arguing that the Supreme Court invented it without any grounding in the text of Section 1983 or its legislative history. The doctrine was not part of the original statute; it was judicially created and has been judicially expanded. In 2020 the Supreme Court declined multiple opportunities to revisit qualified immunity despite signals from several justices that the doctrine deserved reexamination. Colorado, New Mexico and New York City subsequently eliminated qualified immunity under state law, creating test cases that may eventually force a federal reckoning.

Consent Decrees and Federal Oversight

Since the early 1990s the Department of Justice has used a statutory authority under 42 U.S.C. Section 14141, enacted as part of the 1994 Crime Bill, to investigate and reform police departments that engage in patterns of unconstitutional conduct. These investigations, when they find a pattern of violations, typically result in negotiated consent decrees in which departments agree to implement specified reforms under court supervision. DOJ has entered consent decrees with dozens of departments, including Los Angeles, Detroit, New Orleans, Baltimore and Chicago.

The evidence on consent decrees is more positive than critics often acknowledge. A 2017 study by Zusha Elinson and Dan Frosch found that departments under consent decrees showed significant reductions in complaints and use of force incidents. A 2019 study by Rushin and Edwards found that consent decrees were associated with significant reductions in police homicides. The Los Angeles Police Department, which was under a consent decree from 2001 to 2013 following the Rampart scandal, underwent a genuine transformation during that period, rebuilding community relationships and improving both its civil rights record and its clearance rates.

The Trump administration dramatically reduced the use of consent decrees, and many law enforcement organizations argue that federal oversight undermines officer morale and effective policing. The evidence on the "Ferguson effect," the claim that scrutiny causes officers to disengage and crime to rise, is weak and methodologically contested. A comprehensive review by the National Academy of Sciences in 2018 found no consistent evidence that increased accountability led to crime increases, and several studies found the opposite: that improved police-community relations following reform reduced crime by increasing cooperation and information flow.

Alternative Response Models

Perhaps the most practically significant development in policing reform in recent years is not a change to policing itself but a reconsideration of which situations require police response at all. Eugene, Oregon's CAHOOTS program, which launched in 1989, dispatches mental health workers and medics instead of police to calls involving behavioral health crises, welfare checks, intoxicated individuals and similar situations. In 2019 CAHOOTS handled approximately 24,000 calls, roughly 17 percent of the Eugene Police Department's total volume. Police backup was requested in only 150 of those calls. The program costs approximately $2 million annually, compared to roughly $12 million for comparable police response.

Denver's Support Team Assisted Response (STAR) program, launched in 2020, produces similarly striking data. In its first six months STAR responded to 748 calls and required police backup exactly zero times. A subsequent evaluation found that crimes in areas served by STAR did not increase and that costs were significantly lower than police response. Denver expanded the program in 2021. Similar programs have since launched in Oakland, San Francisco, Olympia and dozens of other cities, creating a growing natural experiment in alternative response.

The evidence base for these programs is not yet as deep or as rigorously evaluated as the hot spots literature, in part because most programs are recent. But the core logic is straightforward: sending armed law enforcement officers to situations that do not require the threat of force creates unnecessary risk for both officers and the people they encounter, particularly people in mental health crises who may behave in ways that trigger use of force protocols. The appropriate response to a suicidal individual is not the same as the appropriate response to an active shooter, and treating them as equivalent creates avoidable tragedies.

Civilian Oversight: The Gap Between Form and Function

Most major American cities have some form of civilian oversight of police, whether a civilian review board, an inspector general, an independent monitor or some combination. The actual power of these bodies varies enormously. At the weak end of the spectrum, review boards have subpoena power over neither documents nor witnesses, cannot discipline officers independently, and issue recommendations that departments can and regularly do ignore. At the stronger end, a small number of oversight bodies, including San Francisco's Department of Police Accountability and the Denver Office of the Independent Monitor, have meaningful investigative capacity and genuine accountability teeth.

Research on the effectiveness of civilian oversight is complicated by this variation. Studies that lump all oversight bodies together find weak or inconsistent effects. Studies that distinguish between oversight bodies with real authority and those without find that meaningful oversight is associated with reductions in misconduct complaints, use of force incidents and racial disparities. The conclusion is not that oversight does not work but that oversight without authority does not work, a finding so obvious it barely needs a study to establish it.

Police union contracts add another layer of complexity. Many collective bargaining agreements contain provisions that seriously hamper accountability: requirements that officers be informed of complaints before being interviewed, statute of limitations on discipline that can make it impossible to act on sustained complaints, provisions requiring the destruction of disciplinary records after specified periods, and arbitration processes that routinely reinstate officers fired for documented misconduct. A 2020 study by Dhammika Dharmapala, Richard McAdams and John Rappaport found that states with stronger police collective bargaining laws had 40 percent higher rates of arrest-related deaths, a finding consistent with the hypothesis that accountability gaps produce lethal outcomes.

International Comparisons

Comparisons between American policing and policing in other wealthy democracies are often dismissed as irrelevant on the grounds that American society is uniquely violent and uniquely armed. The dismissal is partly but not entirely justified. The United States does have higher baseline rates of violent crime than most comparable countries, and it does have far higher rates of civilian gun ownership, creating genuine differences in officer risk that any honest comparison must acknowledge.

But several aspects of the international comparison are not explained by those differences. Most European countries have dramatically lower police use of force rates even after adjusting for crime rates. They also typically have significantly longer police training requirements: the United States median is about 21 weeks of academy training, compared to three years in Germany, three years in Finland and two years in the Netherlands. German officers, notably, spend a significant portion of that training on de-escalation, psychology, ethics and law, not just tactical response.

European countries also generally separate the response to mental health crises from policing entirely. In Finland, Sweden and Norway, mental health crises trigger responses from specialized health teams by default, with police called only if there is a specific security concern. The result is both better outcomes for people in crisis and fewer dangerous encounters for officers who are not trained to handle psychiatric emergencies. The lesson is not that American policing should simply import Danish practices but that alternative institutional designs are possible and have been successfully implemented in societies with different but not radically different social conditions.

The Crime Rate Question

Any serious discussion of policing reform has to grapple with the question that dominates political opposition to change: will reform increase crime? The question deserves a more careful answer than either "yes" or "no."

The sharp homicide increase in 2020 and 2021 is real and significant, and explaining it has been genuinely difficult. Criminologists have proposed multiple contributing factors: the disruption of social services and community organizations during COVID lockdowns, pandemic-era stress and economic instability, changes in gun acquisition patterns, the psychological effects of widespread civil unrest, and in some cities the withdrawal of proactive policing following the protests. The relative contribution of each factor is contested, and the research will take years to fully evaluate.

What the evidence does not support is a simple story in which oversight and accountability lead straightforwardly to crime increases. Cities that implemented meaningful reforms under consent decrees did not consistently see crime rise; many saw it fall as community cooperation improved. Cities that reduced police budgets in 2020 did not see uniformly worse crime outcomes than cities that did not. The relationship between policing levels, policing practices and crime rates is not zero, but it is considerably more complicated than either "more police reduce crime" or "police cause more harm than they prevent."

The research consistently supports a more nuanced claim: that how police deploy and behave matters more than simple headcount. Concentrated, problem-oriented patrol at high-crime locations, conducted in a way that maintains community trust and cooperation, appears to reduce crime. Diffuse, aggressive enforcement that alienates communities and reduces information sharing appears to produce neither crime reduction nor improved safety. The choice is not between safety and reform; it is between a model of policing that serves everyone and one that serves some at the expense of others.

What Reform Actually Requires

The evidence points toward a cluster of reforms that are individually well-supported and collectively represent a coherent vision of more effective and more accountable policing. None of them are easy, and each faces substantial political and institutional resistance.

First, investment in non-police response infrastructure: mental health crisis teams, mobile health services, community violence intervention programs, and the housing and substance use treatment services that address the conditions generating repeat calls. These investments reduce the burden on policing while addressing underlying needs more effectively. They require sustained public spending and political will to maintain over years and budget cycles.

Second, genuine accountability mechanisms: mandatory and standardized use of force reporting at the federal level, independent oversight with real investigative authority and disciplinary power, elimination or significant reform of qualified immunity, and collective bargaining agreements that do not shield officers from accountability for documented misconduct. These reforms face fierce resistance from law enforcement organizations but have significant bipartisan support among both civil libertarians and law-and-order conservatives who believe that legitimate authority requires accountability.

Third, evidence-based deployment: hot spots strategies with problem-oriented approaches rather than aggressive enforcement, procedural justice training embedded throughout officer development, and regular assessment of which units and practices are producing results versus which are consuming resources without measurable benefit. Departments that have adopted data-driven management in this sense have seen improvements in both crime outcomes and community perceptions.

Fourth, retraining toward de-escalation, crisis response and procedural justice, with commensurate increases in training time and investment. Twenty-one weeks is not adequate preparation for the complexity of the work police actually do. Extending training to include genuine instruction in mental health, de-escalation, cultural competence and ethics is not soft; it is a prerequisite for the kind of professional judgment the job demands.

The political environment for these reforms is difficult. Policing is one of the most culturally charged issues in American political life, entangled with racial history, partisan identity, media incentives toward extreme framings and the genuine fear that attends any conversation about crime and safety. The "defund" slogan, whatever its policy merits, created political backlash that made reform harder. The "back the blue" response, whatever its genuine concern for officer safety, has too often been used to shut down legitimate questions about accountability.

What the evidence asks of us is something harder than a slogan: a willingness to follow the research wherever it leads, to distinguish between what police should do and what they are currently asked to do, and to build the broader social infrastructure that would let officers focus on the work they are actually best positioned to perform. That is not a defunding argument or a pro-police argument. It is an argument for taking seriously both safety and justice as genuine values, and refusing to pretend that they are in conflict when the evidence suggests they need not be.
