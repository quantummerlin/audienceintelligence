The Drug War's Long Failure: Fifty Years of Evidence

13 min read  ·  Drug Policy  ·  Public Health
81,847 upvotes
Advertisement

In June 1971, President Nixon declared drug abuse "public enemy number one" and called for a comprehensive war on drugs. The announcement was framed as a response to a public health crisis — rising drug use, particularly heroin use among Vietnam veterans, and the social disruption that followed. The proposed solution combined treatment programs with aggressive law enforcement, but over subsequent decades the balance tilted decisively toward enforcement. By the 1980s and 1990s, the drug war was primarily a criminal justice undertaking, characterized by mandatory minimum sentences for drug offenses, zero-tolerance policies in schools, asset forfeiture laws that allowed police to seize property without conviction and international interdiction efforts designed to cut off drug supply at its source.

Fifty years later, the results of this approach can be evaluated against its stated goals. Drug use rates in the United States are not lower than in peer countries that took less punitive approaches. Drug overdose deaths reached a record high of over 107,000 in 2021 and remained above 100,000 in 2022, driven primarily by fentanyl contamination of the illicit drug supply — a development that prohibition itself facilitated by creating the economic incentives for increasingly potent synthetic opioids. The illicit drug market is as large and profitable as it has ever been. And the United States has spent approximately $1 trillion on drug enforcement over the past half century while incarcerating millions of people, primarily from marginalized communities, for drug offenses.

The persistence of drug prohibition despite this evidence reflects not a failure of information but a structural reality: the drug war serves interests and reflects values that its stated public health rationale does not capture. Understanding why drug policy has failed requires understanding both what the evidence shows about prohibition's effects and why that evidence has been systematically insufficient to change policy.

The Iron Law of Prohibition

The "iron law of prohibition," formulated by researcher Richard Cowan in 1986 drawing on the American experience with alcohol prohibition, holds that the more intense the enforcement of prohibition, the more potent the prohibited substance becomes. The mechanism is economic and straightforward: drug trafficking involves smuggling a product across borders and transporting it under law enforcement scrutiny. The riskier these activities are, the greater the incentive to maximize the value per unit of volume and weight. This incentive drives traffickers toward more concentrated, potent and ultimately more dangerous formulations.

The history of American drug prohibition provides multiple illustrations. Alcohol prohibition produced the shift from beer and wine to hard liquor. Crack cocaine emerged partly as a more portable, potent and profitable alternative to powder cocaine during intensified enforcement. The fentanyl crisis is the most lethal contemporary illustration: as enforcement made large-volume heroin smuggling increasingly risky and expensive, trafficking organizations developed fentanyl — which is 50 to 100 times more potent than morphine by weight, making it dramatically easier to smuggle — as a substitute. The resulting contamination of the illicit opioid supply has killed more than 500,000 Americans since 2000.

This dynamic means that the most aggressive enforcement strategies, rather than solving the overdose problem, have exacerbated it. The fentanyl crisis is not despite the drug war; it is, in significant part, because of it. This is not a comfortable conclusion for prohibition advocates, which may partly explain why it has received less policy attention than the magnitude of the crisis would seem to warrant.

The Supply Suppression Fallacy

A central premise of drug prohibition strategy is supply suppression: if the supply of drugs is reduced through enforcement, prices will rise and demand will fall. Decades of experience with this strategy have demonstrated its limitations with remarkable consistency. International drug interdiction, crop eradication programs, border enforcement and domestic trafficking investigations have all been pursued at enormous cost with at best temporary local effects on drug availability.

The fundamental problem is what economists call the balloon effect: when enforcement reduces supply in one area, production and trafficking shift to other areas and the overall supply is maintained or restored within a relatively short period. When Colombian cocaine was successfully suppressed in the 1990s, production shifted to Mexico. When Mexican enforcement targeted particular trafficking organizations, operations shifted and fragmented into smaller and more numerous groups. When heroin supply was constrained, fentanyl filled the gap. The market for psychoactive substances is demand-driven, and as long as demand exists, supply will emerge to meet it through whatever channels are least constrained by enforcement.

Research on the price and availability of major illicit drugs consistently shows that despite fifty years of enforcement, illegal drugs are more available and cheaper in real terms than they were when the drug war began. The Drug Enforcement Administration's own data shows that the inflation-adjusted price of cocaine fell by approximately 80 percent between 1981 and 2012, while purity increased. Heroin prices showed similar trends. The supply suppression strategy, evaluated on its own terms, has not achieved supply suppression.

Portugal's Experiment: What Decriminalization Actually Looks Like

In 2001, Portugal implemented the most comprehensive drug decriminalization policy of any nation to that point, removing criminal penalties for personal possession and use of all drugs — including heroin and cocaine — while maintaining prohibition on trafficking and sales. The policy redirected resources from prosecution and incarceration toward treatment and harm reduction, with individuals found in possession of personal-use quantities referred to "dissuasion commissions" that could impose administrative sanctions or mandate treatment rather than criminal prosecution.

The outcomes, documented extensively by researcher Caitlin Hughes and others, contradicted the predictions of prohibition advocates who argued that decriminalization would produce spikes in drug use and drug-related crime. Drug use rates in Portugal did not increase significantly after decriminalization; by most measures they declined modestly or remained stable while use rates in comparable European countries trended upward. HIV infection rates among people who inject drugs fell dramatically as harm reduction services — including needle exchanges and drug consumption rooms — expanded. Drug-related deaths declined. Treatment uptake increased substantially as the stigma associated with criminalization was reduced.

Portugal's experience has been replicated in various forms in other jurisdictions. Switzerland's heroin-assisted treatment program, which began in 1994 and involves providing pharmaceutical-grade heroin to people with severe opioid addiction under medical supervision, produced dramatic improvements in health and social outcomes and significant reductions in criminal activity among participants. The program has been sustained and expanded for three decades and has been implemented in several other European countries with similar results.

These international examples are not perfect — Portugal's decriminalization occurred alongside significant increases in social spending on health and social services that were part of the same reform agenda, making it difficult to attribute outcomes solely to the drug policy change. But the consistent finding across multiple countries and multiple policy variations is that treatment-centered approaches outperform punishment-centered approaches on essentially every metric relevant to public health, public safety and cost-effectiveness.

Harm Reduction: The Evidence-Based Alternative

Harm reduction is a public health approach to drug use that prioritizes reducing the negative consequences of drug use without requiring abstinence as a prerequisite for assistance. The philosophical foundation is the pragmatic recognition that some people will use drugs regardless of legal status, and that interventions that reduce the harms associated with use — disease transmission, overdose death, crime, social disintegration — are valuable even when they do not eliminate the use itself.

Harm reduction interventions include needle exchange programs that reduce HIV and hepatitis C transmission among people who inject drugs; naloxone distribution that prevents opioid overdose deaths by making the reversal agent widely available; fentanyl test strips that allow drug users to detect fentanyl contamination before ingestion; drug consumption rooms that provide a safe environment for drug use with medical staff available to respond to overdoses; and medications for addiction treatment — particularly methadone and buprenorphine for opioid use disorder — that reduce illicit drug use, overdose risk and criminal activity while improving health and social outcomes.

The evidence base for these interventions is among the strongest in public health. Needle exchange programs have been shown to reduce HIV incidence without increasing drug use. Naloxone distribution has prevented an estimated 47,000 opioid overdose deaths between 1996 and 2014 in the US alone, according to the Harm Reduction Coalition. Drug consumption rooms, which exist in countries including Canada, Australia and many European nations, have zero recorded overdose deaths despite supervising millions of drug consumption episodes. Medications for opioid use disorder are the single most effective treatment for opioid addiction, reducing overdose mortality by approximately 50 percent in consistent users.

Despite this evidence, harm reduction has faced significant political resistance in the United States, driven by the moral conviction that any intervention that does not demand abstinence is enabling drug use rather than addressing it. This resistance has cost lives. The resistance to needle exchange programs in the 1990s contributed to HIV spread among people who inject drugs at a time when other countries with needle exchanges were containing the epidemic. The resistance to drug consumption rooms has meant that opioid users die from overdoses in public spaces and alone in private spaces when supervised consumption sites would have prevented those deaths.

The Racial Arithmetic of Drug Enforcement

Drug use rates in the United States are comparable across racial groups — surveys consistently find that white Americans use drugs at similar or higher rates than Black Americans for most substances. Drug arrest, conviction and incarceration rates are dramatically different. Black Americans are arrested for drug offenses at roughly three to four times the rate of white Americans. This disparity is not explained by differential rates of use but by differential rates of enforcement: drug policing is concentrated in low-income communities of color, where street-level dealing is more visible than suburban or private drug use, and where concentrated poverty creates conditions that make drug markets more visible to law enforcement.

The disparity compounds throughout the system. Black defendants charged with drug offenses receive longer sentences on average than white defendants charged with comparable offenses, after controlling for criminal history and offense severity. The crack/powder cocaine sentencing disparity, which produced penalties 100 times higher for crack than for powder despite the pharmacological equivalence of the two substances, had its most significant real-world effect in the dramatic overincarceration of Black Americans relative to white Americans who used cocaine in its powder form.

The racial dimension of the drug war is not incidental to its history. Nixon aide John Ehrlichman's 1994 interview, published posthumously in 2016, explicitly stated that the war on drugs was designed to target Black people and antiwar liberals: "We knew we couldn't make it illegal to be either against the war or Black, but by getting the public to associate the hippies with marijuana and Blacks with heroin, and then criminalizing both heavily, we could disrupt those communities." Whether this account is accepted as fully accurate or treated as partial, it aligns with a body of historical scholarship documenting the racial politics of drug policy construction throughout the twentieth century.

Cannabis: The Natural Experiment in Legalization

Cannabis legalization in a growing number of US states provides a natural experiment in what happens when a major illegal drug market is brought under legal regulation. The evidence from Colorado, Washington, Oregon, California and other early-legalizing states is now substantial enough to draw some tentative conclusions.

Cannabis use among adults has increased modestly in states following legalization, consistent with what would be expected from reducing price and improving access. Cannabis use among adolescents has not increased in most studies, and some studies find modest decreases — possibly because legal market regulations are more effective at restricting youth access than the informal prohibition market where age verification is absent. Cannabis-related arrests, which were falling the burden of criminal records on hundreds of thousands of people annually, have essentially ceased in legal states, freeing law enforcement resources and eliminating the racial disparities in cannabis enforcement that characterized the prohibition era.

Tax revenue from legal cannabis has been substantial in some states — Colorado and California have each collected hundreds of millions of dollars annually that have been directed toward public education and substance abuse programs. The illicit market has not been eliminated — legal product prices, which include taxes, are higher than illegal market prices — but it has contracted significantly in most legal states as consumers migrate to the convenience, quality consistency and safety of legal retail.

The cannabis legalization experience does not directly translate to harder drugs with higher addiction liability. The case for cannabis legalization rests partly on the relative safety of cannabis compared to alcohol and other legal substances; the same argument is more complicated for opioids, methamphetamine or cocaine. But it demonstrates that regulated legal markets can address most of the harms of prohibition — arrest and incarceration, violence in illegal markets, adulteration and product safety, tax revenue loss — while producing drug use outcomes that are comparable to or better than prohibition.

What a Genuinely Public Health Approach Would Look Like

A genuine public health approach to drug use would begin from the premise that addiction is a medical condition requiring treatment rather than a moral failing requiring punishment. It would direct the resources currently spent on drug enforcement — the federal drug control budget exceeds $40 billion annually — toward prevention, treatment and harm reduction. It would measure success not by arrest numbers but by overdose deaths, HIV transmission, treatment access and social integration outcomes.

Concrete elements of such an approach include expanding access to medication-assisted treatment for opioid use disorder, which remains unavailable or heavily restricted in many parts of the country due to regulatory barriers and stigma. Reducing legal barriers to harm reduction services — needle exchanges, drug consumption rooms, fentanyl test strips — in the jurisdictions that still prohibit or restrict them. Decriminalizing personal possession of drugs to redirect criminal justice resources toward treatment and eliminate the barriers to treatment that criminalization creates. Addressing the social determinants of addiction — poverty, trauma, housing instability, lack of economic opportunity — that make drug dependence both more likely and harder to escape.

None of these changes requires pretending that drug use is without risk. Addiction is genuinely harmful, and preventing it through effective education and by addressing the social conditions that make it more likely is entirely consistent with a public health approach. What the public health approach rejects is the evidence-free proposition that criminal punishment of people who use drugs is an effective tool for reducing addiction or its harms. Fifty years of experience is a long time to learn a lesson. The lesson from those fifty years is clear enough to be actionable, if the political will to act on it can be found.
