Mass Incarceration and Its Hidden Costs

13 min read  |  Law & Governance
83,291 upvotes

Numbers have a way of losing their meaning when they grow large enough. 2.1 million people incarcerated is a statistic that most Americans can recite and very few can actually feel. So consider instead a single block. Pick an average city block in a low-income Black neighborhood in Chicago or Baltimore or New Orleans. The research suggests that on a block like that, between one in four and one in three working-age men have been incarcerated. Their sisters and mothers and partners are raising children whose fathers are absent not because of abandonment but because of concrete walls and iron doors. The tax base that might support the neighborhood school has been redirected to the county jail. The social fabric that once governed informal order, the norms about appropriate behavior, the networks of mutual assistance, the models of stable adult life, has been systematically destroyed by decades of aggressive enforcement and inadequate reentry support.

That block is not exceptional. It is America's policy made visible. The United States incarcerates its citizens at a rate approximately five times higher than any other wealthy democracy. It imprisons more people in absolute numbers than China, a country with four times the population and an authoritarian government that does not pretend to value personal liberty. It spends roughly $80 billion annually on corrections, more than it spends on housing assistance or food stamps. It has constructed an apparatus of confinement so vast that it reshapes entire communities, distorts democratic politics and inflicts generational damage that no reform has yet come close to reversing.

How this happened, who it happened to and what it costs beyond the dollar figures is a story that American political culture has been extraordinarily reluctant to tell clearly. This article tries to tell it.

The Numbers and Their Shape

In 1972 the United States imprisoned approximately 200,000 people. By 2009 that number had reached 2.3 million, an increase of more than 1,000 percent in less than four decades. The rate of incarceration per 100,000 residents went from roughly 100 to approximately 760 during that period. No comparable democracy experienced anything approaching this trajectory. Germany's incarceration rate today is around 69 per 100,000. Finland's is 51. Norway's is 54. France is at 104. Even the United Kingdom, which incarcerates at a higher rate than most of its European neighbors, sits around 130. The United States number is not in the same order of magnitude.

Since the 2009 peak, incarceration has declined somewhat: the total incarcerated population fell to approximately 2.1 million before the pandemic briefly reduced it further. But the structural machinery that produced mass incarceration has not been dismantled. Mandatory minimum sentences remain on the books in every state and at the federal level. Three-strikes laws remain in force. Truth-in-sentencing requirements continue to eliminate parole discretion. The policies that drove the explosion are still largely in place; the modest reductions reflect the edges being sanded off, not the edifice being torn down.

The racial distribution of incarceration is not incidental to understanding what mass incarceration is and why it exists. Black Americans are incarcerated at approximately five times the rate of white Americans. Black men specifically are incarcerated at roughly six times the rate of white men. At current rates, approximately one in three Black men born today will spend time in prison at some point during their life, compared to approximately one in seventeen white men. These are not disparities that reflect proportional differences in criminal behavior; research consistently shows that similar conduct is punished more severely when the accused is Black, and that policing and prosecution practices direct enforcement resources disproportionately into Black and Latino communities.

How It Was Built

The carceral explosion did not happen by accident. It was the product of deliberate policy choices made over several decades, driven by a combination of genuine public fear about rising crime, political incentives that rewarded toughness and punished mercy, racial backlash against civil rights gains and, in some cases, explicit strategic calculation about using the criminal justice system to suppress political organizing.

Nixon aide John Ehrlichman provided one of the most candid accounts of the political logic in a 2016 interview published by journalist Dan Baum: "The Nixon campaign in 1968, and the Nixon White House after that, had two enemies: the antiwar left and Black people. We knew we couldn't make it illegal to be either against the war or Black, but by getting the public to associate the hippies with marijuana and Blacks with heroin, and then criminalizing both heavily, we could disrupt those communities. We could arrest their leaders, raid their homes, break up their meetings and vilify them night after night on the evening news." The quote is contested by some Nixon defenders, but it articulates a dynamic that historians document through less direct channels in the policy record of the period.

The legislative machinery assembled from the 1970s through the 1990s was remarkably effective at its core function. The Rockefeller Drug Laws in New York, passed in 1973, established mandatory minimums for drug offenses that stripped judges of discretion and sent low-level dealers and users to prison for years. The federal Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 created mandatory federal guidelines and abolished parole. The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 established the notorious 100:1 crack-to-powder cocaine sentencing disparity, requiring five years for five grams of crack cocaine while requiring the same sentence for 500 grams of powder cocaine. Since crack was overwhelmingly used by Black Americans and powder cocaine more common among white users, the disparity was a mechanism for producing racial sentencing inequality through facially neutral law.

The 1994 Crime Bill, signed by President Clinton, added "three strikes" provisions at the federal level, required states to adopt truth-in-sentencing policies as a condition of receiving prison construction grants, and funded the expansion of prison capacity. It was supported by most of the Congressional Black Caucus at the time, driven partly by genuine concern about violence in Black communities, which had been devastated by the crack epidemic. Understanding mass incarceration requires holding this complexity: the policies that built the carceral state were not simply imposed on Black communities against their will; there was substantial Black political support for tough enforcement, even as the implementation of that enforcement produced racially skewed outcomes. Michelle Alexander's "The New Jim Crow," published in 2010, crystallized a counter-narrative that emphasized the continuity between slavery, Jim Crow and mass incarceration, generating both significant intellectual influence and significant pushback from historians who found the direct-continuity argument historically oversimplified.

The Fiscal Cost

The most visible cost of mass incarceration is fiscal. State and local governments spend approximately $80 billion annually on corrections. The federal Bureau of Prisons adds another $8 billion. The Vera Institute of Justice, which has conducted the most comprehensive analysis of the true cost of incarceration, estimated in 2012 that the total economic burden when ancillary costs are included, capital expenditure, employee benefits, pension obligations and retiree health care costs, approached $39 billion for states alone, substantially higher than the official corrections budget figure.

Per-inmate costs vary dramatically by state and facility type but average approximately $38,000 per year for state prisons. In high-cost states like New York and California, housing a single incarcerated person costs over $60,000 annually. The cost of incarcerating elderly people, who have dramatically lower recidivism risk and substantially higher medical costs, can exceed $100,000 per person per year. The United States now holds approximately 200,000 people aged 55 or older in its prisons and jails, a number that has grown by more than 280 percent since 1999.

The fiscal argument for criminal justice reform has attracted unusual bipartisan support. The Koch brothers' network, motivated by libertarian concerns about government overreach and fiscal responsibility, has funded criminal justice reform efforts alongside progressive organizations, producing coalitions that would have seemed improbable in the 1990s. The Right on Crime campaign, which includes Republican governors and former Bush administration officials among its signatories, has argued explicitly that mass incarceration is poor fiscal and social policy. This fiscal argument helped produce the First Step Act of 2018, which modestly reduced federal mandatory minimums, retroactively applied the Fair Sentencing Act's crack sentencing reforms and expanded programming in federal prisons.

The Cost to Families

The fiscal costs, substantial as they are, are arguably the least important of the costs mass incarceration imposes. The damage to families and communities is harder to quantify but more pervasive and more lasting.

Children with an incarcerated parent are one of the most extensively studied vulnerable populations in social science research. According to the Bureau of Justice Statistics, approximately 1.5 million children had an incarcerated parent in 2016. The figure has been as high as 2.7 million if parents in local jails are included. Research consistently finds that parental incarceration is associated with a constellation of adverse outcomes for children: higher rates of anxiety and depression, increased likelihood of school dropout, greater risk of juvenile justice involvement, reduced adult earnings and elevated probability of incarceration in their own right. Joseph Murray and colleagues, in a landmark 2012 meta-analysis, found that parental incarceration increased children's probability of antisocial behavior by a factor of 3 compared to children who experienced other forms of family separation, including parental death.

The mechanism is not simply absence. It includes the trauma of witnessing a parent's arrest, the instability of housing and caregiving arrangements during incarceration, the stigma children experience in school and community settings, the financial strain of supporting a family member inside while managing a household from outside, and the difficulty of maintaining parent-child relationships across prison visiting rooms and expensive phone calls. Prison phone service was, for decades, a privatized industry that charged rates of up to $14 per minute for collect calls, a price structure that functioned as a tax on maintaining family bonds and generated revenue for corrections agencies that received kickbacks from the phone contractors. Regulatory action beginning in 2013 reduced some of the most egregious rates, but phone and video call costs remain significant barriers for low-income families.

The Cost to Communities

Robert Sampson, the Harvard sociologist whose decades of research on Chicago neighborhoods produced the landmark study "Great American City," argues that mass incarceration has become a structural feature of certain communities, not an individual experience that affects scattered residents. In neighborhoods with very high incarceration rates, the removal and return of residents at scale disrupts the social mechanisms that informally regulate behavior and maintain community cohesion.

Sampson's research finds that concentrated incarceration reduces "collective efficacy," the shared willingness of neighbors to intervene in problems and maintain informal social control. When substantial fractions of working-age men cycle through the prison system, the social capital networks, trust relationships and informal authority structures that communities use to manage conflict and transmit norms are disrupted. The result is a feedback loop: neighborhoods with high incarceration rates have weakened informal control, which produces more crime and disorder, which produces more incarceration, which further weakens informal control.

The economic dimension is also severe. Incarceration reduces lifetime earnings by 40 percent on average. Criminal records bar people from housing assistance, student loans and a wide range of occupational licenses, creating legal barriers to legitimate employment that persist long after sentences are served. Research by Bruce Western and Becky Pettit at Harvard found that by the early 2000s the wage growth of Black men without a high school diploma was systematically overstated in official statistics because the prison population, which was growing rapidly and which had lower wages than the comparison group, was excluded from survey samples. The economic marginalization of formerly incarcerated people is not merely a private hardship; it reduces tax revenue, increases demand for public services and concentrates poverty in the communities where returned citizens settle.

The Democratic Cost

Mass incarceration has a political dimension that receives less attention than it deserves. Forty-eight states and the federal government permanently or temporarily disenfranchise people with felony convictions. In twelve states, permanent disenfranchisement requires an affirmative pardon or gubernatorial clemency. In Florida, voters passed a constitutional amendment in 2018 restoring voting rights to people with completed sentences, but the Republican-controlled legislature subsequently passed a law conditioning restoration on payment of all fines and fees, effectively creating a modern poll tax. Approximately 5.2 million Americans are currently unable to vote because of a felony conviction, including approximately 1 in 13 Black adults nationally and higher fractions in several southern states.

The disenfranchisement is compounded by a Census counting rule that has resisted reform for decades. The Census Bureau counts incarcerated people as residents of the prison county rather than their home community. Since legislative districts are drawn based on Census population counts, this means that rural communities with large prisons gain population for reapportionment purposes from the bodies of urban residents they do not represent and who cannot vote for them. The effect is to shift political representation from urban, predominantly minority communities to rural, predominantly white communities with correctional facilities, a phenomenon researchers call "prison gerrymandering." The Prison Policy Initiative estimates that this distortion affects hundreds of legislative districts at the state level.

The political economy of mass incarceration also creates powerful institutional interests in its perpetuation. The corrections industry employs approximately 800,000 people, making it one of the largest employment sectors in many rural counties. Guard unions in California, New York and Florida have been among the most consistent opponents of sentencing reform, spending millions on campaigns to defeat ballot initiatives and lobby against legislative changes that would reduce prison populations. Private prison companies, including CoreCivic (formerly CCA) and GEO Group, spend significantly on lobbying for policies that maintain incarceration levels. The financial interests invested in maintaining the carceral system are substantial, organized and politically sophisticated in a way that advocates for the incarcerated are not.

Does Incarceration Reduce Crime?

The question that defenders of the carceral system most often invoke is whether it works: whether the crime reduction benefits of incarceration justify its costs. The honest answer, supported by several decades of research, is that incarceration reduced crime significantly during the period when it was expanding from historically low levels, that those returns have substantially diminished as incarceration reached historically high levels, and that the United States is now well past the point where additional incarceration produces meaningful crime reduction at any defensible cost-benefit ratio.

The foundational research is from economists William Spelman and Steven Levitt, who estimated in the 1990s that increased incarceration accounted for somewhere between 10 and 35 percent of the crime decline of the 1990s. More recent work by Brennan Center researchers and by economists including Jeffrey Miron has revised those estimates downward, finding that incarceration's contribution to the crime decline was more modest than the initial studies suggested and that the marginal crime-reduction effect of additional incarceration has fallen sharply as the system expanded.

A 2014 National Research Council report, "The Growth of Incarceration in the United States," concluded that the high rates of incarceration do not serve the high social goals of public safety that are claimed and that the marginal crime prevention effects of incarceration are small. The report found that while prison does incapacitate individuals who would otherwise commit crimes, the deterrence effect of longer sentences is small because potential offenders are not well-informed about sentencing consequences and heavily discount future punishment. It also found that imprisonment can increase recidivism in some populations by disrupting employment, housing and family connections, meaning that incarceration's long-term crime-prevention effects may be partially offset by post-release criminogenic effects.

What Alternatives Look Like

Countries that incarcerate at dramatically lower rates than the United States do not simply tolerate more crime. Norway, with an incarceration rate of 54 per 100,000, has dramatically lower violent crime rates than the United States. Germany, the Netherlands and Finland all have substantially lower crime rates with incarceration rates one-seventh to one-twelfth of the American figure. The comparison is complicated by differences in social structure, economic inequality, gun availability and cultural factors, but the idea that American incarceration rates are a necessary consequence of the crime problem, rather than a policy choice that could be different, is not supported by international evidence.

The Scandinavian prison model, most prominently illustrated by Norway's Halden Prison, emphasizes rehabilitation, individual dignity and the goal of returning people to society as functional members. Halden has no guards in gun towers. Cells have televisions and private bathrooms. Officers eat meals with inmates. There are music studios, cooking facilities and extensive vocational training. Norway's recidivism rate, measuring reincarceration within two years of release, is approximately 20 percent. The comparable American figure is approximately 68 percent within three years. The difference cannot be entirely attributed to the prison environment, since the populations and social contexts differ, but the direction of the evidence is consistent: treating incarcerated people as human beings capable of change produces better reentry outcomes than treating them as dangers to be managed and punished.

Within the United States, several states have demonstrated that meaningful reductions in incarceration are possible without crime increases. New York reduced its prison population by more than 25 percent between 1999 and 2019 while experiencing significant crime reductions. New Jersey reduced its prison population by approximately 26 percent between 2000 and 2012 while violent crime fell by 30 percent. California reduced its prison population by roughly 25 percent following a 2011 Supreme Court order to reduce overcrowding and saw no statewide crime increase. These are not controlled experiments, and causation cannot be cleanly established, but they demonstrate that the relationship between incarceration and crime is not what tough-on-crime rhetoric implies.

The Moral Question

Behind all the statistics is a moral question that the policy debate tends to avoid: what does it mean to be a society that responds to human failure, addiction, poverty and mental illness primarily with cages? Bryan Stevenson, the founder of the Equal Justice Initiative and author of "Just Mercy," argues that the measure of a society is how it treats the most vulnerable and most condemned among its members. By that measure, American mass incarceration is not a crime policy; it is a statement about who counts as fully human and whose suffering deserves response.

The incarcerated population is not a random sample of people who have done bad things. It is disproportionately composed of people who were born into poverty, experienced childhood trauma, were inadequately educated, had untreated mental illness or addiction, and made consequential bad decisions in environments that provided few good alternatives. This is not an argument that harmful conduct should go unaddressed or that victims' suffering doesn't matter. It is an argument that punishment calibrated to the depth of human misery and structural disadvantage that produced the harm is both more just and, the evidence suggests, more effective at actually reducing harm than punishment calibrated to moral outrage.

The United States has the resources, the knowledge and the institutional capacity to reduce incarceration dramatically while maintaining public safety. What it has lacked is the political will to challenge the interests, assumptions and racial dynamics that built the carceral state. That is a harder problem than any policy detail, because it requires confronting who built this system, whom it was built to contain and what we are willing to say about ourselves when we acknowledge what our incarceration rate actually means.
