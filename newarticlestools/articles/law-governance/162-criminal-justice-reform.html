Criminal Justice Reform: What the Evidence Actually Shows

13 min read  ·  Criminal Justice  ·  Policy
74,293 upvotes
Advertisement

The United States incarcerates more people per capita than any other country on Earth — more than Russia, more than China, more than any authoritarian state whose human rights record draws regular condemnation. With roughly 2.1 million people behind bars and another 4.5 million on probation or parole, the American carceral system touches approximately one in 37 adults. The racial dimensions of this system are stark: Black Americans are incarcerated at five times the rate of white Americans, and Hispanic Americans at nearly twice the rate. One in three Black men born today can expect to be incarcerated at some point in their lives.

These numbers are not controversial. They are documented in government statistics, confirmed by independent research and acknowledged across the political spectrum, albeit with dramatically different explanations for what they mean and what should be done about them. The criminal justice reform debate in the United States is one of the few policy areas where genuine bipartisan agreement exists at the level of diagnosis — the system is expensive, the recidivism rate is high, racial disparities are real — while profound disagreement persists about causes and solutions.

Getting the causes right matters because the wrong diagnosis produces the wrong treatment. If mass incarceration is primarily a product of crime rates, then the incarceration rate is roughly appropriate and the question is how to make prisons more humane. If it is primarily a product of policy choices made in the 1980s and 1990s — mandatory minimums, three-strikes laws, the war on drugs, the elimination of judicial discretion — then those policy choices can be revisited. If it is primarily a product of racial discrimination embedded in policing, prosecution and sentencing at every level, then the required interventions go much deeper than sentencing guidelines. The evidence, examined seriously, points toward all three being true simultaneously, which makes reform both necessary and complicated.

How We Got Here: The Incarceration Explosion

The United States did not always have the world's highest incarceration rate. Through most of the twentieth century, the American incarceration rate was roughly comparable to Western European rates and had been relatively stable for decades. The explosion began in the mid-1970s and continued for forty years, with the incarceration rate quintupling between 1972 and 2009 before leveling off and beginning a modest decline.

The causes of this explosion are documented in the work of sociologist Michelle Alexander, criminologist Barry Latzer, legal scholar Jonathan Simon and many others who have studied the period from different analytical perspectives. The convergence of several policy developments drove the increase. The declaration of a "War on Drugs" under Nixon, intensified under Reagan, produced dramatically increased enforcement, prosecution and incarceration for drug offenses — particularly crack cocaine offenses, where the sentencing disparity between crack and powder cocaine (100:1 in the original Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986, reduced to 18:1 by the Fair Sentencing Act of 2010) produced racially disparate outcomes that critics argued were designed rather than incidental.

Mandatory minimum sentencing laws, adopted by Congress and most states during the 1980s and 1990s, removed judicial discretion in a wide range of cases and produced lengthy sentences for offenses that previously would have resulted in shorter terms or alternatives to incarceration. Three-strikes laws, adopted in California in 1994 and subsequently in many other states, mandated life sentences for third felony convictions regardless of the severity of the third offense. Truth-in-sentencing requirements forced offenders to serve at least 85 percent of their nominal sentence, eliminating parole as a mechanism for early release based on rehabilitation and behavior.

The cumulative effect of these policy choices was the massive expansion of the prison population that distinguishes the United States from every other wealthy democracy. The crime rate did rise significantly in the 1970s and 1980s and then fell dramatically from the mid-1990s onward — the famous crime drop. But the incarceration rate continued rising long after crime rates declined, and international comparisons consistently show that countries with similar or even higher crime rates incarcerate far fewer people by using different policy responses.

People act like mass incarceration just happened, like it's a natural response to crime that any society would have. It's not. It was a series of specific political choices made by specific politicians, many of whom explicitly knew what effects the policies would have on Black communities. The 1971 Nixon tapes where his aide John Ehrlichman says the war on drugs was designed to target "the antiwar left and Black people" — that's not a fringe academic claim. Does Incarceration Reduce Crime?

The primary justification for high incarceration rates is crime reduction. The argument runs: incarcerating offenders removes them from society (incapacitation), deters others from committing crimes by making punishment severe and credible (deterrence) and potentially rehabilitates those who are incarcerated so they return to society less likely to reoffend. The empirical evidence on each of these mechanisms is more complicated than the argument's simplicity implies.

Incapacitation does reduce crime — a person who is incarcerated cannot commit crimes in the community. But the magnitude of the effect depends heavily on who is being incarcerated. Research by economists William Spelman and Patrick Langan suggests that the incarceration boom contributed to the crime decline of the 1990s, though the magnitude of the contribution is contested. More recent work has found that the marginal contribution of further incarceration to crime reduction is very low, because the people being incarcerated at current margins are lower-risk individuals for whom the incapacitation effect is small.

Deterrence research consistently finds that the certainty of punishment matters much more than its severity. People contemplating crimes think very little about sentencing guidelines; they think primarily about whether they will get caught. Policies that increase the probability of detection and swift sanction are much more effective deterrents than policies that increase sentence length for those who are caught. Long mandatory minimum sentences, on this evidence, are expensive and damaging without providing the deterrent benefit that justifies them.

Rehabilitation outcomes from American prisons are poor. The recidivism rate — the proportion of released offenders who are arrested, convicted or reincarcerated within a defined period — is approximately 68 percent within three years and 83 percent within nine years, according to the Bureau of Justice Statistics. These numbers reflect both the failure of rehabilitation programming in prisons and the significant barriers to successful reentry that people face after incarceration: difficulty finding employment, housing restrictions, disenfranchisement, the social stigma of a criminal record and the disruption of family and community ties.

Racial Disparity: Discrimination or Differences in Offending?

The racial disparity in incarceration rates is among the most politically charged aspects of criminal justice reform. The conservative explanation emphasizes differential rates of criminal behavior as the primary driver of differential incarceration — if Black Americans are disproportionately incarcerated, it is because they commit crimes at higher rates, which in turn reflects social and economic conditions rather than criminal justice discrimination. The progressive explanation emphasizes discrimination at every stage of the system — policing concentrated in Black communities, racial bias in prosecutorial charging, differential bail setting, unequal access to effective legal representation and biased sentencing — as the primary driver.

The evidence is complex and supports multiple partial explanations simultaneously. Differential rates of police contact do drive some of the observed disparity — if drug enforcement is concentrated in heavily policed Black neighborhoods, more drug arrests will occur there regardless of actual drug use rates, which are comparable across racial groups. Research consistently shows that racial bias operates at multiple points in the system: studies using identical criminal records with racially distinguishable names find differential treatment; research on judicial sentencing finds racial disparities that persist after controlling for offense severity and criminal history; research on prosecutorial charging finds disparities in the charges brought against similarly situated defendants of different races.

The National Registry of Exonerations, which documents wrongful convictions, provides particularly stark evidence of racial disparity at the conviction stage. Black defendants are substantially more likely to be wrongfully convicted than white defendants, and the disparity is largest for capital offenses. Conviction integrity units established by prosecutors' offices to review questionable convictions consistently find that wrongful convictions are more common among Black defendants than among white defendants with similar case profiles.

I'm a public defender and I see racial disparity every single day. Same offense, similar criminal history, different outcome. Not always, not every case. But the pattern is consistent enough that I can predict with reasonable accuracy how a case will go based partly on my client's race, the race of the victim and the race of the judge. That's not statistical noise. That's the system working as it actually works rather than as it's theoretically supposed to work. — r/law, 67,384 upvotes

What Actually Works: The Evidence on Effective Alternatives

Decades of research on alternatives to traditional incarceration have produced a reasonably clear picture of what reduces crime and recidivism more effectively and at lower cost than standard imprisonment. The challenge is not primarily a lack of evidence but a lack of political will to implement what the evidence supports.

Drug courts, which divert drug-involved offenders into treatment programs with judicial supervision rather than prosecution and incarceration, consistently produce better outcomes than traditional prosecution on virtually every metric: recidivism rates are significantly lower, costs are substantially lower and public safety outcomes are comparable or better. A RAND Corporation study found that treatment is approximately seven times more cost-effective than incarceration for reducing drug use and drug-related crime. Despite this evidence, treatment capacity in the United States remains dramatically underfunded relative to incarceration capacity.

Cognitive behavioral therapy programs, delivered either in prison or in community settings, show consistent effectiveness in reducing recidivism across multiple rigorous studies. Programs that address criminogenic thinking patterns — the cognitive distortions and social learning that support criminal behavior — produce measurable reductions in reoffending that persist over extended follow-up periods. These programs are inexpensive relative to incarceration costs and have strong evidence bases, yet they are available in only a fraction of correctional settings.

Reentry support — transitional housing, job placement assistance, substance abuse treatment, mental health services and case management during the critical period immediately following release — dramatically affects the probability of successful reintegration. Research on reentry programs consistently finds significant reductions in recidivism when adequate support is provided during the first months after release, which are the highest-risk period for reoffending. The per-person cost of comprehensive reentry support is a small fraction of the cost of reincarceration.

Community-based supervision alternatives to incarceration for nonviolent offenders — intensive probation, electronic monitoring, day reporting centers — can provide meaningful accountability at a fraction of incarceration costs. Research on these alternatives generally finds comparable or better recidivism outcomes compared to incarceration for appropriate populations, along with dramatically better employment and family outcomes for the people supervised and their communities.

Bail Reform and Pretrial Detention

One of the most significant and underappreciated injustices in the American criminal justice system operates before conviction rather than after: the cash bail system. Cash bail requires defendants to pay money to be released pending trial. Those who cannot pay remain incarcerated until their case is resolved, which can take months or years for complex cases. Those who can pay, or whose families can pay, go home regardless of the risk they pose to public safety.

The practical consequence is a system where pretrial detention turns on wealth rather than risk. A wealthy defendant accused of a violent crime pays bail and goes home. A poor defendant accused of a misdemeanor cannot pay and spends weeks or months in jail awaiting trial, typically losing employment, housing and sometimes custody of children in the process. The pressure to plead guilty, regardless of actual culpability, is enormous for someone in pretrial detention who can end the ordeal by accepting a plea deal.

Research on cash bail's effects consistently finds that pretrial detention has severe consequences for defendants beyond the detention itself: detained defendants are more likely to plead guilty, receive longer sentences when convicted and have worse subsequent employment and family outcomes — not because they committed more serious offenses but because detention itself creates cascading disadvantages. Studies have found that otherwise identical defendants are 25 to 43 percent more likely to be convicted if detained pretrial compared to released, an effect attributable to the pressure of detention on plea decision-making.

Several states have reformed their bail systems to use risk assessment instruments that evaluate the probability of flight or reoffending rather than the defendant's ability to pay. New Jersey's bail reform, implemented in 2017, resulted in significant reductions in pretrial detention without increases in failure to appear or crime rates — the feared public safety consequences did not materialize. The evidence from bail reform experiments generally supports the conclusion that risk-based release can reduce pretrial detention substantially while maintaining comparable or better public safety outcomes.

The Political Economy of Reform

If the evidence on what works is relatively clear, why is criminal justice reform so difficult? The answer lies in the political economy of the carceral system — the constellation of interests, incentives and institutional dynamics that make the current system self-perpetuating despite its costs and failures.

The prison industry is a significant economic actor in many states, providing employment in rural communities where prisons are often located and generating political support from unions representing correctional officers, private prison companies and the industries that supply prison systems. The private prison industry specifically — which operates approximately 8 percent of American prisons — has a direct financial interest in high incarceration rates and spends significant resources lobbying for policies that maintain or increase those rates.

The electoral politics of crime and punishment historically favored tough-on-crime positioning. Being seen as "soft on crime" was a reliable political liability from the 1970s through the 1990s. The bipartisan consensus that produced the 1994 Crime Bill — which included provisions that expanded mandatory minimums, funded prison construction and incentivized states to adopt truth-in-sentencing requirements — was driven by politicians on both sides of the aisle who believed that criminal justice leniency was an electoral liability.

That political landscape has shifted substantially, driven by the financial unsustainability of mass incarceration, the increasing visibility of racial injustice through video documentation and the Black Lives Matter movement, conservative libertarian critiques of overcriminalization and fiscal arguments about the cost of imprisonment versus alternatives. The First Step Act, passed with bipartisan support in 2018, represented the most significant federal criminal justice reform in decades, reducing some mandatory minimums and improving reentry programming. But it was modest relative to the scale of the problem, and subsequent political dynamics have made further federal reform difficult.

Innocence and Wrongful Conviction

One dimension of criminal justice failure that commands broad moral agreement across the political spectrum is wrongful conviction. The Innocence Project, founded in 1992 to use DNA evidence to review convictions, has exonerated more than 375 people who were wrongfully convicted — including 21 who served time on death row. The National Registry of Exonerations has documented more than 3,300 exonerations since 1989, with the registry acknowledging that known exonerations represent a small fraction of actual wrongful convictions.

The most common contributors to wrongful conviction are eyewitness misidentification (present in approximately 25 percent of exoneration cases), false confessions (present in approximately 12 percent of exoneration cases, particularly prevalent in cases involving young defendants, people with intellectual disabilities and prolonged interrogation), official misconduct and the use of unvalidated or misleading forensic science. Each of these contributors is addressable through reformed practices — eyewitness identification procedures, interrogation rules, forensic science standards — that have been developed by researchers and are known to reduce error rates.

The wrongful conviction problem is both a human tragedy for those who are wrongfully imprisoned and a systemic failure that erodes the legitimacy of the criminal justice system itself. A system that cannot reliably distinguish the guilty from the innocent, that executes people who are later exonerated, and that maintains racial disparities in wrongful conviction rates has not earned the authority it exercises over human freedom and life.

The Path Forward

Criminal justice reform is not a single policy change but a complex of interconnected reforms spanning policing practices, prosecution standards, sentencing policy, pretrial detention, prison conditions, rehabilitation programming and reentry support. Progress on any one element is limited if others are not addressed simultaneously. A person who receives treatment-based diversion from incarceration still faces a conviction record that affects employment. A person who completes an effective rehabilitation program still faces a reentry environment with inadequate housing and employment support. A person who is correctly identified as low-risk and released pretrial still faces a system where wealth determines access to effective legal representation.

The evidence on what works is available. The policy tools exist. The examples from other countries — and from some states and localities within the United States — demonstrate that dramatically lower incarceration rates are compatible with public safety. What remains scarce is the political will to prioritize evidence over tradition, rehabilitation over punishment and proportionality over the political convenience of appearing tough. That scarcity is not inevitable. It is a product of specific institutional arrangements, political calculations and cultural assumptions that have changed before and can change again.
