Corporate Personhood: The Legal Fiction That Shapes Everything

12 min read  |  Law & Governance
69,384 upvotes

In January 2010, the Supreme Court of the United States decided that the First Amendment prohibits the government from restricting political expenditures by corporations. Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission was a 5-4 decision that produced immediate fury, a rare public rebuke from a sitting president during a State of the Union address, and a political controversy that has not subsided in the fifteen years since. But the ruling did not emerge from nowhere. It built on more than a century of legal evolution in the doctrine of corporate personhood, a set of judicial and legislative developments that have, in the aggregate, granted corporations many of the constitutional rights originally designed to protect human beings from government power.

Corporate personhood is one of those legal concepts that most people have heard of and very few actually understand. It is frequently discussed as though it were a single, discrete ruling, usually misattributed to an 1886 Supreme Court case involving a headnote written by a court reporter rather than the justices themselves. In reality, it is a complex, layered accumulation of judicial decisions, statutory provisions and constitutional interpretations that have developed over more than two centuries. Understanding what it actually is, how it developed and what it means for democracy requires unpacking that complexity rather than reaching for simple narratives.

What Corporate Personhood Actually Means

A corporation is a legal entity created by state law. When a corporation is formed, it comes into existence as an entity that can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued, and persist through changes in ownership and management. This basic legal personality is not particularly controversial and has been recognized in various forms since ancient Rome. A business organization that can hold title to property and enforce contracts is enormously useful for organizing complex economic activity, and most legal systems recognize some version of it.

What is more contested is the extension of constitutional rights, rights originally designed to protect natural persons from government overreach, to corporate entities. The Constitution's Bill of Rights was designed in the context of individuals facing government power. The question of whether its protections apply to corporations, and which ones, has been worked out through litigation over more than two centuries, producing a complicated and inconsistent body of doctrine.

Corporations currently have some constitutional rights and not others. They have Fourth Amendment protection against unreasonable searches, though somewhat weaker than the protection available to individuals. They have Fifth Amendment protection against takings of property without compensation. They have Fourteenth Amendment due process and equal protection rights in the sense that they can challenge government actions that arbitrarily harm their economic interests. Courts have consistently held that corporations do not have Fifth Amendment protection against self-incrimination: a corporation cannot refuse to produce documents on Fifth Amendment grounds, even though the documents may incriminate the individual officers who created them.

The most contested extensions of constitutional rights to corporations involve the First Amendment. This is where Citizens United and its predecessors become relevant, and where the implications for democratic governance are most direct.

The History: From Dartmouth to Citizens United

The legal history of corporate rights in the United States begins with Dartmouth College v. Woodward in 1819, in which Chief Justice John Marshall held that a corporation's charter was a contract that the state could not unilaterally modify without violating the Contracts Clause. This ruling was primarily about protecting the economic expectations of those who invested in corporate enterprises, not about extending human rights to artificial entities, but it established the principle that corporations had legally cognizable interests that the Constitution protected.

The much-cited 1886 case, Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad, is frequently described as the ruling that granted corporations Fourteenth Amendment equal protection rights. The actual opinion did not decide the question; Chief Justice Morrison Waite stated before oral argument that "the court does not wish to hear argument on the question whether the equal protection clause of the Fourteenth Amendment applies to these corporations. We are all of the opinion that it does." This statement appeared in the headnote prepared by court reporter Sylvester Pennoyer, not in the opinion itself, but it was subsequently cited as precedent establishing the principle as settled law. Legal historian Adam Winkler describes this as one of the most consequential errors in American legal history: a statement that was not a ruling, made before arguments were heard, that subsequently became treated as establishing corporate constitutional rights through a kind of citational laundering.

The twentieth century saw a gradual but substantial expansion of corporate First Amendment rights. In 1978, First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti struck down a Massachusetts law prohibiting corporations from spending money on referendum campaigns unrelated to their direct business interests. The Court held that political speech did not lose its First Amendment protection simply because its source was a corporation. Justice Lewis Powell's majority opinion explicitly rejected the argument that corporations' First Amendment rights were coextensive with those of individuals; rather, it held that the speech itself was protected regardless of the speaker's identity. The dissent by Justices White, Brennan and Marshall argued that allowing corporations to spend unlimited money on political referendums gave entities that existed for economic purposes and faced no ballot-box accountability a power to distort democratic deliberation that the Framers never contemplated.

Citizens United extended this logic to candidate elections. The case arose from a challenge to a provision of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002 (commonly known as McCain-Feingold) that prohibited corporations and unions from using general treasury funds for "electioneering communications" within 60 days of an election. Citizens United, a nonprofit corporation, wanted to air a film critical of Hillary Clinton during the 2008 primary season and was told it could not because the film would constitute an illegal electioneering communication. The Court's five-justice majority held that the government could not restrict political speech based on the corporate identity of the speaker, overruling two prior precedents that had allowed such restrictions. The result was that corporations could spend unlimited amounts from general treasury funds on independent political expenditures supporting or opposing candidates.

The Religious Liberty Extension: Hobby Lobby

The extension of constitutional rights to corporations reached what many observers considered a new frontier in 2014's Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores. The case concerned a provision of the Affordable Care Act requiring employer-provided health insurance to cover contraception. Hobby Lobby, a closely held for-profit corporation owned by a Christian family, argued that being required to provide contraceptive coverage violated the Religious Freedom Restoration Act by substantially burdening the owners' exercise of religion.

The Court's 5-4 majority, written by Justice Samuel Alito, held that the RFRA applied to closely held for-profit corporations and that requiring them to provide contraceptive coverage did substantially burden the religious beliefs of their owners. The decision stopped short of holding that corporations had First Amendment free exercise rights directly, grounding the ruling in the statutory RFRA rather than the Constitution. But the practical effect was to allow a for-profit corporation to claim religious beliefs and use those beliefs to deny a government-mandated benefit to its employees.

The dissent by Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg argued that the ruling opened a "minefield" by allowing commercial enterprises to use the religious beliefs of their owners to seek exemptions from laws of general applicability. If Hobby Lobby could claim religious exemption from contraception coverage, what prevented other employers from claiming religious exemptions from civil rights laws, anti-discrimination requirements or any other regulation they found religiously objectionable? The majority insisted the ruling was narrow. Critics argued that the underlying logic had no clear limiting principle.

What Corporate Political Power Looks Like

The practical effects of Citizens United and the broader deregulation of corporate political spending are visible in the campaign finance data. OpenSecrets, which tracks federal campaign finance, documented a dramatic increase in outside spending following Citizens United. Super PACs, which can accept unlimited corporate and individual contributions for independent expenditures, spent approximately $1 billion in the 2012 election cycle, $2 billion in 2016 and over $3 billion in 2020. "Dark money" organizations, which spend on politics without disclosing their donors, have spent hundreds of millions more in each cycle.

The concentrated source of much of this spending is striking. Research by political scientists Benjamin Page, Larry Bartels and Jason Seawright finds that the policy preferences of wealthy Americans and business interest groups significantly predict policy outcomes in ways that the preferences of average citizens do not. Their 2014 study, "Testing Theories of American Politics," found that when the preferences of economic elites and organized interests conflicted with the preferences of the majority of Americans, the majority preferences generally lost. The study generated significant methodological debate but pointed toward a structural feature of American politics that multiple other analyses have confirmed: government policy is more responsive to concentrated economic interests than to diffuse public preferences.

The lobbying expenditure picture is equally striking. The total reported lobbying expenditure in Washington tracks above $3.5 billion annually. The Chamber of Commerce, an organization nominally representing business broadly but in practice dominated by large corporations, regularly spends more on lobbying than any other organization. Individual corporations in heavily regulated industries, including pharmaceuticals, finance, oil and gas, and technology, each spend tens to hundreds of millions annually on direct lobbying, campaign contributions, think tank funding, academic research support and public relations. The asymmetry between corporate political resources and those available to diffuse public interests is structural and enormous.

The Originalist Puzzle

One of the intellectually interesting features of the corporate personhood debate is that it cuts across the usual ideological divide between originalists and living constitutionalists in unexpected ways. The Citizens United majority included the Court's most committed originalists: Scalia, Thomas and the author of the opinion, Kennedy. Yet the historical record for the proposition that the Founders intended constitutional rights to protect corporations is weak at best.

The Founders were familiar with corporations. The East India Company was one of the most powerful political and economic forces of the colonial era. Early American political culture was deeply suspicious of corporations, viewing them as creatures of government monopoly privilege that threatened republican equality. James Madison warned against the accumulation of power in large corporate entities. Thomas Jefferson expressed concern about the dangers of banking and commercial corporations to republican self-governance. The word "corporation" does not appear in the Constitution. The idea that the Framers intended the Bill of Rights to protect corporate entities from government regulation would have been, to most of them, bizarre.

Legal scholars like Winkler and Thom Hartmann have documented the extent to which corporate constitutional rights were built up through legal argumentation that had little basis in original meaning and that was driven largely by corporate legal advocates rather than neutral judicial development. The claim that restricting corporate political spending violates original constitutional meaning is, on this account, not originalism but its opposite: a judicially created expansion of rights that the document's authors never contemplated.

The Accountability Asymmetry

A central tension in the expansion of corporate constitutional rights is the asymmetry between rights and accountability. Natural persons who exercise constitutional rights can be voted out of office, publicly shamed, taxed, regulated in their private as well as public behavior, and ultimately face their own mortality. The individuals who control corporations face many of these constraints in their personal capacity. But the corporation itself faces a dramatically different accountability structure.

Limited liability, one of the foundational features of corporate law, means that shareholders are not personally responsible for the corporation's debts or legal judgments beyond their investment. This is an enormously valuable privilege that the state grants through incorporation, allowing capital aggregation at scales impossible if investors faced full personal liability. But it also means that corporate actors can cause harm at scales that would be impossible for individuals, while limiting the personal accountability that normally follows from causing harm. The result, as legal scholar Lynn Stout argued before her death in 2018, is a structure that systematically produces what economists call "externalities": costs imposed on third parties and society that the corporation does not bear itself.

When corporations exercise First Amendment rights to spend money on political campaigns, they do so without the accountability mechanism that democracy provides for human political actors. A politician who takes corporate money faces voters. A corporation that spends on politics faces no democratic check. If corporate political spending shifts policy in ways that harm the public, there is no mechanism for the public to hold the corporation accountable for that political activity. The shareholders can vote out management, but only if the political spending demonstrably reduces shareholder value, which may not be true even when it harms the public.

Reform Proposals and Their Limits

The most frequently proposed response to Citizens United is a constitutional amendment reversing it, declaring that constitutional rights belong to natural persons and that Congress and states may regulate campaign finance without infringing constitutional rights. Several versions of such amendments have been introduced, and a substantial advocacy movement coalesced around this approach. The practical obstacle is severe: constitutional amendments require two-thirds supermajorities in both chambers of Congress and ratification by three-fourths of states, a threshold that is currently impossible to reach on any contested issue in American politics.

Short of a constitutional amendment, several statutory and regulatory approaches could reduce the influence of corporate political spending even within the current legal framework. The DISCLOSE Act, which would require political spenders to disclose their donors, has passed the House multiple times and failed in the Senate due to Republican filibuster. Shareholder democracy requirements that give investors a vote on corporate political expenditures would bring some market accountability to corporate political spending. Public campaign financing, which provides candidates with matching funds for small donations, could reduce candidates' dependence on large corporate donors without restricting corporate speech.

At the deeper structural level, the expansion of corporate constitutional rights reflects a broader question about what corporations are and what they are for. The dominant shareholder primacy model, associated with economist Milton Friedman's 1970 argument that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits, treats the corporation as an instrument for maximizing returns to investors. An alternative stakeholder model, gaining renewed attention through movements like Benefit Corporation legislation and the Business Roundtable's 2019 statement on corporate purpose, treats corporations as entities with obligations to employees, communities and the environment, not just shareholders.

The tension between these models is directly relevant to the corporate personhood debate. If corporations are purely profit-maximizing machines with no obligations beyond legal compliance, there is little basis for claiming that they have values, beliefs or interests that constitutional protection should serve. If corporations are genuine communities of stakeholders with values and purposes beyond profit, the argument for some degree of constitutional recognition becomes more coherent, but so does the argument for holding corporations accountable to the values they claim when seeking those protections.

The current legal framework gives corporations the best of both worlds: constitutional rights that expand their political and legal power while maintaining the limited liability and shareholder primacy structures that insulate their human controllers from accountability for corporate harms. Whether that combination is defensible is a question that American democracy has not yet had a genuine opportunity to decide, in part because the entities most interested in the current arrangement are among those with the greatest capacity to shape the political discussion about it.
