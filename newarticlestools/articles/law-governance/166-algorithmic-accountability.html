Algorithmic Accountability: When Code Makes Consequential Decisions

12 min read  |  Law & Governance
71,847 upvotes

In 2015 Eric Loomis stood before a judge in La Crosse County, Wisconsin, awaiting sentencing for eluding police. The judge had before him a pre-sentence investigation report that included a risk assessment score generated by a proprietary software tool called COMPAS, developed by a company called Northpointe. The score classified Loomis as high risk for recidivism. The judge sentenced him to six years in prison, partially citing the COMPAS assessment. Loomis appealed, arguing that using a secret algorithm in his sentencing violated his due process rights because neither he nor his attorney could examine how the score was calculated or challenge its underlying methodology.

The Wisconsin Supreme Court upheld the sentence. The court acknowledged that the algorithm could not be independently examined but ruled that the risk score was only one factor among many in the judge's decision and that Loomis's due process rights were not violated by using a tool whose inner workings were trade secrets. The US Supreme Court declined to hear the case. The algorithm remained secret. The sentence stood.

The Loomis case crystallized a problem that is rapidly becoming one of the most consequential governance challenges of the twenty-first century. Automated decision systems are now embedded in virtually every domain of public and private life that significantly affects individuals: criminal sentencing and parole, child welfare removal decisions, credit scoring and lending, hiring and resume screening, healthcare resource allocation, welfare benefit eligibility, immigration enforcement and the content moderation decisions that determine whose speech gets amplified and whose gets suppressed. These systems make decisions at a scale and speed that no human process could match, often with less transparency, less accountability and fewer appeal mechanisms than the human decision-making they replaced.

The Opacity Problem

The fundamental challenge of algorithmic accountability begins with opacity. Most consequential automated decision systems are either proprietary black boxes, like COMPAS, or so complex, even when technically open, that their decision logic is practically incomprehensible. A logistic regression model with a few dozen variables can be examined, understood and challenged. A gradient boosting ensemble trained on millions of data points or a deep neural network with billions of parameters cannot be explained in any intuitive sense, even in principle. The model's developers may be able to describe its architecture and training procedure, but explaining why it assigned any specific individual a specific score or classification often lies beyond the capacity of any current interpretability technique.

Frank Pasquale, a legal scholar whose 2015 book "The Black Box Society" helped establish the academic framework for this problem, argues that opacity is not accidental. Companies and governments that deploy decision systems have incentives to resist transparency: transparency enables gaming of the system, reveals proprietary methodology, exposes embarrassing or legally problematic decision patterns and creates avenues for challenge and appeal that slow down automated processing. The black box is, in many cases, a deliberate design choice rather than a technical limitation.

The trade secret protection that shielded COMPAS in the Loomis case illustrates the legal vacuum. No existing federal law requires that automated decision systems used in government contexts be explainable to the people they affect. The Administrative Procedure Act requires that agency decisions be justified by "reasoned explanation," but courts have been reluctant to apply that standard in ways that would require agencies to explain algorithmic decisions they themselves may not fully understand. The result is that the rule-of-law principle requiring that people be able to understand and contest the reasons for decisions made about them is being quietly hollowed out by technical complexity, one system at a time.

ProPublica, COMPAS and the Definition of Fairness

In 2016 investigative outlet ProPublica published an analysis of COMPAS risk scores assigned to more than 7,000 people arrested in Broward County, Florida. The analysis found that Black defendants were nearly twice as likely as white defendants to be falsely flagged as future criminals, while white defendants were more likely to be incorrectly labeled as low risk. Among defendants who did not go on to reoffend, 44.9 percent of Black defendants had been classified as high risk, compared to 23.5 percent of white defendants. Among those who did reoffend, 48 percent of white defendants had been classified as low risk, compared to 28 percent of Black defendants.

Northpointe responded that the tool was race-neutral because it produced similar predictive accuracy rates across racial groups overall. This counter-argument highlighted a profound problem that has since generated an extensive technical and philosophical literature: multiple definitions of algorithmic fairness are mathematically incompatible with each other. Calibration (equal accuracy across groups), equalized false positive rates and equalized false negative rates cannot all be satisfied simultaneously when base rates of the predicted outcome differ between groups, as they do in recidivism prediction because of underlying differences in incarceration history driven by racially biased enforcement. You must choose which fairness criterion to prioritize. COMPAS prioritized predictive accuracy overall; ProPublica's analysis showed the distributional consequences for false positives by race. Both measurements were correct. The disagreement was not about math but about values.

The COMPAS controversy spawned a literature on "algorithmic fairness" that is now one of the most active research areas in computer science, statistics and law. Researchers have catalogued more than twenty distinct formal definitions of algorithmic fairness, shown that most pairs of definitions are mutually incompatible and begun developing methods for making the tradeoffs between definitions explicit rather than hidden. The practical implication is that there is no such thing as a "fair algorithm" in the abstract; there are only algorithms that embody specific value choices about whose errors are more tolerable and how to distribute the costs of prediction mistakes across groups.

Predictive Policing and Feedback Loops

Predictive policing systems, which use historical crime data to direct patrol resources toward locations or individuals predicted to be at elevated crime risk, illustrate a different form of algorithmic harm: the self-fulfilling feedback loop. PredPol, Palantir and similar systems work by identifying patterns in historical arrest or incident data and generating predictions about where crime is likely to occur next. Police are directed to those locations and make arrests there. Those arrests become data that confirms the prediction and generates further direction to the same locations.

The feedback loop is particularly problematic because historical arrest data does not measure crime; it measures where police have previously chosen to make arrests. If police have historically patrolled Black and Latino neighborhoods more intensively than white neighborhoods for reasons unrelated to underlying crime rates, the historical data will reflect that enforcement pattern. A model trained on that data will direct police back to those neighborhoods, generating more arrests there, producing data that further confirms the predicted hotspot and amplifying the original enforcement disparity through mechanical iteration. The model is not discovering where crime is; it is recapitulating where police have historically directed enforcement.

A 2019 study by William Isaac, Otieno Obeng and Cathy O'Neil demonstrated this feedback loop quantitatively using data from Oakland, California. They showed that PredPol-directed enforcement in Oakland would direct police disproportionately to low-income and minority neighborhoods even if the underlying crime rate were identical across all neighborhoods, simply because the historical arrest data from which the model learned was already skewed. The study contributed to Oakland's eventual decision to discontinue using PredPol in 2020, joining Santa Cruz, Los Angeles and several other jurisdictions that had discontinued similar systems after similar analyses.

Automated Benefits and the Welfare Trap

The deployment of automated decision systems in public benefits administration has produced some of the most direct and documented harms. The state of Arkansas deployed an algorithm developed by a private contractor called InterRAI to determine home care hours for Medicaid patients with disabilities. When the system launched in 2016, thousands of patients had their hours dramatically cut with no explanation beyond a form letter. People who had been receiving thirty hours per week of in-home assistance, needed to dress, bathe, eat and manage medications, suddenly received twelve. Some had their benefits cut to zero.

Legal advocacy organization Arkansas Disability Rights sued. In discovery they obtained the algorithm's logic, which turned out to contain calculation errors: it was subtracting care hours for diabetic patients when it should have been adding them. A judge ordered the state to restore benefits and provide meaningful explanations for care determinations. The case, Ledgerwood v. Navistar, became a landmark in algorithmic accountability litigation and demonstrated both the potential for automated systems to cause direct physical harm and the importance of transparency and appeal mechanisms in any benefits determination system.

The Netherlands provides a cautionary tale at national scale. The Dutch tax authority (Belastingdienst) operated a fraud detection algorithm for years that flagged applicants for childcare benefits as potentially fraudulent based in part on nationality, specifically targeting people with dual citizenship. The algorithm wrongly identified approximately 26,000 families as committing fraud and demanded repayment of benefits, in some cases amounting to tens of thousands of euros, from families who had done nothing wrong. Many of those targeted were disproportionately from immigrant communities. Families lost homes. Marriages broke down. Children were placed in foster care because parents suddenly could not afford to care for them. The scandal, which came to be known as the "childcare benefit affair," brought down the Dutch government in 2021 and resulted in a parliamentary inquiry that concluded the system had systematically discriminated based on ethnicity and that the human oversight mechanisms that should have caught the errors had failed entirely.

Hiring Algorithms and the Invisible Gatekeepers

Automated screening of job applications is now nearly universal among large employers. The vast majority of Fortune 500 companies use Applicant Tracking Systems that screen resumes before any human sees them. Some systems go further: video interview platforms like HireVue use machine learning to analyze facial expressions, vocal patterns and word choice to produce candidate assessments. Amazon famously developed and then abandoned a hiring algorithm that had been trained on a decade of its own hiring decisions, which predominantly favored men, and had learned to penalize resumes containing words like "women's" as in "women's chess club" and to discount graduates of all-female colleges.

The Amazon case illustrates the central problem with training hiring algorithms on historical decisions: the historical decisions embedded whatever biases existed in the human process. If an organization has systematically hired fewer women, people of color or people with non-traditional educational backgrounds than qualified candidates from those groups warranted, a model trained to replicate those decisions will perpetuate and potentially amplify those biases at scale. The process is described as "objective" because it is automated, but objectivity requires that the data used to train the system accurately represent merit rather than the intersection of merit and historical discrimination.

The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has begun attempting to apply existing anti-discrimination law to algorithmic hiring, issuing guidance in 2023 that Title VII and the Americans with Disabilities Act apply to automated employment tools that produce disparate impact or treat applicants with disabilities differently. But enforcement capacity is thin, most algorithmic hiring decisions are invisible to applicants and the legal framework for challenging adverse algorithmic employment decisions remains underdeveloped. Illinois passed a law in 2020 requiring companies using AI video interview tools to notify applicants and obtain consent, but it does not require disclosure of how the systems work or mandate access to the analysis of individual interviews.

Credit Scoring and Financial Exclusion

Credit scoring is the oldest and most thoroughly institutionalized form of algorithmic decision-making affecting individuals. The FICO score, first introduced commercially in 1989, is now used by the vast majority of US lenders to make or inform credit decisions. More than 90 percent of top lenders use FICO scores. Approximately 45 million Americans have no credit score because they lack sufficient credit history to generate one, a group that is disproportionately young, low-income, Black and Latino. Being unscored is effectively the same as having a poor score for access to credit, creating a bootstrapping problem: you cannot build credit history without access to credit, but you cannot access credit without credit history.

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act and the Fair Housing Act prohibit credit decisions based on race, sex, national origin and other protected characteristics. But FICO and alternative credit scoring models routinely incorporate factors like zip code, employment type and bill payment patterns that are highly correlated with race and income, producing racially disparate credit access through facially neutral means. Research by economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia found that mortgage applicants in majority-minority neighborhoods received less favorable terms even after controlling for credit scores and loan characteristics, suggesting that scoring systems are not capturing all the discrimination embedded in lending decisions.

Alternative data credit scoring, which uses non-traditional data including utility payments, rent, streaming service subscriptions and even social media activity, has been promoted as a way to expand credit access to the unscored population. Some alternative data does improve access for thin-file applicants. But incorporating social media activity and behavioral data into credit decisions raises serious concerns about surveillance and about the use of data collected for one purpose to make decisions with major financial consequences. China's Social Credit System, which aggregates behavioral data across multiple domains to produce a score affecting access to everything from loans to flights, is widely regarded in the West as an authoritarian surveillance tool; the extent to which American fintech credit scoring is moving in an analogous direction rarely receives the same critical attention.

The Regulatory Landscape

The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation, which took effect in 2018, includes a provision often described as a "right to explanation" for automated decisions. Article 22 gives individuals the right not to be subject to solely automated decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects, and Article 13 requires organizations to provide "meaningful information about the logic involved" in automated processing. In practice, EU courts and regulators have interpreted these provisions cautiously, and most explanations companies provide under GDPR are technically compliant but practically uninformative: "Your score was based on your financial history" tells an individual almost nothing useful about why they were denied a loan or how to improve their situation.

The EU AI Act, which entered into force in 2024 after years of negotiation, represents the most ambitious attempt yet to regulate high-stakes automated systems. It classifies AI applications into risk tiers. "High-risk" applications, including systems used in criminal justice, employment, credit, education and critical infrastructure, face mandatory conformity assessments, risk management requirements, transparency obligations and registration in a public database. Prohibited applications include social scoring systems, real-time biometric identification in public spaces with narrow exceptions, and systems that exploit psychological vulnerabilities. The Act's effectiveness will depend heavily on enforcement, which is delegated to national authorities with varying capacity and willingness to challenge established industries.

The United States lacks a comparable federal framework. Existing laws, including the Fair Credit Reporting Act, Equal Credit Opportunity Act, Fair Housing Act and Title VII, provide some protection against discriminatory algorithmic decisions but were not designed for the current environment and leave vast swaths of automated decision-making unregulated. The FTC has used its authority over unfair and deceptive practices to bring some algorithmic accountability cases. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has issued guidance on adverse action notices for credit algorithms. Several states, including California, Colorado and New York City, have passed more targeted laws requiring audits of automated employment tools or restricting specific applications. But the federal legislative landscape remains fragmented, and the United States is substantially behind the EU in establishing clear accountability rules for high-stakes automated systems.

Toward Meaningful Accountability

What would meaningful algorithmic accountability look like? Researchers and advocates across law, computer science and policy have converged on several core elements, though significant disagreement remains about implementation details and relative priority.

Transparency requirements need to be calibrated to the stakes involved. Low-stakes automated decisions may not require full technical disclosure. High-stakes decisions affecting liberty, housing, employment, credit, healthcare and benefits should require that the people affected be told that an automated system was used, given a meaningful explanation of the factors that drove the decision, and provided a genuine opportunity to contest the decision through a human review process. The GDPR model points in the right direction but needs stronger enforcement and more substantive explanation standards.

Mandatory algorithmic impact assessments, analogous to environmental impact assessments for major infrastructure projects, would require developers and deployers of high-stakes systems to systematically evaluate potential harms, including disparate impacts on protected groups, before deployment. Several proposals along these lines have been introduced in Congress without passing, and the EU AI Act incorporates elements of this approach for high-risk systems.

Independent auditing by qualified third parties, with access to system architecture, training data and testing results, would provide a check on self-reported assessments. The challenge is technical: meaningful auditing of complex machine learning systems requires expertise that few regulatory bodies currently possess, and the audit market is still developing standards and methodologies. But the principle, that systems making consequential decisions should be independently evaluated for accuracy, fairness and adherence to stated design goals, is both defensible and achievable.

Perhaps most fundamentally, the shift toward automated decision-making has happened faster than democratic deliberation about acceptable uses, permissible inputs and required accountability mechanisms. The choices embedded in these systems are not technical choices; they are choices about whose errors are more tolerable, which values should govern the distribution of opportunity and risk, and what relationship between individuals and institutions is acceptable in a democratic society. Those choices deserve the kind of public scrutiny and democratic authorization that they are currently bypassing because they are being made by engineers in conference rooms rather than legislators in public debate.
