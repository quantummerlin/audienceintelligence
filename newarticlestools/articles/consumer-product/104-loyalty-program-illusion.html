The Loyalty Program Illusion: Why Your Points Are Worth Less Than You Think

36,529 upvotes · by u/points_accounting · 13 min read
▲ 36,529 people found this relatable
Advertisement

Somewhere in your email, wallet or phone there is almost certainly a collection of loyalty currencies you have not fully redeemed. Airline miles from a flight two years ago, hotel points from a conference stay, credit card rewards sitting in a portal you opened once and found confusing, a coffee shop punch card with eight of the required ten stamps. The aggregate value of all unredeemed loyalty points in the United States has been estimated at over $48 billion. That number is not a measure of consumer wealth sitting in waiting. It is largely a measure of how effectively loyalty programs have created the psychological experience of accumulating value while engineering conditions that prevent, delay and erode the actual redemption of that value.

Loyalty programs are among the most successful financial engineering products in the history of consumer marketing. They generate revenue, reduce price sensitivity, create switching costs, extract behavioral data and build a form of emotional attachment to brands that traditional advertising struggles to produce. They accomplish this while maintaining the appearance of giving customers something for free. The most sophisticated programs have become so central to their companies' financial structures that they dwarf the core business in terms of balance sheet complexity and, in some cases, actual profitability.

Understanding loyalty programs requires looking at them not as customer appreciation tools, which is how they present themselves, but as liability management instruments with a psychological manipulation layer on top. The design choices embedded in every program, from how points are earned and valued to how they expire and how redemption is structured, are not arbitrary. They reflect decades of behavioral research and financial optimization aimed at a single goal: maximizing the gap between what customers think they are earning and what they actually extract from the program.

The Origin Story: Green Stamps and Behavioral Lock-In

Loyalty programs in their modern form trace to S&H Green Stamps, introduced in 1896 and reaching their peak in the 1960s when the S&H company was printing three times as many stamps as the US Postal Service. Retailers purchased the stamps from S&H and distributed them with purchases; consumers collected them in booklets and redeemed them for merchandise from an S&H catalog. At peak penetration, around 80 percent of American households were saving Green Stamps. The program worked because it created a tangible, visible accumulation of value that motivated return visits and discouraged shopping at competitors who did not distribute the stamps.

American Airlines launched the first modern frequent flyer program in 1981, the AAdvantage program, initially conceived as a promotion to fill empty seats with regular customers rather than discounting fares. The program distributed miles for flights and allowed redemption for free travel. The response was immediate and overwhelming: customers began routing their travel specifically to maximize miles, booking connections and layovers they would not otherwise have chosen and developing genuine emotional attachment to the program that translated into brand loyalty at levels no advertising campaign had achieved.

What the airlines discovered was that the behavioral effects of a loyalty currency extended far beyond rational expected-value calculations. People made economically irrational decisions to protect their mileage status. They spent money specifically to reach tier thresholds that required significant additional spending to justify. They felt genuine loss when points expired and genuine satisfaction from accumulation that had no relationship to whether they were likely to redeem those points in a useful way. The behavioral psychology of loyalty currencies proved to be extraordinarily powerful, and every major consumer-facing industry moved to replicate it.

"I booked a $400 flight with a three-hour layover instead of a $280 direct flight because the connection was on 'my' airline and I was close to Gold status. I did the math later. The status upgrade was worth maybe $60 in actual benefits annually. I spent $120 extra to chase it." r/personalfinance · 49,283 upvotes

The Liability Accounting Problem

From a corporate finance perspective, unredeemed loyalty points are a liability. When an airline sells a mile to a credit card company, it records the revenue. The obligation to provide a free seat when that mile is eventually redeemed is a future cost that must be accounted for. How companies value that future obligation is one of the most consequential and least discussed aspects of loyalty program economics.

Airlines, hotels and other loyalty program operators use an accounting approach called "deferred revenue" to track unredeemed point obligations. The critical variable is the assumed redemption rate, which determines what value the company assigns to outstanding points. If a company assumes that 20 percent of points will never be redeemed, it can reduce its liability accordingly. That reduction flows through to the income statement as revenue, a practice called "breakage." Breakage is the accounting recognition of value that customers earned but will never actually receive.

Breakage rates in loyalty programs are substantial. Industry estimates suggest that somewhere between 20 and 30 percent of all loyalty points issued are never redeemed. For some categories, the rates are higher. Gift cards, which function similarly as prepaid loyalty instruments, have estimated breakage rates of 10 to 19 percent depending on denomination and issuer. The financial materiality of breakage is significant enough that program design choices specifically intended to maximize it have a direct, measurable impact on earnings.

The most powerful breakage mechanism is expiration. When points expire unused, the company recognizes the value as revenue without delivering any product or service. Expiration policies vary widely across programs, from airlines that expire miles after 18 to 24 months of account inactivity to hotel programs that expire points after 12 months without either earning or burning. The policies are structured to catch the customer who earns points infrequently enough that natural expiration becomes likely, which tends to be precisely the less-engaged customer who believes they are accumulating value for a future trip they will eventually take.

"Saved up hotel points for five years for a dream trip. Checked my balance six months before we were planning to go and found out they'd expired eighteen months earlier. I had no idea there was even an expiration policy. That's not a loyalty program. That's a savings account they can just seize." r/travel · 71,847 upvotes

The Devaluation Mechanism

Points expiration represents the crude end of value reduction. The more sophisticated mechanism is devaluation: changing the redemption rates so that the same number of points buys less than it previously did. Airlines and hotels have devalued their loyalty currencies repeatedly over the past two decades, with each devaluation announced with minimal notice and framed in language designed to minimize the appearance of loss.

The structural problem is that loyalty points are issued in nominal amounts (10,000 miles, 50,000 hotel points) but redeemed for real goods and services whose costs change over time. Program operators have complete unilateral control over the conversion rate between their points and those real goods. There is no regulatory requirement for advance notice of devaluation, no contractual obligation to maintain current redemption values and no mechanism by which customers can compel a company to honor the implied value at which points were originally issued.

American Airlines' AAdvantage program moved from a fixed award chart (a specific number of miles for a specific route) to dynamic pricing in 2023, following Delta's similar move in 2022. The shift from fixed award charts to dynamic pricing is significant because it eliminates the ability to plan redemptions around known rates. Under dynamic pricing, the same flight that costs 25,000 miles when demand is low might cost 75,000 miles when demand is high. The "best" redemptions require vigilance, flexibility and time investment that not all customers can or will provide. Dynamic pricing effectively transfers the most favorable redemption opportunities to the most sophisticated and time-rich customers while the majority of program members redeem at rates that approximate or worsen on the historic fixed rates.

The cumulative effect of devaluations over a period of years is significant. A study by The Points Guy, a travel rewards publication, estimated that Delta SkyMiles lost approximately 60 percent of their purchasing power for economy redemptions between 2011 and 2023. A mile that could buy one cent's worth of airfare in 2011 bought approximately 0.4 cents worth in 2023. Customers who accumulated miles over this period experienced a slow, steady erosion of real value that was never announced as a single event large enough to provoke outrage, but which accumulated into a substantial transfer of value from program members to the company.

The Credit Card Rewards Complication

The credit card rewards ecosystem adds a layer to loyalty program economics that most consumers miss. Credit card rewards, whether miles, hotel points or cash back, are not funded by the card issuer out of benevolence. They are funded primarily by interchange fees: the percentage of each transaction (typically 1.5 to 3.5 percent) that flows from merchant to card network to issuing bank. Premium rewards cards carry higher interchange rates than basic cards, which is why merchants collectively lose billions of dollars annually to the payment card system.

The distributional consequences of this structure are not often discussed. Interchange fees are a hidden tax on every consumer transaction, including cash and debit transactions, because merchants price their goods to recover payment processing costs across their entire customer base. Cash-paying customers, who tend to be lower income, pay the same prices as credit card users but receive none of the rewards. The rewards flow disproportionately to higher-income customers who qualify for premium rewards cards and who use credit as a cash-flow management tool rather than as a credit instrument. A 2010 paper by economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston estimated that cash-using households transfer approximately $149 annually to card-using households through this mechanism, with lower-income households disproportionately in the cash category.

The credit card rewards game has become increasingly complex, with different cards offering different rewards rates for different spending categories, sign-up bonuses requiring minimum spending in short windows and an entire secondary ecosystem of "points optimization" publishing that treats miles and points as an asset class requiring professional management. The optimization opportunity is real for customers who invest significant time in it. For the majority who do not, the rewards they earn are typically worth less than the behavioral effects of using a rewards card, including increased spending at the point of purchase, reduced price sensitivity and occasional high-interest debt if balances are not paid in full.

"I used to be obsessed with credit card points. Spreadsheets, transfer partners, sweet spots, the whole thing. One day I calculated how many hours I'd spent on it and what my hourly 'return' was. It was embarrassing. I would have been better off with a 2% cash back card and zero time investment." r/churning · 43,719 upvotes

The Status Tier Psychological Trap

Beyond points accumulation, the most psychologically sophisticated element of loyalty programs is the tiered status system. Airline elite status (Silver, Gold, Platinum, Executive Platinum and their equivalents across carriers) and hotel elite status create a hierarchy of benefits that function as social currency as well as practical advantages. Upgrades, priority boarding, lounge access and dedicated customer service lines are real benefits. But the status tier system also creates a set of psychological dynamics that drive economically irrational spending in ways that pure point accumulation does not.

Status tiers trigger loss aversion in ways that simple accumulation does not. Once a customer achieves a tier, the prospect of losing it at year-end is psychologically weighted more heavily than the prospect of not having achieved it would have been. Research on loss aversion, pioneered by Kahneman and Tversky, consistently finds that losses are psychologically weighted roughly twice as heavily as equivalent gains. A frequent flyer who has been Platinum for three years will spend considerably more to retain Platinum than a new flyer would spend to first achieve it, because the existing Platinum holder is avoiding a loss while the new flyer is pursuing a gain.

The threshold effect intensifies this dynamic. Airlines and hotels structure their qualifying requirements so that customers sitting just below a tier threshold at year-end face a specific, visible gap. The customer who needs 5,000 more miles by December 31 to retain Gold status receives targeted communications about their threshold and faces a concrete, time-limited decision. The mileage run, in which a traveler takes a flight purely to accumulate qualifying miles with no interest in the destination, is the most extreme manifestation of this dynamic. Thousands of people annually take roundtrip flights to nowhere specifically to cross status thresholds they would not otherwise reach, paying real money for a purely accounting artifact.

The retail analog to tier status, the tiered rewards card at coffee shops and clothing retailers, operates on the same psychological logic at smaller scale. The "punch card" progression toward a free item creates sunk-cost effects: having invested in earning stamps makes quitting the program feel wasteful, so customers return to a specific vendor they might otherwise substitute rather than lose the invested progress. Research has found that customers spend more per transaction and return more frequently as they approach a reward threshold, a phenomenon sometimes called the "goal gradient effect," with behavior that the reward structure specifically engineers.

Data as the Hidden Currency

The explicit financial proposition of loyalty programs, earn points for purchases, represents only part of their value to companies. The behavioral data generated by loyalty program membership has become an increasingly central component of the value proposition for program operators.

A loyalty program that links purchases across time, location and category provides a detailed profile of individual consumer behavior that is extraordinarily valuable for targeted marketing and pricing. Airlines know not just that you fly frequently but which routes, at what price points, booked how far in advance, with what seat preferences and with what ancillary purchase patterns. Hotel chains know not just that you stay frequently but what room types you prefer, what amenities you use, how much you spend at the bar and restaurant and what events correlate with your bookings. Grocery loyalty programs, which a large majority of American households participate in, track every purchase in granular detail over years, generating data that is sold to consumer goods companies and used to optimize pricing in ways that are not visible to shoppers.

A practice that emerged clearly from investigative reporting by the Markup and others is differential pricing within loyalty programs. Several grocery chains have been documented offering sale prices that are available only to loyalty card members at the register, effectively making the nominal "full price" a theoretical construct that nobody without a card pays and using the loyalty card requirement as a mechanism to collect purchasing data in exchange for standard market pricing. The framing is that loyalty members receive a "discount." The economic reality is that non-members pay a premium, and the discount is the data collection fee.

The Programs That Are Actually Worth Participating In

The critique above should not be read as an argument against all loyalty program participation. Some programs genuinely return value to members in ways that exceed the behavioral costs. The analysis requires distinguishing programs where participation represents a genuine financial positive from programs where the psychological design extracts more value from members than the nominal benefits provide.

Cash back credit cards represent the cleanest form of loyalty program benefit because the value is in a transparent, non-depreciating currency. A 2 percent cash back card on all purchases delivers a straightforward, predictable return with no redemption complexity, no expiration risk and no devaluation mechanism. The value cannot be eroded through award chart changes because it is not denominated in a proprietary currency. For most consumers who pay their balances in full and do not invest significant time in optimization, cash back cards dominate points-based travel cards on a risk-adjusted return basis.

Airline and hotel programs remain worth participating in for one specific reason: the signup bonus. Credit card welcome offers for premium travel cards frequently offer 50,000 to 100,000 points after meeting a minimum spend requirement. At even conservative valuations, these bonuses represent $500 to $1,500 in travel value. The signup bonus typically exceeds the ongoing value of the card by a significant margin, which is why the most financially sophisticated approach to travel rewards is to collect signup bonuses and cancel or downgrade cards before annual fees recur, a practice known as "churning." It is financially rational but requires disciplined credit management.

Simple grocery, coffee and retail programs that offer straightforward discounts without elaborate tier structures tend to be worth participating in simply because the incremental behavioral cost is minimal and the discount is immediate. A grocery store loyalty card that provides access to sale prices costs nothing beyond the card scan and privacy consideration. A coffee shop app that offers a free drink after ten purchases rewards a behavior you would have engaged in anyway. These programs are not manipulating behavior so much as rewarding existing patterns, which is a fundamentally different and more defensible proposition than the elaborate tier-and-expiry structures of the travel industry.

The underlying principle for evaluating any loyalty program is to ask who is being optimized for. Programs designed to be simple to understand, with points that do not expire easily, with transparent redemption values and with a history of treating accumulated points as genuine obligations rather than liabilities to be minimized are programs worth participating in. Programs with complex tier structures, routine devaluations, opaque dynamic pricing and expiration policies calibrated to catch infrequent customers are programs whose design reflects optimization for company interests at the expense of member interests. The enjoyment of accumulation feels real in both cases. The financial outcomes are not equivalent, and learning to read the difference before committing to a program is worth the time it takes.
