Why Products Are Getting Worse: The Engineered Obsolescence Economy

▲ 51,384 people found this relatable

Your grandmother's Kitchen Aid stand mixer still works. The one she bought in 1972 sits on the same counter it always has, and it will probably outlast whoever inherits it. The one you bought four years ago has already had a gear replaced, and you are quietly wondering whether the repair is worth it or whether you should just buy another. The vacuum cleaner your parents owned for twenty years. The jeans that faded rather than fraying. The television set that a repairman could fix rather than one that a technician deems "not cost-effective to service." This is not nostalgia distorting your memory. It is a documented pattern, and it has a name, a history and an economic logic that benefits almost everyone in the production chain except the person who actually buys the thing.

Product quality has declined across a remarkable range of categories over the past several decades, in ways that are measurable, documented and the result of deliberate design choices rather than inevitable trade-offs. The phenomenon operates through several distinct but reinforcing mechanisms: planned obsolescence, where products are designed to fail within a specific timeframe; cheapening, where materials and components are substituted to reduce costs in ways customers cannot easily detect at point of purchase; financial engineering, where the acquisition of formerly quality brands is followed by systematic reduction of the quality that made those brands valuable; and software-enabled degradation, a newer phenomenon in which functional products are intentionally made worse through firmware updates to protect revenue streams.

Together these mechanisms constitute an economy built not on producing things that last but on producing things that need to be replaced. The customer who never needs to replace a product is, from a manufacturer's perspective, a lost sale. The customer who replaces a product every three to four years instead of every ten to fifteen is worth three to five times as much over a product lifetime. The business case for building things that last is weak when building things that fail can be made profitable enough.

The Phoebus Cartel and the Invention of Planned Obsolescence

The concept of planned obsolescence was not a post-war invention or a Silicon Valley innovation. It was formalized, with remarkable explicitness, in 1924 through the Phoebus cartel, a secret agreement among the world's major light bulb manufacturers including General Electric, Philips, Osram and associated companies across Europe, Japan and elsewhere. The cartel existed to coordinate pricing and market allocation, but its most consequential decision was a technical one: member companies agreed to limit the lifespan of incandescent light bulbs to approximately 1,000 hours.

This is a remarkable historical fact. Before the cartel agreement, bulbs routinely lasted 2,500 hours or longer. The Shelby Electric Company had produced a bulb in 1901 that was still burning in Livermore, California's Fire Station 6 over a century later (it remains operational today, making it the longest-burning light bulb in history). The cartel's engineering teams did not shorten bulb lifespan because they could not do better. They shortened it because they could, and because shorter lifespans meant more frequent purchases, which meant more revenue. They even imposed fines on member companies whose bulbs tested above the 1,000-hour limit.

The Phoebus cartel operated openly enough that its documents were eventually discovered and published by historians. The cartel was later dissolved under antitrust pressure, but the mindset it institutionalized, that product lifespan is an engineering variable to be optimized for business outcomes rather than user value, had already propagated throughout manufacturing culture. The industrial designer Brooks Stevens popularized the term "planned obsolescence" in a 1954 speech defining it as "instilling in the buyer the desire to own something a little newer, a little better, a little sooner than is necessary." The definition is somewhat euphemistic. The operational reality is often less about desire and more about engineering degradation into the product itself.

"I collect vintage appliances as a hobby. I've tested dozens of motors from appliances made in the 1950s and 60s. The specifications they were built to -- the tolerances, the materials, the thermal ratings -- are simply better than equivalent modern products at the same inflation-adjusted price point. It's not even close." r/appliancerepair · 44,291 upvotes

The Mechanics of Modern Cheapening

Contemporary quality reduction is less often the result of explicit design mandates than of a slow, iterative process of cost pressure that operates through supply chains over years and decades. The mechanism works roughly as follows: a product line generates margins that a company's financial leadership considers acceptable. Pressure to improve margins leads procurement teams to identify cost reduction opportunities in components and materials. Engineers are tasked with finding substitutions that maintain the product's apparent quality while reducing its cost. Those substitutions are implemented, margins improve and the process repeats at the next earnings review.

The substitutions are rarely visible to consumers at the point of purchase and often not apparent until the product fails. Zinc die-cast components replace steel ones in appliance gears. Engineered wood (MDF, particleboard) replaces solid wood in furniture while retaining the wood veneer surface. Thinner gauge steel goes into kitchen appliances, making them lighter and cheaper to ship. Plastic components replace metal ones in power tools, appearing identical until the stress of use reveals the difference. Synthetic fibers are blended into fabrics at increasing percentages. Spring counts and padding density in mattresses decrease. Thread counts in bedding are calculated using methods that count individual threads within plied yarns, inflating the number relative to historical standards.

In the food industry, the equivalent practice is called "shrinkflation," which has received considerable public attention: packages that remain the same size and price while containing less product. But shrinkflation is merely the visible surface of a broader ingredient substitution practice. Recipe reformulation replaces more expensive ingredients with cheaper alternatives while maintaining the product's visual and nominal identity. Chocolate products substitute cocoa butter with palm oil and other vegetable fats. Ice cream formulated to include less cream and more air by volume. Canned goods use smaller cuts of lower-grade product while maintaining the labeling conventions that signal premium quality. These substitutions are legal, rarely disclosed in marketing and often invisible until a consumer compares an older and newer version of the same product side by side.

"I've been making the same recipe with Campbell's chicken broth for fifteen years. Three years ago it started tasting different. I compared the old and new nutrition labels. Sodium way up, protein down. They'd changed something fundamental in the formulation. The label says 'same great taste' on the carton." r/Cooking · 38,517 upvotes

Financialization and the Brand Acquisition Squeeze

A distinct but related mechanism operates through private equity and corporate acquisition of formerly quality-focused brands. The pattern is consistent enough that industry observers have identified it as a reliable template: an investment firm or conglomerate acquires a brand that has spent decades building a reputation for quality, extracts that reputation by cutting the manufacturing costs that produced it, harvests the margin improvement over a period of years and either sells the brand (at a lower reputation value that the acquirer does not intend to own long enough to absorb) or allows the reputation to erode while the brand continues to trade on its historical name recognition.

Stanley tools, once synonymous with professional-grade hand tools, was acquired by Black & Decker in 2002 and merged with it into Stanley Black & Decker. The subsequent decades saw the manufacturing of many Stanley-branded tools move offshore, with quality reductions documented extensively by professional tradespeople and repair communities. The brand name remained; the manufacturing philosophy that built the name was gradually replaced by the cost optimization that characterized large-scale consumer product manufacturing.

The same pattern recurred with Craftsman, Sears' legendary tool brand, sold to Stanley Black & Decker in 2017 following Sears' bankruptcy. With Sunbeam and Oster appliances after their acquisition by Jarden Corporation. With Maytag after its acquisition by Whirlpool in 2006, which Consumer Reports subsequently documented with years of reliability data showing meaningful degradation from pre-acquisition performance. With Pyrex, whose US licensing was transferred to World Kitchen in 1998; the original soda-lime tempered glass formulation that made Pyrex famous was replaced by borosilicate glass in European markets while the US product retained the Pyrex name with the new formulation, resulting in widely documented breakage incidents that the original formula had not produced.

The financial logic is straightforward. A brand that has spent decades building a quality reputation has accumulated goodwill that takes time to erode. Consumers who trusted the brand will continue to trust it, initially, even as the products beneath the brand degrade. That trust is a form of capital that can be liquidated by reducing manufacturing quality faster than reputation erodes. The question for any acquirer is not whether to liquidate the brand's quality reputation but how quickly to do so relative to the intended holding period.

Software Degradation and the Subscription Conversion

The newest and in some respects most disturbing form of product quality reduction is the deliberate software degradation of functional products. Unlike physical cheapening, which typically occurs at manufacturing and is permanent, software degradation can be applied to products already in customers' hands and can be reversed or adjusted based on business conditions. It represents a fundamentally new type of planned obsolescence that extends the logic of the Phoebus cartel into the digital age with capabilities the cartel could not have imagined.

The Apple iPhone battery throttling controversy, which became public in 2017, provided the most high-profile documented case. Apple acknowledged that iOS updates had included code that throttled processor performance on iPhones with degraded batteries, a practice it framed as preventing unexpected shutdowns. The practical effect was that phones that functioned well became noticeably slower after software updates, creating the perception that the hardware was aging out. Users who did not know about battery replacement as an option (a $79 service) were effectively pushed toward new phone purchases. Apple paid $113 million to settle a multistate attorney general investigation and approximately $500 million in class action settlements, acknowledging that the disclosure around the feature was inadequate.

Printer manufacturers have built a multi-decade ecosystem of software-enabled degradation that remains one of the most blatant examples in any consumer product category. HP, Epson, Canon and Brother have all been documented using firmware updates to enforce ink cartridge restrictions that go beyond their stated purposes. Dynamic security features embedded in printer firmware prevent the use of third-party cartridges by flagging them as incompatible, even when the cartridges are functionally identical. In 2022, HP deployed an update that caused printers to reject third-party ink cartridges that had been working normally in those same printers for months or years, generating widespread consumer complaints and a class action lawsuit alleging that the update was deliberately designed to eliminate competition in the cartridge market.

John Deere has deployed a software access restriction system that prevents farmers from repairing their own equipment using third-party diagnostic tools, requiring authorized dealer service for software-controlled components even when the underlying mechanical problem could be diagnosed and repaired independently. The company has lobbied extensively against right-to-repair legislation, arguing that software access restrictions protect proprietary technology. Critics argue that they function primarily to eliminate competitive repair markets and force warranty service and parts purchases through the company's own channel. The agricultural implications are particularly acute: a farmer whose equipment fails during harvest has very limited leverage to negotiate reasonable service terms with a sole-source repair provider.

"My tractor broke during harvest. The nearest John Deere dealer was two hours away and had a three-day backlog. An independent mechanic could see the exact problem on his own diagnostic equipment but couldn't access the repair codes. We lost four days of harvest to a software lock." r/farming · 89,342 upvotes

The Right to Repair Movement

The right to repair movement emerged as a response to the convergence of physical design choices that make products harder to repair (glued components, proprietary screws, soldered-in batteries, sealed housings) and software restrictions that make repair impossible without manufacturer authorization. The movement has achieved meaningful legislative progress in several jurisdictions while facing sustained opposition from manufacturers whose post-sale service revenue depends on the repair monopoly.

Colorado became the first US state to pass right-to-repair legislation for agricultural equipment in 2023, followed by several other states. A federal executive order in 2021 directed the FTC to address repair restrictions, and the agency subsequently issued a report documenting the consumer and competitive harm from repair monopolies. The EU's right to repair directive, adopted in 2024, requires that manufacturers of specific product categories make spare parts and repair information available for a minimum period after sale and prohibits software locks that prevent third-party repairs.

The resistance from manufacturers has been vigorous and well-resourced. Industry lobbying arguments have cycled through several framings: that repair restrictions protect intellectual property, that third-party repairs compromise safety, that opening repair access enables counterfeiting and that consumers are better served by professional repair than independent attempts. These arguments have found limited legislative traction when tested against empirical evidence, but they have been effective at slowing legislative progress in jurisdictions where the manufacturer lobbying is well-funded relative to the repair-rights advocacy community.

iFixit, the repair information and parts company that has become the most prominent institutional voice in the right-to-repair movement, publishes repairability scores for consumer electronics based on design choices including component accessibility, availability of repair documentation and parts availability. Apple's iPhone has historically scored among the lowest in repairability; the company's shift toward making battery replacement somewhat easier in recent models reflects a combination of right-to-repair regulatory pressure and consumer backlash rather than a change in fundamental philosophy about product longevity.

The Market for Nostalgia and the Quality Premium

The degradation of mainstream product quality has created predictable market responses. Heritage brands, vintage products and products marketed explicitly on durability have found audiences willing to pay significant premiums. The market for vintage denim, driven in large part by the perception that pre-1990s denim construction was genuinely superior to modern equivalents (a perception supported by textile analysis comparing the weight, weave and shrinkage properties of vintage and contemporary denim), sustains a secondary market in which individual pairs of jeans from specific mills and decades sell for hundreds or thousands of dollars.

Companies marketing explicitly on longevity have found loyal audiences. Darn Tough, a Vermont sock company, offers an unconditional lifetime guarantee on socks that are manufactured to specifications that cost more than commodity equivalents. Filson, a Seattle-based outdoor goods company, has maintained a reputation for extreme durability since 1897 and charges prices that reflect that reputation. These companies demonstrate that durable product manufacturing remains technically possible. The constraint is not technological. It is economic: durable products are manufactured and sold at higher margins per unit but lower unit volumes than disposable equivalents, and the business model is viable only in markets where customers are informed enough to value longevity at a premium price.

The broader market failure is that most consumers cannot reliably identify product quality at point of purchase. Marketing budgets allow inferior products to present themselves as quality alternatives. The review systems designed to fill this information gap have, as described elsewhere, been substantially compromised. Consumer testing organizations provide reliable information but are underused relative to more convenient but less reliable sources. The result is a market in which quality signals are systematically corrupted, allowing commodity-quality products to trade at quality-brand prices through marketing investment alone.

What Changed and What Would Have to Change

The causes of quality decline are structural rather than a matter of corporate ethics in the simple sense. Individual engineers, procurement managers and executives operating within the incentive systems of their organizations are responding rationally to those incentives. The incentive systems are the product of financial market expectations (quarterly earnings pressure, short-term return on capital), competitive dynamics (cost reduction at one company becoming a competitive necessity for rivals), consumer behavior patterns (revealed preference for lower prices over durability in point-of-purchase decisions, even when surveys show stated preference for durability) and the information asymmetry that prevents consumers from accurately evaluating quality at purchase.

Addressing those structural causes would require changes that are individually achievable but collectively ambitious. Extended producer responsibility legislation that makes manufacturers financially responsible for product waste creates incentives for durability by internalizingsome of the cost of disposal. The EU's ecodesign regulations, which set minimum repairability and recyclability requirements for product categories, represent this approach in policy form. Right-to-repair legislation removes one mechanism of artificial obsolescence. Mandatory disclosure of expected product lifespans, analogous to energy efficiency labels, would reduce the information asymmetry at point of purchase. Accounting standard changes that extend the period over which product reputation is recognized as a balance sheet asset would reduce the financial attractiveness of brand liquidation strategies.

None of these changes is politically costless. Manufacturer lobbying against each of them has been and will continue to be substantial. The consumer constituency for product durability is diffuse and poorly organized relative to the concentrated industry interests aligned against it. Individual action, buying secondhand where possible, researching repairability before purchase, supporting right-to-repair advocacy and patronizing companies with documented longevity commitments, is meaningful at the margin. But the scale of the quality decline problem reflects structural incentives that individual consumer choices, however well-directed, are insufficient to reverse. The products getting worse is a policy failure as much as a market failure, and it will be fixed, if it is fixed, by regulation as much as by consumer pressure. Your grandmother's mixer is not coming back by itself.
