Homeownership Is Now an Impossible Dream for Most

22,318 upvotes  ·  by u/perpetual_renter  ·  13 min read
▲ 22,318 people found this relatable
Advertisement

For most of the twentieth century, homeownership was the most accessible form of wealth-building available to ordinary working families. You didn't need to understand financial markets. You didn't need to inherit money. You needed a steady income, a modest down payment, acceptable credit and some patience. The bank lent you the rest. You made monthly payments that were roughly comparable to what renting would have cost. Thirty years later you owned an asset worth many times your original investment and had a place to live rent-free in retirement. The mechanism was not complex. It was available, with varying degrees of difficulty, to a very broad range of households.

That mechanism has broken for most people in most desirable housing markets. The income required to qualify for a mortgage on a median-priced home in a major American city now substantially exceeds the median household income in that city. This is not a recent development. It is the end point of a trend that has been building for decades but that accelerated dramatically during the post-pandemic period when a collision of record-low interest rates, supply chain disruptions, pandemic-driven migration patterns and institutional investment in residential property drove median home prices to levels that were, in many markets, simply disconnected from any relationship to local wages.

"My parents bought their house in 1989 for eighty-two thousand dollars. They were both teachers. The mortgage was about twenty-four percent of their combined gross income. The same house, in the same neighborhood, is now listed at six hundred and forty thousand. I am also a teacher, earning roughly what two teachers earned in 1989 adjusted for inflation. The mortgage on that house would be seventy-eight percent of my gross income. I will never own a house in the neighborhood where I grew up. This is not a spending problem." — r/Teachers, 29.4k upvotes

The Down Payment Arithmetic That Doesn't Work

Before the question of whether a mortgage is affordable comes the prior question of whether a down payment is achievable. The standard down payment for a conventional mortgage is twenty percent of the purchase price, both to avoid private mortgage insurance and to access the best available interest rates. On a median-priced home in a major metropolitan area, twenty percent can represent two hundred thousand dollars or more. On a median household income, saving that amount while also paying rent, servicing any existing debt and covering ordinary life expenses is a multi-decade project in the absence of other income sources.

Lower down payment options exist. FHA loans allow down payments as low as three and a half percent. Various first-time buyer programs offer assistance. But lower down payments increase monthly payments, trigger insurance premiums and reduce the equity cushion that makes homeownership economically resilient. They also still require accumulating tens of thousands of dollars in cash savings, a task that is genuinely difficult for the large proportion of working households that are carrying student loan debt, paying high rents, and operating with little or no monthly surplus, all of which describes the circumstances of a substantial proportion of first-time buyers in expensive markets.

The result is that down payment assistance most commonly comes from parental wealth transfers. Federal Reserve data consistently shows that first-time homebuyers with parental help purchase homes earlier, at higher price points and with lower stress than those without it. The bank of mom and dad is not a myth or a media exaggeration. It is the primary mechanism by which many people in expensive markets make the transition from renter to owner in their twenties and early thirties. For people without parents who have accumulated enough wealth to make a meaningful gift, the timeline extends dramatically, if it remains possible at all.

The Interest Rate Trap of the Current Market

The post-pandemic housing market created a particularly painful dynamic through the collision of high prices with rising interest rates. During the period of historically low rates from roughly 2020 to early 2022, home prices in most markets rose rapidly as low financing costs allowed buyers to bid higher. Then, as the Federal Reserve raised rates aggressively to combat inflation, those high prices remained largely sticky while monthly mortgage payments increased dramatically because the same loan amount now carried a much higher interest rate.

A household that could have afforded a monthly payment of two thousand dollars at three percent interest could finance roughly five hundred thousand dollars. At seven percent, the same two-thousand-dollar monthly payment supports only about three hundred thousand dollars in borrowing. The prices didn't drop commensurately, partly because existing homeowners with low locked-in rates were reluctant to sell and give up their rate advantage, which constrained supply further. The practical result was a market in which prices were calibrated to low-rate affordability while actual financing costs reflected high-rate reality. For buyers entering the market in this environment, the gap between what they could afford at the current rate and what the market was asking was a chasm.

"I was pre-approved for a mortgage in early 2022. The amount I was approved for was enough to buy something decent in my market. By mid-2023, the same income and credit profile qualified me for about sixty percent of that amount because rates had roughly doubled. The homes I was looking at hadn't dropped to match my reduced borrowing power. I basically got priced out between one pre-approval and the next with nothing in my own circumstances changing at all." — r/RealEstate, 17.6k upvotes

Institutional Investors and What They Did to the Market

The entry of institutional investors into the single-family home market at scale is a relatively recent development that has received significant media attention and generated significant controversy. Large investment firms, hedge funds and real estate investment trusts began purchasing single-family homes in bulk in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, initially targeting foreclosed properties in distressed markets. The model proved profitable: convert owned homes to rentals, aggregate them into portfolios and generate returns through rent income and long-term appreciation.

The scale of institutional ownership in some markets became significant enough to affect local prices and availability. When a large investor can outbid individual buyers by paying cash and waiving contingencies, ordinary buyers competing for the same homes find themselves at a structural disadvantage. The homes that leave the for-sale market and enter institutional rental portfolios do not become available for individual purchase without the investor choosing to sell, which they have little incentive to do in appreciating markets.

The policy response to institutional home buying has been contentious. Some states and municipalities have moved to restrict or tax institutional ownership of single-family homes. The economic evidence on the actual impact of institutional buying on prices and homeownership rates is mixed, with some studies finding significant effects in specific markets and others finding more modest ones. But the optics and the experience of individual buyers in markets with high institutional activity are consistently negative, and the fundamental question of whether it is desirable public policy for the stock of family homes to migrate into investment portfolios rather than individual ownership is a legitimate one that housing policy has barely begun to address.

The Emotional Weight of Permanent Renting

Beyond the financial stakes of homeownership, there is a cultural and psychological dimension that deserves acknowledgment. In American culture, homeownership is not just an economic decision. It is bound up with notions of stability, adulthood, belonging and generational responsibility. Owning a home means you have somewhere to put down roots, to modify to your tastes, to know your neighbors over decades, to have a place that is genuinely and permanently yours in a way that a lease never quite is.

People who are systematically excluded from this experience by economics they cannot control report a particular kind of grief about it. Not just frustration at the financial barrier, but genuine sadness about the version of adulthood and belonging that homeownership symbolizes and that feels increasingly inaccessible. The couple who has been saving for a decade and still can't close the gap. The person who moves from rental to rental every year when the lease renews at a higher rate. The family that has to change schools when the landlord sells the property. These are not just financial inconveniences. They are disruptions to the kind of stable, rooted life that people overwhelmingly say they want.

"I'm thirty-seven. I've moved nine times in twelve years, always because of rent increases or lease non-renewals, never by choice. I don't have a garden. I can't get a dog because no landlord will allow it. I can't paint my walls. I can't hang art without anxiety about the deposit. I'm a successful professional by most measures. I just live like I'm in my twenties and always will. Not because I want to. Because I can't afford not to." — r/Adulting, 21.7k upvotes

What People Are Actually Doing

Faced with an unaffordable housing market, people are improvising a range of responses, some of which represent genuine innovation and some of which represent capitulation to an unacceptable situation reframed as a lifestyle choice. House hacking, buying a small multi-unit property and living in one unit while renting the others to offset the mortgage, works in markets where such properties are available and where the math supports it, but it requires the down payment capital and landlord skills that not everyone has. Co-buying, where two or more unrelated buyers purchase a property together, is growing as a workaround to the income requirements for individual qualification, but it introduces legal and relational complexity that can be significant.

Geographic arbitrage, moving to lower-cost areas to access homeownership, is perhaps the most common genuine solution, but it requires the labor market conditions to support it. Remote work made this viable for a new category of workers during the pandemic, allowing people to maintain higher salaries while living in lower-cost areas. As return-to-office pressures mount and remote work options narrow, the arbitrage window for many workers is closing again.

The honest conclusion is that the homeownership dream is not equally lost for everyone. It remains accessible in lower-cost markets, particularly in smaller cities and rural areas. It remains accessible to people with parental wealth to draw on. It remains accessible to high earners in almost any market. What it has ceased to be is broadly accessible to working and middle-class households in the cities where working and middle-class jobs are concentrated. That closing of a previously open door is not a natural economic event. It is the consequence of specific policy choices that prioritized asset holders over future buyers, and it can be reopened by different choices. Whether those choices will be made is the central housing policy question of the next generation.
