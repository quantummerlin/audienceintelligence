The City That's Pricing Out Its Own Workers

14,881 upvotes  ·  by u/essential_but_evicted  ·  11 min read
▲ 14,881 people found this relatable
Advertisement

San Francisco has a teacher shortage so severe that the district has recruited from abroad, offered signing bonuses and still struggled to fill classrooms. The starting teacher salary in the district is around sixty thousand dollars a year. The median one-bedroom rent in San Francisco is around three thousand dollars a month. A starting teacher earning sixty thousand, taking home perhaps forty-two thousand after taxes, is paying roughly eighty-six percent of their net income on a single room in the city where they work. This is not a livable situation. It is not a situation in which any rational person would choose to begin a career, and an increasing number of young teachers are choosing not to.

San Francisco is the most extreme version of a pattern playing out in every major American city. The workers whose labor is most essential to a city's daily functioning, the teachers, nurses, firefighters, paramedics, bus drivers, social workers, sanitation workers and restaurant cooks, are being systematically priced out of the cities that depend on them. The market logic is clean and brutal: housing prices are set by what the highest-paid workers can afford, and the gap between those wages and median essential-worker wages has grown so large that essential workers simply cannot compete for housing in the market the city's success has created.

"I'm a nurse. I work in one of the highest-rated hospitals in the country, in one of the most expensive cities in the country. I commute ninety minutes each way because I cannot afford to live anywhere near where I work. I provide essential care to people in a crisis. I drive two and a half hours a day to do it. My patients are often richer than I'll ever be. None of them have any idea where I live or how long it took me to get to their bedside." — r/nursing, 21.3k upvotes

The Essential Worker Paradox

The paradox at the heart of this story is that the workers being priced out are not low-value workers in any meaningful sense. Teachers shape children's development and social mobility. Nurses provide hands-on care that cannot be automated. Firefighters protect the property and lives of the entire city. Transit workers make it possible for millions of people to get to work. Without these workers, the city does not function. With them priced into impossible commutes or out of the city entirely, the city begins to malfunction in ways that affect everyone.

The teacher shortage is perhaps the most visible manifestation of this. Classes without qualified teachers, substitute-heavy classrooms, unfilled specialist positions in music, art and special education, high turnover that prevents the development of sustained relationships between educators and students, all of these outcomes follow from the inability of teacher compensation to keep pace with housing costs in major cities. The students most affected are, predictably, those in lower-income neighborhoods where the schools are already under-resourced and where teacher retention is already most difficult.

Healthcare worker shortages in expensive urban areas show the same dynamics. Hospitals serving low-income urban populations have struggled to recruit and retain staff at every level. The nursing shortage predated the pandemic but was dramatically worsened by it, and the housing cost component of the recruitment problem is documented clearly in exit surveys and union negotiations. Nurses leaving high-cost cities cite housing costs explicitly among their primary reasons, often for hospital systems in lower-cost regions or states where their wage goes much further.

The Commute That Replaces a Life

For essential workers who can't afford to leave their jobs but also can't afford to live near them, the solution is typically a very long commute from a more affordable area. This commute is not neutral. It consumes time that would otherwise go to family, rest, social connection, health maintenance and community participation. A nurse doing two ninety-minute commutes daily is working a twelve-hour shift plus three hours of unpaid transit time. The effective workday is fifteen hours. The effective hourly rate, calculated against total time committed, is dramatically lower than the nominal wage suggests.

Long commutes are also not free. They cost transit fares or fuel, parking, vehicle maintenance and wear. In many areas without adequate public transit, essential workers with long commutes are required to own and operate a car, with all the associated costs, as a condition of employment. The car is not a lifestyle choice for these workers. It is a tool required to access their job, and it costs money that erodes the wage further.

The health consequences of extended commuting are well-documented. Commutes over forty-five minutes each way are associated with elevated stress, worse sleep quality, higher blood pressure, greater rates of anxiety and depression and reduced physical activity. These effects compound over years and are particularly severe for commutes involving congested highway driving. Essential workers enduring these commutes to serve the health and education needs of city residents are themselves having their health quietly damaged by the commute their housing situation requires.

"I teach third grade in a great school in a wealthy neighborhood. My students' parents are doctors and lawyers and executives. I leave my apartment at six fifteen every morning to catch the bus that connects to the train that gets me to school by seven forty-five. I leave school at four thirty and get home by six fifteen. My school day is eight hours. My workday including transit is twelve hours. I'm twenty-eight and I'm already exhausted in a way I don't think I can sustain for thirty years." — r/Teachers, 17.5k upvotes

What Cities Are Doing About It

A growing number of cities and institutions have begun attempting to address workforce housing directly, though the scale of these efforts is almost everywhere dwarfed by the scale of the problem. Teacher housing programs, where school districts or municipalities develop below-market housing specifically for teachers and other public servants, have been piloted in San Francisco, Los Angeles, Boston and other high-cost cities. These programs help, and the teachers who get into them express significant gratitude and improved job satisfaction and retention. They serve a tiny fraction of the workforce that needs housing and require ongoing public subsidy that is politically contested.

Employer-assisted housing programs, where hospitals or other large employers provide housing subsidies or develop affordable housing for their workforce, represent another approach. Several hospital systems in expensive markets have invested in workforce housing development as a talent retention strategy, calculating that the housing investment is cheaper than the continuous cost of recruitment and turnover. This is a reasonable calculation, but it transfers what is fundamentally a public policy problem into the private sector, creating employer-linked housing that may bind workers to specific employers in ways that reduce their bargaining power.

The more scalable solutions involve changing the land use and zoning environment rather than providing subsidized housing unit by unit. Cities that have reformed zoning to allow more diverse housing types across more areas of the city have seen more housing built, and more housing built has generally produced relative price moderation. Minneapolis, which eliminated single-family-only zoning citywide, and Auckland, which reformed zoning to allow up to three units on essentially every residential lot, have seen meaningfully more housing construction and some relative price moderation compared to similar cities that maintained restrictive zoning. Neither city has solved its affordability problem, but both have slowed its deterioration.

The Long-Term Fragility This Creates

Cities that cannot house their essential workers are creating a slow-developing fragility that does not show up on any balance sheet but that represents a genuine long-term threat to urban function. When the teacher pipeline dries up because young people see no viable path to affordable housing in the city they'd like to teach in, school quality deteriorates over years in ways that don't become fully visible until the damage is significant. When nursing staff shortages force hospitals to close units, to reduce elective procedures or to use expensive agency nurses at two to three times the cost of permanent staff, healthcare quality and cost are both affected. When transit worker shortages reduce service frequency, more people drive, congestion increases and the transit system enters a spiral of declining ridership and declining funding that makes it less useful to everyone.

These are not hypothetical futures. They are trends already underway in multiple major American cities, documented in school district reports, hospital staffing studies and transit agency rider surveys. The market that priced out essential workers did so efficiently and legally according to its own logic. The city that results from that pricing-out is more expensive, less functional and less resilient than the city it replaced. The residents who caused the housing appreciation by bidding up prices also live in the fragile city their demand created, and they rely every day on workers who can barely afford to show up.
