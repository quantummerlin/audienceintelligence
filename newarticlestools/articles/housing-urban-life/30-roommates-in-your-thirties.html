Why Having Roommates in Your Thirties Isn't What Anyone Planned

11,628 upvotes  ·  by u/shared_bathroom_blues  ·  10 min read
▲ 11,628 people found this relatable
Advertisement

When you were twenty-two it was obvious and unremarkable. Everyone had roommates. You found them through your college network or a Craigslist posting or a mutual friend, moved into an apartment with too little cabinet space and not enough hot water, split everything down the middle and called it the beginning of adult life. The plan was always to eventually reach the point where you had your own place. A studio, even. Somewhere small but entirely yours, where you controlled the thermostat and the kitchen and the soundtrack and the guests and the hour that the lights went out. That was the adulthood you were moving toward.

For a large and growing number of people in expensive cities, that adulthood never arrived. Or it arrived briefly and then got economically priced back out. At thirty-two you are still explaining to a new roommate that you prefer the bathroom after seven, still negotiating over whether the dishes have to be done the same night or can wait until morning, still negotiating the invisible social contract of shared space that you thought you'd left behind when you got your first real paycheck. Not because you want to be here. Because the alternative, your own one-bedroom in this city, would cost more than you can reasonably pay.

"I've had roommates for thirteen years. I'm thirty-five. I'm not broke, I'm not irresponsible, I have savings and a career. I live with roommates because a one-bedroom in my city costs two thousand seven hundred a month and I'm not willing to pay that when I can pay nine hundred and use the rest for something. Rationally this is the correct choice. Emotionally I am so tired of it I cannot fully articulate it." — r/Adulting, 16.3k upvotes

The Economics That Made This the Default

The arithmetic of roommate living in expensive cities is straightforward and compelling. A two-bedroom apartment in many major markets costs roughly sixty to seventy percent of what a comparable one-bedroom costs, meaning two people sharing a two-bedroom each pay thirty to thirty-five percent of the one-bedroom price. In a city where a one-bedroom runs twenty-five hundred dollars, a shared two-bedroom might cost thirty-two hundred, meaning each roommate pays sixteen hundred. That nine-hundred-dollar monthly difference is eleven thousand dollars a year. Over four years of sharing versus going solo, the difference is forty-four thousand dollars, enough to meaningfully affect savings, debt reduction and the timeline to purchasing a home.

These numbers explain why the roommate market in expensive cities includes not just the fresh-out-of-college cohort but a broad cross-section of working adults at all career stages. The rental listings in San Francisco, New York, Boston and Los Angeles regularly feature rooms in shared apartments sought and occupied by people with professional degrees, established careers and the full markers of adult competence. They are sharing not because they cannot find their own place but because the financial argument for sharing is overwhelming in a housing market this extreme.

Remote work changed the calculus for some people by making cheaper cities accessible, but the return-to-office pressure that intensified through 2023 and 2024 reversed much of that migration, particularly for workers in industries with strong in-person expectations. The people who had briefly escaped the roommate market by moving to a lower-cost city found themselves choosing between the cheaper city and their career trajectory, and many chose their career. Back in the expensive city. Back in the shared apartment.

What Changes When You're Thirty and Not Twenty-Two

The roommate dynamic that was unremarkable at twenty-two is substantially different at thirty-two, and the difference is not just cultural expectation. People change in ways that make shared living genuinely harder as they age. Sleep schedules become more important to health and performance. Social preferences become more specific. Tolerance for other people's habits, the midnight Netflix with the volume up, the passive attitude toward dishes, the partner who effectively moves in without being on the lease, decreases substantially with age and with the exhaustion of a demanding career. The capacity for the endless negotiation of shared space is a depleting resource, and by the time you're in your mid-thirties most people have used much of it up.

Professional identity also complicates roommate dynamics in ways that don't exist at twenty-two. When you need to take a work call, host a professional contact for dinner, get to bed early for an important presentation or work from home in silence, the shared apartment is no longer a neutral backdrop. It is a series of potential conflicts between your professional needs and your roommate's legitimate use of a space they also pay for. The studio apartment, even a mediocre one, eliminates those conflicts completely. The roommate situation preserves them in perpetuity.

"The hardest part isn't the practical stuff. I've managed shared kitchens and bathrooms for a decade. I know how to do it. The hardest part is the feeling of impermanence. I've moved six times in seven years because roommates move on, partnerships end, leases expire. I don't own any furniture that requires more than one person to move. My whole life is structured around portability because I have never had housing stability. It does something to your sense of yourself that's hard to name." — r/malelivingspace, 9.4k upvotes

The Relationship Problem

Romantic relationships and the roommate life interact in ways that range from awkward to genuinely damaging. The person in a serious relationship who has a roommate and whose partner also has a roommate faces a specific logistical problem: neither person has a private space. Every night together requires negotiating the domestic politics of someone else's home. Every intimate conversation risks being heard. Every argument has an audience. The relationship develops in a context of permanent semi-publicity that is not how serious adult relationships are supposed to work.

Partners who move in together partly to solve the roommate problem, renting a shared apartment rather than a room in a shared apartment, sometimes find themselves making a housing-driven relationship escalation that the relationship wasn't quite ready for. The move-in decision that would ideally happen when both people genuinely want to share a life happens instead when the roommate situation becomes untenable and shared housing is the most financially available solution. Some relationships are robust enough to absorb this sequence. Others accelerate into cohabitation and discover incompatibilities that more gradual intimacy would have revealed before a joint lease was signed.

The Roommate Market at Thirty: What It Actually Looks Like

Finding a roommate as a professional adult in your thirties is a specific and slightly absurd social experience. You post on roommate-finding apps alongside twenty-two-year-olds describing themselves as "chill" and "social but also cool with quiet nights in." You write a bio that attempts to convey that you are a functional adult who works and sleeps and does dishes in a reasonable timeframe. You conduct what are essentially job interviews with strangers, each of you presenting your best self and attempting to evaluate whether the other person's habits are compatible with your own in the areas that matter most: cleanliness standards, social volume, guest frequency and kitchen use.

Then you move in and find out what neither of you said in the interview. That they turn the heat up to seventy-eight and you keep it at sixty-eight. That their definition of "clean" is "dishes done within forty-eight hours" and yours is "done before bed." That they work from home and yours is a remote office and there are eight hours a day of shared space that neither of you anticipated. The roommate relationship is fundamentally an arranged partnership between two people who met as near-strangers, and its success depends on a combination of compatible habits, genuine mutual respect and a certain tolerance for the irritations of proximity that erodes with fatigue and stress.

"I've had probably twelve different roommates over the years. Most were fine. Two were great, people I'm still friends with. Two were genuinely difficult situations that affected my mental health for months. The variance is enormous and you can't fully predict it from an interview. I'm now thirty-four and I'm still doing this. The exhaustion is real but so is the math. I'm not going to pay two thousand seven hundred a month to have a space to myself when I can pay eleven hundred and share. Not at this stage of saving for a house. But I am deeply tired." — r/personalfinance, 12.7k upvotes

Making It Work Without Losing Your Mind

The roommate arrangements that work best at this life stage have a few things in common. Explicit communication at the outset about the arrangements that matter most is far more valuable than hoping for alignment to emerge organically. The conversations about cleanliness standards, guest policies, shared food, noise levels after certain hours and bathroom scheduling are uncomfortable to have before you've even signed a lease, but they prevent the slow accumulation of unaddressed friction that destroys more roommate relationships than any single dramatic incident. Most roommate conflicts are not caused by bad people. They are caused by people with different but equally reasonable assumptions who never made those assumptions explicit.

Physical boundaries within shared spaces matter more at thirty than at twenty-two. Having a bedroom that is fully and exclusively yours, organized entirely to your preferences, is not a luxury when you're sharing common spaces. It is the necessary private zone that makes sharing common spaces bearable. Investing in your own room as a genuine refuge, rather than treating it as just a place to sleep, gives you somewhere to decompress that doesn't require negotiating with anyone else. The quality of the private space compensates for the shared nature of the rest.

The most useful reframe of all is the same one that applies to living with parents: treating this as a deliberate financial strategy rather than a failure of adulthood. The person saving aggressively for a down payment while sharing an apartment is not failing to grow up. They are executing a plan. The shared apartment is not the destination. It is the vehicle. Knowing clearly where you're going and why you're in this vehicle makes the journey considerably less demoralizing than treating every shared bathroom morning as evidence that the life you wanted didn't happen.
