How Remote Work Broke the Housing Market in Small Cities

12,193 upvotes  ·  by u/priced_out_of_home  ·  10 min read
▲ 12,193 people found this relatable
Advertisement

Bozeman, Montana was a college town with outdoor recreation access, a pleasant downtown and housing prices that made sense relative to local incomes. A teacher, a nurse, a small business owner or a ski resort employee could realistically afford to live there, maybe not in the biggest house but in something decent, somewhere in or near the community they worked in. Then the pandemic arrived, remote work became normalized and the tech workers from Seattle, San Francisco and New York discovered Bozeman. They arrived with salaries calibrated to coastal cost-of-living and a genuine desire for mountains, space and quality of life. Their purchasing power in the local housing market was extraordinary. Within a few years, Bozeman's housing prices had increased by percentages that matched or exceeded those of the coastal cities those workers were fleeing. The people who lived and worked in Bozeman before that migration were left competing in a market where they were outgunned by incomes many times their own.

Bozeman is the most cited example but it is far from the only one. Boise, Idaho. Spokane, Washington. Asheville, North Carolina. Missoula, Montana. Bend, Oregon. Flagstaff, Arizona. Every city that combined natural amenity, decent infrastructure and a significant gap between local housing prices and those of major coastal metros became a destination for pandemic remote workers engaged in geographic arbitrage. The arbitrage logic was individually rational. Take a high coastal salary, move somewhere cheaper and enjoy a dramatically improved financial position and quality of life. The systemic logic was a disaster for the people already living in those markets.

"I grew up in Boise. My parents own their home there. I moved away for a few years after college and came back in 2022 thinking I'd be able to afford to buy near them. I was wrong. The market that was accessible to a young local adult in 2019 is now a market calibrated to tech workers making twice what most people who actually live here make. I cannot afford to live in the city where I was raised. I'm not sure that can be fixed." — r/Boise, 15.7k upvotes

The Wage Geography Problem

The fundamental dynamic that produced the remote work housing shock is a collision between two geographic realities that had previously been kept separate by the requirement of physical presence at work. High wages in major tech and finance hubs are calibrated to, and partly offset by, the high cost of living in those hubs. Low housing costs in smaller cities are calibrated to, and partly offset by, the lower wages available in smaller city labor markets. These two systems existed in approximate equilibrium for a long time because workers generally had to live where their employers were.

Remote work broke the equilibrium. A worker earning a San Francisco software engineer salary no longer needed to live in San Francisco to do their job. They could move to Boise, where housing cost a fraction of San Francisco's prices, and bank the difference. The move was a spectacular financial upgrade for them individually. It exported their high salary's purchasing power into a housing market that had never been priced for it, instantly making them the most competitive buyer or renter in that market and pushing up prices for everyone else.

The price increases in pandemic-era migration destinations were staggering and rapid. Markets that had seen modest appreciation for years experienced thirty, forty and in some cases fifty percent price increases within two to three years. The compression of that price appreciation into such a short window gave local residents almost no time to adapt. Someone who had been saving for a down payment for five years watching the market patiently found themselves further from purchase in 2022 than they had been in 2019, despite five years of additional saving, because the market had moved so much faster than their savings.

Local Workers Who Could No Longer Afford Local Housing

The workers most affected by the remote work migration were those whose incomes were tied to the local economy rather than to coastal salaries. Teachers, healthcare workers, local government employees, retail and hospitality workers, tradespeople, the entire service and essential worker ecosystem that makes a city function, all of these workers saw housing costs skyrocket without any corresponding increase in their wages. Their wages are set by local labor markets. Their housing costs were being set by national labor markets through the purchasing power of remote workers.

The resulting situation mirrored, in compressed form, the dynamic that had been building over decades in cities like San Francisco and Seattle. Essential workers priced out of the communities they served. Long commutes replacing neighborhood proximity. Teachers unable to afford homes in the school districts where they taught. Nurses commuting from increasingly distant suburbs to hospitals in cities they can't afford to live in. The remote work migration reproduced the essential-worker-displacement crisis of major metros in the smaller cities that had previously been the affordable alternative those workers could retreat to.

"I'm a respiratory therapist. I work at the hospital here. I've lived in this area my whole life. My salary is good for here, not good for the coast. In 2020 I was looking at houses in the two hundred and fifty thousand range. By 2022 everything in that range was gone. The same houses were selling for four hundred thousand with multiple cash offers over asking. I couldn't compete. I'm renting now and my rent has gone up forty percent in three years. The people bidding cash on those houses make three times what I do. They work for tech companies and they're good people. But they broke my housing market." — r/Missoula, 13.1k upvotes

The Return-to-Office Backlash and What It Left Behind

Beginning in 2022 and accelerating through 2023 and 2024, major employers began pushing back against full remote work, requiring employees to return to offices some or all of the time. The tech sector that had led the remote work expansion began pulling back. The geographic arbitrage that had driven migration to smaller cities became less available to workers whose employers now expected physical presence two, three or five days a week in a major metro office.

Some workers who had migrated to smaller cities during the pandemic chose to return to major metros when return-to-office requirements made staying impractical. Some chose to leave their employers rather than give up their new location. Others negotiated hybrid arrangements that allowed them to stay. The net effect on smaller city housing markets was mixed and geographically variable. Some markets that had seen the most dramatic in-migration did see some price moderation as some remote workers returned to coastal cities. But the price increases had been so substantial and had been absorbed so completely into local expectations that even with moderation, prices remained far above their pre-pandemic levels and far above what local wages could support.

The people who arrived during the pandemic and stayed because they genuinely fell in love with their new cities now faced a different version of the local vs. newcomer tension. They had contributed to pricing out local workers. They recognized this, mostly. They were also genuinely invested in their new communities. The ethical complexity of this position, of being both a beneficiary of a process and a participant in a harm caused to others, was not something the remote work discourse handled with much sophistication during the boom years or after.

The Policy Gap That Made It All Worse

The cities that experienced the most severe remote work migration housing shocks were, in most cases, also cities with inadequate housing supply relative to demand. Zoning rules designed for a slow-growth small city did not accommodate rapid population increases. Permitting processes were not built for the volume of applications that arrived. Construction capacity was limited relative to the pace of demand. The collision of sudden demand increase with constrained supply produced the price spikes that were devastating to local affordability.

The cities that held up better tended to be those that had, for various reasons, more permissive zoning and faster permitting that allowed housing supply to respond more quickly to demand. The lesson is the same as the lesson in major metros: supply matters enormously, and jurisdictions that restrict supply will see prices rise dramatically when demand increases for any reason. The remote work migration was a stress test for housing supply adequacy, and most smaller American cities failed it because they had never needed to build at the pace the migration required.

The legacy of the remote work migration to smaller cities is a housing affordability crisis that reproduces, at smaller scale, the dynamics of the major metros that drove workers away in the first place. The places people fled are expensive. The places they fled to are now expensive. The workers who can't earn coastal salaries and can't work remotely are losing access to affordable housing in both categories of city simultaneously. The geographic escape hatch that cheaper smaller cities represented is considerably more narrow than it was five years ago, and for workers whose jobs require local physical presence, it has closed entirely in many markets.
