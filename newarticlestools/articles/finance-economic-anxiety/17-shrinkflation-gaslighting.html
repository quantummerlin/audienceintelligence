Shrinkflation Is Gaslighting You

10,389 upvotes  ·  by u/smaller_every_year  ·  10 min read
▲ 10,389 people found this relatable
Advertisement

The bag of chips is the same price. The packaging looks roughly the same. The brand is the same, the font is the same, the same smiling mascot is on the front promising the same great taste. But there are fewer chips in the bag. This has been true for several years now across thousands of products in supermarkets across every developed economy, and the companies responsible for it have spent enormous resources ensuring that you either don't notice, or when you do notice, that you feel vaguely uncertain whether you're remembering correctly.

That's shrinkflation. And calling it what it actually is matters: it's a deliberate pricing strategy that functions by exploiting the limits of human memory and the discomfort we feel when reality contradicts our expectations. The product got smaller. The price didn't drop proportionally. You're paying more per unit of product than you were before. But because the package still looks roughly the same size, and because your memory of how heavy a 200-gram bag of crackers feels in your hand is imprecise, the company bets that your discomfort will be mild and short-lived and that you'll put the item in your basket anyway.

They've been right. For decades. And the scale of this practice, when you total it across the entire consumer goods market, represents an extraordinary transfer of money from households to corporations through a mechanism specifically designed to avoid triggering the scrutiny that an outright price increase would invite.

"I bought a tub of ice cream I've bought my whole life. Got home, started scooping it, thought something felt off. Checked the label. It had dropped from 1.89 liters to 1.5 liters. Same price, same tub that somehow looked the same size, just a lot of empty space at the top I'd never noticed before. That was the moment I genuinely felt like I was losing my mind. Had it always been like that? Was I misremembering? I wasn't." — r/mildlyinfuriating, 41.2k upvotes

The History of Making Less Look Like the Same

Shrinkflation is not new. Consumer protection advocates and journalists have been documenting it for decades. But the pace and scale of it intensified dramatically in the post-pandemic period as companies faced genuine cost pressures from supply chain disruptions, energy prices and ingredient costs, and then faced a choice: raise prices visibly or reduce contents invisibly. The math on which option would cost them fewer customers was not complicated.

A visible price increase triggers immediate comparison. A shopper reaching for their usual orange juice and finding it costs fifty cents more than last week will notice that immediately. They may decide it's still worth it, but the decision is made consciously and the feeling of cost is vivid. A size reduction does not trigger the same response. The price on the shelf tag is the same or close to it. The package looks roughly similar. The weight difference of thirty grams is not something human hands can reliably detect. The decision to put it in the basket happens on autopilot, the same autopilot that has handled this particular purchase a hundred times before.

The companies are not subtle about why they prefer this approach. Internal documents from several consumer goods companies that have been disclosed in regulatory proceedings or reported by journalists describe package size as a "pricing lever" that can be deployed with less consumer sensitivity than direct price increases. This language, which treats customers as subjects to be managed rather than participants in a fair exchange, captures the relationship precisely. You are not being sold less product. You are being given a smaller portion through a mechanism calibrated to sit just below your threshold of reliable noticing.

The Geometry of Deception

The specific techniques companies use to obscure size reductions have become increasingly sophisticated. The most basic is the concave bottom: press the bottom of a container inward to reduce interior volume while keeping the exterior dimensions identical. Peanut butter jars have used this technique for years. The jar sits on your shelf, looks exactly the same from the outside, but holds eight percent less product than it did before the redesign. Unless you weigh it or are told to look for the indentation, you will not notice.

For bags and flexible packaging, the standard approach is simply reducing the fill while maintaining the outer dimensions of the bag. The snack industry has normalized this so thoroughly that most chip bags are between forty and sixty percent air by volume. This means a company can reduce the amount of product in the bag by ten percent while leaving the bag itself completely unchanged. The bag doesn't get smaller. The air-to-chip ratio just quietly shifts.

Rectangular products like chocolate bars use the "notch" technique: adding an extra groove or ridge to the bar's surface. The bar looks the same from the wrapper. The individual segments still snap satisfyingly. But the recessed channel takes up space that used to be chocolate. A six-segment bar becomes a seven-segment bar where each segment is smaller. The company often markets this as a "redesigned" or "new look" product. The new look that contains less chocolate.

"I work in product development. I can tell you that every major consumer goods company has a dedicated packaging team whose job is finding ways to reduce material costs and content while minimizing consumer awareness of the change. It's not a secret internally. It's just never discussed publicly. The phrase used in meetings is 'value engineering.' The phrase that would be used honestly is 'charging the same for less.'" — r/antiwork, 22.7k upvotes

The Official Numbers Don't Capture It

This is where shrinkflation becomes a genuine policy problem beyond just personal frustration. Inflation is measured through price indices that track the cost of a basket of goods over time. These measures do attempt to account for size changes, through a process called quality adjustment, but they do so imperfectly and with significant lag. When a product shrinks and a new unit price is calculated for the index, that adjustment is made. But the detection of the size change depends on statistical agencies having access to current packaging data, and in a market with thousands of products changing sizes in ways designed to be non-obvious, the coverage is necessarily incomplete.

The practical result is that official inflation figures undercount the actual increase in the cost of living in the specific, concrete sense that households experience it. You are not being paranoid when you feel like your grocery bill keeps going up even when official inflation statistics seem moderate. Some portion of what you're experiencing is shrinkflation that the index hasn't fully captured, combined with the compounding effect of multiple products simultaneously getting smaller in ways that only become apparent when the bill is tallied at the end of the month.

Several central banks and consumer protection agencies have acknowledged this gap between measured and experienced inflation, particularly for lower-income households who spend a higher proportion of income on food and household goods, the categories most heavily affected by shrinkflation. The acknowledgment is usually accompanied by assurances that measurement methodologies will be improved. The grocery bills continue regardless.

Why Your Memory Is Being Exploited

Human memory is not built for exact quantity recall. We remember shapes, colors, brand identities and very rough weight-in-hand estimates. We do not reliably remember that a specific product weighed 450 grams versus 400 grams. We remember whether it felt roughly heavy or roughly light, and we remember whether it felt similar to previous purchases. Companies design their size reductions to stay within the margin of this imprecision.

Consumer research on shrinkflation detection consistently finds that most people are unable to detect reductions below about fifteen percent of product volume without being primed to look for them. Below that threshold, the packaging aesthetics, brand familiarity and purchase habit override the subtle physical difference. Companies generally target reductions of five to twelve percent precisely because this keeps the change within the undetectable range for most consumers under normal shopping conditions.

The gaslighting quality of this comes from what happens when you do notice. You pick up the product, something feels slightly off, you check the label and it says 380 grams. You could have sworn it used to be 400. You check your memory and find it's imprecise. Maybe it was always 380 and you misremembered. Maybe you're thinking of a different size. The company's bet is that the combination of your imprecise memory, the unchanged packaging and your general reluctance to make a fuss will lead you to resolve the doubt in their favor. Most of the time, that bet pays off.

"My partner and I had an actual argument about whether a product had gotten smaller. We both had strong memories of it being a different size but neither of us could prove it. So I went online and found an archived version of the product page from two years ago. The previous size was listed. We were right. We hadn't been imagining it. The company had just banked on us not having the means or inclination to verify. Almost nobody does." — r/shrinkflation, 7.4k upvotes

The Profits That Followed

The thing that makes shrinkflation genuinely difficult to accept is what happened to corporate profits during the same period that consumers were being asked to accept less for the same price. The years from 2021 to 2024 were, by most measures, extraordinarily profitable for the consumer goods companies most aggressively engaged in shrinkflation. Several of the largest packaged food companies reported record profit margins during this period, margins that exceeded their pre-pandemic levels despite the genuine cost pressures they cited as the justification for size reductions.

The cost pressures were real. Ingredient prices did rise. Energy costs did increase. Supply chains did cost more. But in many documented cases, these costs had stabilized or declined by the time the size reductions were implemented or maintained. The initial justification for shrinking the product had ceased to be accurate, but the smaller product continued to be sold at the unchanged price, with the difference flowing directly to margins. Consumer advocacy groups and economists who analyzed this pattern described it as a ratchet effect: costs go up and product sizes shrink, costs come down and product sizes do not return to their previous levels.

This is not speculation or conspiracy thinking. It is documented in earnings calls and financial filings where executives describe their pricing power, discuss the importance of protecting margins and note with evident satisfaction that consumer price sensitivity had not significantly affected volume. The language of these disclosures, read alongside the simultaneous reduction in product sizes, tells a story with very little ambiguity about what was happening and why.

The Particular Cruelty for Budget Shoppers

Shrinkflation does not affect everyone equally. Higher-income shoppers who buy premium products are somewhat less exposed, partly because premium products shrink less frequently (the brand equity costs of being caught are higher) and partly because the proportional impact on their budget is smaller when it does happen. The heaviest burden falls on people shopping for value and own-brand products who are already spending the majority of their food budget and have limited ability to absorb the effective price increase that shrinkflation represents.

For households spending two hundred dollars a week on groceries, a consistent five to ten percent reduction across dozens of products while prices remain nominally flat represents a real reduction in food purchasing power. They are buying the same number of items for the same nominal amount but receiving less food. The official budget is unchanged. The actual food available is less. For households already calibrating carefully, this creates shortfalls that are confusing and demoralizing because the numbers on paper suggest nothing should have changed.

"I'm a single parent on a tight budget and I plan my weekly shop carefully. I noticed things weren't going as far as they used to and I genuinely thought I was losing track somewhere. I started keeping a spreadsheet. The issue wasn't my math. Products across my whole shop had gotten measurably smaller while the costs stayed flat. I was buying the same basket for roughly the same money and getting about eight percent less food overall. I was not imagining it." — r/povertyfinance, 18.3k upvotes

What Resistance Looks Like

Individual resistance to shrinkflation is limited because the information asymmetry is built into the system. You don't have the previous packaging in front of you when you're standing in the supermarket aisle. You don't carry a reference database of product weights in your memory. The companies know this. The friction of comparison is high and the stakes of any individual purchase feel low enough that sustained resistance is difficult to maintain.

What does help, modestly, is unit price comparison. Most supermarkets are now required in many jurisdictions to display unit prices alongside shelf prices. The price per 100 grams, per 100ml or per individual unit cuts through the packaging game and allows direct comparison. Using this number rather than the total price when comparing products across sizes and between brands removes much of the leverage that packaging design exploits. A company cannot shrink-inflate the unit price without it being visible on the shelf label.

Keeping a simple note of the weights of products you buy regularly also creates the comparison baseline that companies depend on you not having. A photograph of a label, a quick note in a grocery app, even a physical record of regular purchases creates accountability that the system is designed to prevent. It's more effort than a normal grocery shop requires, but it transforms the information asymmetry that makes shrinkflation so effective.

At the policy level, several countries including France and Brazil have introduced or are considering requirements that companies disclose size reductions on packaging with explicit comparison labeling. The labeling would require something like "new size: 380g, previously 420g" to appear on the product for a defined period after any reduction. The consumer goods industry has lobbied hard against these requirements. The vigor of that lobbying is, in its own way, a data point about how much the industry has to lose if consumers are systematically informed of changes they were specifically counted on not to notice.

The chips are smaller. The chocolate bar has a new groove. The ice cream doesn't fill the tub. The cereal box has a larger inner bag so the box doesn't rattle. None of this is your imagination. None of it is a misremembering. You are getting less for the same money and that has been a deliberate choice by the people selling you these products, made with full knowledge that your memory is imprecise enough that most of you will never be sure enough to complain. Some of you are sure now.
