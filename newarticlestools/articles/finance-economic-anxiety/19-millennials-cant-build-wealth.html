Why Millennials Can't Build Wealth No Matter What They Do

16,882 upvotes  ·  by u/generational_gap  ·  13 min read
▲ 16,882 people found this relatable
Advertisement

The advice was consistent. Go to college, get a degree in something practical, work hard, don't overspend, start saving early and the wealth will come. Millions of people followed this advice faithfully. They studied, graduated, entered the workforce, kept their spending reasonable and started putting money away when they could. And then they looked around at forty and found themselves with a fraction of the wealth their parents had at the same age, despite earning more in nominal terms, despite following the script more or less exactly. The script was wrong. Or more precisely, the script was written for a different economy than the one they actually lived through.

This is not a generational whine. The data on millennial wealth accumulation is stark enough that even commentators who spent years dismissing generational economic complaints have had to acknowledge it. Millennials, broadly defined as people born between 1981 and 1996, accumulated significantly less wealth in their twenties and thirties than Gen X and Boomers did at equivalent ages, even after controlling for the effects of the 2008 financial crisis. By the time the most recent comprehensive wealth surveys were conducted, the median millennial household held less net worth than the median Gen X household held at thirty, adjusting for inflation. The gap is real, it is large and it is not explained by spending habits.

"I did literally everything I was told to do. College degree, no credit card debt, started a 401k at twenty-three, never bought a new car, rented modestly for years while saving for a down payment. I'm forty-one and I have less real wealth than my father had at thirty-five. He didn't have a college degree. He bought a house on a factory worker's salary. The math simply doesn't work the same way anymore and I'm exhausted by people pretending it does." — r/lostgeneration, 21.4k upvotes

The Three Structural Forces That Changed Everything

Understanding why this happened requires looking at three structural shifts that converged in the decades when millennials were building their financial foundations. None of these shifts were secrets. None were random. All of them redistributed wealth upward in ways that compound over time and that no amount of individual financial discipline can overcome.

The first is the wage-to-asset-price divergence that has been explored in other articles in this series. Real wages for workers without inherited capital or equity compensation grew modestly over the period from roughly 1980 to today. Asset prices, particularly housing, equities and land, grew dramatically. The result is a world where the primary path to wealth is no longer earning and saving income but owning assets that appreciate. People who entered the 2000s with inherited assets or accumulated assets from the previous low-price era watched those assets multiply. People who entered the 2000s without assets, which is to say the vast majority of millennials at the start of their financial lives, could only access those assets at the higher prices, with the bulk of the appreciation already captured by previous holders.

This is not a subtle effect. The compound appreciation of assets over thirty years represents far more wealth than most workers could accumulate through labor income in a lifetime at current wage levels. The economy stopped primarily rewarding work and started primarily rewarding prior ownership. Millennials largely arrived at the ownership starting line after the race had already run most of its distance.

The Student Debt Machine

The second structural force is the transformation of higher education from a modest personal investment into a significant debt obligation. This transformation happened gradually and then quite suddenly. Tuition costs at American universities roughly tripled in real terms between 1980 and 2010. State funding for public universities declined consistently over this period, pushing more of the cost onto students. Simultaneously, the labor market signaling value of a degree remained high, meaning the rational response to rising costs was not to skip college but to borrow more to attend it.

The federal student loan program expanded to accommodate this, and private lending filled the gaps. By the time the median millennial graduated, they carried a debt burden at the start of their financial life that previous generations had not faced in anything like the same magnitude. This debt had compounding effects beyond the monthly payment. It delayed homeownership, since lenders count student loan obligations in debt-to-income ratios. It delayed retirement saving, since the cash flow consumed by debt service couldn't be invested. It increased risk aversion, since people carrying large debt burdens make more conservative career choices, accepting lower-paying stable employment over higher-risk higher-reward entrepreneurial paths.

"I graduated with sixty-eight thousand dollars in student loans for a degree that was supposed to set me up for life. By the time I paid down the principal to a manageable level in my mid-thirties, housing prices in my city had roughly doubled from when I graduated. I missed the window. The degree cost me the down payment I would have needed to catch it. I think about this constantly." — r/studentloans, 13.1k upvotes

The long-term wealth effect of student debt is larger than the debt itself. Every dollar spent on debt service is a dollar not invested at a time when compounding would have the most impact. A twenty-five-year-old who invests five hundred dollars a month for forty years accumulates dramatically more than the same person who spends their twenties and early thirties making debt payments and only begins investing at thirty-five. The arithmetic of compounding rewards early starts. The student debt crisis systematically delayed the starts of millions of people by a decade or more, with compounding wealth effects that will follow them for the rest of their financial lives.

The Housing Market That Closed

The third structural force is housing, and it deserves extended treatment because housing is simultaneously the most important wealth-building vehicle for ordinary households and the most thoroughly broken market in the modern economy.

For most of the twentieth century, the pathway from renter to homeowner was accessible to working and middle-class households within a relatively short window of adult life. A household earning a median income could typically afford a median-priced home within a few years of stable employment, provided they saved reasonably and maintained decent credit. The math worked. Not comfortably everywhere, not without sacrifice, but it worked.

By the 2020s that math had broken in most urban and suburban markets. Home prices in cities where jobs were concentrated had increased dramatically faster than incomes, often by factors of three to five over the preceding two decades. The down payment required for an entry-level home in a major metropolitan area represented, in many cases, more than twice the median annual household income in that area. At a savings rate that was, by any objective measure, quite disciplined, ten to fifteen percent of income after tax, accumulating a down payment sufficient to enter the market would take fifteen to twenty years. During those fifteen to twenty years, prices continued to rise, perpetually extending the horizon.

This created a generational wealth lock. People who bought homes in the 1990s or early 2000s sat on assets that appreciated by hundreds of thousands of dollars through no particular effort of their own, simply by virtue of having been in a position to buy at the right time. Their children, who were not in a position to buy at that time because they were children, found those same assets priced out of reach. The wealth created by housing appreciation flowed to people who already had it. Those who didn't were excluded from the market that generated it.

"My parents bought their house in 1994 for one hundred and ten thousand dollars. It's now worth nine hundred thousand. They didn't do anything special. They just bought when they were thirty and the price was still reachable on a normal income. I'm thirty-two. The equivalent house in the same neighborhood is nine hundred thousand dollars. I can't buy that on the same salary my father had when he bought his. It's not close. It's not even the same game." — r/firsttimehomebuyer, 19.6k upvotes

The Two Recessions at the Worst Possible Times

Into this already challenging structural context, millennials absorbed two major recessions at the most financially damaging possible moments in a working life. The 2008 financial crisis hit when most millennials were entering the workforce, an event economists have identified as particularly damaging because it is at career entry that the foundations of professional trajectory and savings habits are established. A recession at career entry means lower starting salaries, which tend to depress lifetime earnings relative to workers who entered in better conditions, less early employment in career-relevant roles, delayed promotions and slower progression up salary ladders that were themselves growing more slowly than they had for previous generations.

Research on the long-term effects of graduating into a recession shows that the income penalty follows workers for ten to fifteen years. The millennial cohort that graduated between 2008 and 2012 carried this penalty through their prime early-career wealth-building years. And then, just as many of that cohort were approaching their late thirties and beginning to recover ground, the pandemic recession arrived and generated its own set of disruptions, particularly concentrated in the service, hospitality and retail sectors where large numbers of younger workers were employed.

The compounding of two major recessions at sequential stages of wealth building is genuinely without precedent for a single generation in recent American economic history. The Boomers built their foundational wealth during an extended economic expansion. Gen X experienced one significant recession in their early careers and one in their late thirties, damaging but not sequential. Millennials experienced recession, fragile recovery, and recession again during the years that matter most for setting a financial trajectory.

The Comparison That Stops the Conversation

Whenever this analysis gets raised in public discourse, a predictable rejoinder arrives: previous generations had it hard too. They dealt with high interest rates in the 1980s, volatile employment in the 1990s, genuine poverty in earlier decades. The comparison is meant to reframe millennial economic complaints as excessive or historically illiterate.

It doesn't survive scrutiny. The high interest rates of the early 1980s were damaging to people servicing debt but happened at a time when housing prices relative to income were dramatically lower than today, meaning the option to wait and buy later as rates fell was available in a way it simply isn't now. Waiting to buy a house in 1985 meant buying a relatively more affordable asset in 1990. Waiting to buy in 2015 meant buying an even more expensive asset in 2020. The interest rate context and the asset price context interact, and comparing one era's rate environment to another's without accounting for the corresponding asset prices produces a misleading picture.

The honest comparison is not of hardships but of outcomes. At equivalent ages, how much wealth did different generations hold? That data is clear and it does not favor millennials. The gap is not explained by spending patterns or values differences. It is explained by the structural economic shifts outlined here and by the timing misfortune of entering financial adulthood at precisely the moment those shifts compounded most damagingly.

"My boomer relatives keep telling me they had it hard too. Their mortgage rate was fourteen percent in 1983. Okay. Their house cost fifty thousand dollars. My mortgage rate is six percent. My house cost four hundred and eighty thousand. The rate environment was worse for them. Everything else was dramatically better. You cannot just compare interest rates in isolation." — r/personalfinance, 31.2k upvotes

What Wealth Without Inheritance Looks Like Now

For millennials without parental wealth to draw on, which is most of them, the paths to meaningful wealth accumulation have narrowed to a degree that would have seemed implausible to a financial planner writing in 1990. The conventional path of earn, save and buy a home works only in markets where home prices are still at accessible multiples of local income, which increasingly means smaller cities and rural areas where job markets are also thinner. The equity compensation path works for a subset of workers in technology companies who receive stock that appreciates, but this requires access to a specific sector through networks and credentials that are themselves unevenly distributed. The inheritance path works for people with wealthy parents, who are, by definition, the people who already had a structural advantage.

What remains for people outside these paths is a more precarious kind of financial life, where income is sufficient to cover current expenses reasonably, but the mechanism by which ordinary people historically built wealth and transmitted it to their children, owning appreciating assets, is largely unavailable. The result is not poverty in the traditional sense. It's something more like a permanent middle: earning enough to not be in crisis, not enough to be building the kind of financial base that previous generations built at this age.

The political and economic implications of this will compound as millennials age and approach the traditional wealth transfer years. A generation that cannot build wealth cannot transmit it. The children of millennials without property wealth will also start without property wealth, continuing a cycle that diverges further from the Boomer children who inherited equity and used it to buy into the housing market before it became fully inaccessible. Wealth in the twenty-first century is becoming increasingly heritable in the specific sense that only people who already have it can reliably pass it on. That trajectory is not fixed. But acknowledging it honestly is the prerequisite to addressing it.
