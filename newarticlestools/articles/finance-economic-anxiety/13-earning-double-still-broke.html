Earning Double Your Parents Did and Still Feeling Broke

14,201 upvotes·MindBloom Editorial·12 min read
▲ 14,201 people found this relatable
Advertisement

Your father bought a house at 27 on a factory worker's wage. Your mother went back to college at 35, part-time, with three kids. Your parents took holidays. They had a pension. They owned things. By the time they were your age they had accumulated a material life that looked, from the outside, like stability. You earn more than they ever did — in some cases twice as much, in absolute terms — and you are renting a flat you can barely afford, you have no savings that would survive a serious emergency, and the prospect of owning property feels like a fantasy that belongs to a different generation's story rather than yours.

This experience — earning significantly more than previous generations while materially achieving significantly less — is so widespread it has generated its own vocabulary. "Wage stagnation relative to asset prices." "The housing affordability crisis." "The cost of living squeeze." These phrases are accurate but they're also clinical in a way that fails to capture the specific psychological weight of the experience, which is not just financial frustration but a profound sense of narrative betrayal. The story you were told — work hard, earn more, build a life — appears to have had crucial terms and conditions that were never disclosed.

The Numbers That Tell the Story

The gap between wage growth and asset price growth over the past three decades is one of the most consequential economic facts of modern life and one of the least understood by the people most affected by it. Between 1980 and 2024 in most developed economies, average wages grew modestly in real terms while house prices in major urban areas grew by anywhere from three hundred to one thousand percent. The same pattern holds for childcare costs, healthcare costs in markets without universal coverage, education and the cumulative cost of the subscription-and-service economy that replaced the purchase-and-own model of previous decades.

What this means in practice is that the items your parents could reach on a middle-income salary — a house, a car owned outright, a holiday, a pension funded by your employer, a degree with no debt — have either become dramatically more expensive relative to income or have been restructured in ways that extract ongoing payment rather than allowing one-time ownership. Your parents bought records. You subscribe to music. Your parents bought a car. You may never own one outright if you're using finance. Your parents had defined-benefit pensions. You have a defined-contribution scheme whose outcome depends on market performance you cannot control.

The Shame Trap

The dominant cultural response to financial struggle in a society that believes in meritocracy is to locate the problem in the individual. You're not managing well enough. You're spending on the wrong things. Have you tried making your own coffee? Cutting the streaming services? The specifics change but the structure is consistent: your financial difficulty is evidence of a personal failing and the solution is personal behaviour change.

This framing is not entirely wrong — individual financial behaviour matters and can be improved. But it is grotesquely insufficient as a complete explanation. The person earning forty thousand pounds in London who cannot save for a deposit is not primarily failing at budgeting. They are encountering a structural reality in which median income in that city is insufficient to participate in the housing market that previous median incomes could access. No amount of coffee abstinence resolves a structural gap of that magnitude. The shame the individual carries for "failing" to achieve what their parents achieved is therefore shame for a failure that is largely not theirs — which is both unjust and, because it is internalised rather than recognised as structural, deeply counterproductive.

"My dad bought his first house for three and a half times his annual salary. Mine would cost fifteen times mine. I'm not worse at money than he was. I'm in a different world."

The Hidden Cost of Living Crisis

One of the most insidious aspects of contemporary economic life is the number of costs that are real and significant but invisible in the headline numbers. The data that shows median income has grown tends not to fully account for the shift from employer-provided benefits to self-funded equivalents. The salary that looks similar to your parents' adjusted for inflation doesn't account for the fact that they had a pension their employer largely funded while yours is a pot you fill yourself. It doesn't account for the student loan repayments that come out before you ever see the number. It doesn't account for the cost of childcare that a previous generation could often have covered through family networks that are now geographically dispersed.

When you add back all the costs that are now individual responsibilities rather than social or employer-provided ones, the real purchasing power gap between your generation and your parents' at the same age tends to be significantly larger than the nominal wage comparison suggests. You are not imagining that things are harder. Things are harder. The difficulty has been systematically redistributed from collective to individual and the adjustment in wages has not come close to compensating for the transfer.

The Psychological Cost of Permanent Precarity

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from never quite having enough buffer. Not destitution — you are paying your bills, you are eating — but the chronic low-level anxiety of living without the safety margin that allows genuine rest. The awareness that one significant unexpected expense — a car repair, a medical bill, a period of reduced hours — would cause real material difficulty. This state is not emergency but it is not security either. It is a kind of suspended precarity in which you are always one event away from a genuine problem and the energy required to manage that awareness is substantial and continuous.

Research on financial stress consistently finds that the cognitive load of managing money under constraint is itself a significant burden — equivalent in some studies to a drop of roughly thirteen IQ points. You are not just struggling financially. The struggle itself is impairing your capacity to think, plan and make good decisions. The poverty penalty is real and it operates at every income level below genuine security, which is a much higher threshold than most people assume.

What This Generation Actually Needs to Hear

It needs to hear that the difficulty is real and structural rather than personal and correctable through attitude adjustment. It needs to hear that earning more than your parents in absolute terms while achieving less in real terms is not a paradox or a personal contradiction — it is the mathematically predictable outcome of specific policy choices made over several decades that shifted wealth upward and costs downward to individuals. It needs to hear that the shame of not having what your parents had at your age is misplaced and is doing active damage to your wellbeing without serving any useful function.

And then, once that is genuinely absorbed, it needs to hear the harder thing: that waiting for structural change that may come slowly will not solve your specific situation. That the systems that created this problem are not going to fix themselves on a timeline that serves you. That the realistic path forward involves a combination of genuine political engagement with the structural causes and a clear-eyed individual strategy for navigating the world as it actually is rather than as it ought to be. Those two things can coexist without the second one implying that the first is unnecessary. Your individual situation is not your fault. It is also, practically speaking, yours to solve.
