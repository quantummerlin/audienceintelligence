Does Moral Progress Actually Happen?

12 min read • Ethics & Philosophy
▲ 82,947 found this thought-provoking

In 1780, a man named Francis could legally own another human being in the United States. He could sell that person's children, beat them with limited legal consequence, and work them until they died. By 1865, this was abolished. By 1948, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights proclaimed that no one shall be held in slavery under any circumstances. The arc seems to bend toward justice. But the harder you look at the concept of moral progress, the more complicated it becomes to say what exactly is progressing, whether it can reverse, who gets to define the destination and what it means that people in the past were confidently wrong about ethics in ways we cannot yet see in ourselves.

The most intuitive argument for moral progress is simply the historical record. Steven Pinker's 2011 book "The Better Angels of Our Nature" assembled an extraordinary quantity of data showing that interpersonal violence, state-sanctioned torture, war deaths as a proportion of population and judicial cruelty had all declined dramatically over centuries. The abolition of slavery, the extension of the vote to women, the decriminalization of homosexuality across most of the world, the near-universal legal prohibition on torture, the end of public executions, the emergence of animal welfare law and the development of international humanitarian norms all mark what appear to be clear moral advances. When we look at past practices that were once routine and defended, we feel something more than aesthetic distaste. We feel they were genuinely wrong, and that recognizing their wrongness was a form of getting something right.

"I've been thinking about how confident people in the past were about things we now consider obviously monstrous. Aristotle defended natural slavery. Respectable intellectuals argued women were biologically incapable of sound reasoning. The question that haunts me is: what are we equally confident about right now that future generations will look back on with horror?" r/philosophy • 14,293 upvotes

This discomfort is sometimes called "moral galaxy-brained hindsight" by critics of the progress narrative, but it is actually one of the most intellectually honest positions available. The philosopher Peter Singer has made it a central feature of his work: if there is a consistent logic of moral progress, it seems to involve the gradual expansion of the circle of moral concern outward from narrow in-group to broader categories. We stopped (mostly) accepting that foreigners could be killed without justification. We stopped accepting that women lacked full moral standing. We are still in the process of working out the full implications of animal suffering. The trajectory suggests that whatever comes next in expanding moral concern is probably something we are currently minimizing or explaining away.

Three Theories of Why Progress Happens

Philosophers have offered several distinct accounts of why moral progress occurs when it does. The first is roughly rationalist: moral progress happens when better arguments displace worse ones. Peter Singer's utilitarian framework suggests that suffering is bad regardless of species, sex or race, and that historical moral failures were mostly failures of logical consistency applied to premises people already held. Once you accept that pain hurts equally whether experienced by a woman or a man, a Black person or a white person, it becomes incoherent to treat them differently. Progress on this account is the gradual working out of implications already present in our own moral premises.

The second account is more sociological. Moral norms change because material conditions change, and moral language follows. Marx's base-superstructure model suggests that slavery was defended philosophically as long as it was economically central, and moral arguments against it gained traction as industrial capitalism made wage labor more efficient. Women's moral and legal emancipation accelerated alongside their entry into the industrial workforce. On this view, moral progress is real but its engine is largely material rather than purely argumentative, which has the uncomfortable implication that moral regress is possible whenever material incentives realign.

The third account, associated with philosophers like Christine Korsgaard and Jürgen Habermas, is communicative or procedural. Moral progress happens when more people are included in the conversation about what morality requires. Historically, ethical norms were set by small groups of men with shared interests. When enslaved people, women, colonized populations and sexual minorities gained voice in public discourse, norms shifted because the moral costs previously invisible to those setting the rules became impossible to ignore. Progress on this account is driven less by better logic than by whose suffering gets heard.

The Problem of the Standard

Every claim that moral progress has occurred requires some standard against which progress is being measured. And that standard is itself contested. When we say that the abolition of slavery was moral progress, we are implicitly appealing to a principle like "enslaving human beings is wrong regardless of what any society believes." But this is precisely the kind of moral realism that many philosophers deny. If morality is merely the codification of evolving social norms, then calling abolition "progress" rather than simply "change" is smuggling in a criterion that the relativist framework cannot supply.

The philosopher Simon Blackburn has argued for a middle path he calls "quasi-realism": we can speak meaningfully of moral progress without committing to the existence of mind-independent moral facts. Our moral sensibilities evolve through processes of reflection, dialogue and revision, and we can legitimately evaluate earlier moral views as worse by our own lights without claiming cosmic authority. This is pragmatically satisfying but leaves open the question of whether our current lights are any more reliable than those of the people we are criticizing.

"What gets called 'moral progress' is often the moral views of the powerful getting updated. The same abolitionists who fought slavery often still thought the colonization of Africa was acceptable. The suffragettes who won votes for women frequently wanted nothing to do with votes for Black women. Progress is real but it's always partial and usually benefits the most privileged among the previously excluded first." r/AskHistorians • 18,492 upvotes

This criticism touches something important. Moral progress is rarely uniform across all affected groups. The abolition of formal slavery was followed immediately by convict leasing, sharecropping and Jim Crow segregation that preserved much of slavery's economic structure under different legal names. Women gained voting rights while domestic violence remained effectively legal. The LGBTQ rights movement's most visible victories have disproportionately benefited white, middle-class, gender-conforming gay and lesbian people. This does not mean progress is an illusion, but it does mean the narrative of a steady, expanding circle of concern badly simplifies a messier historical reality in which gains for some groups often coexist with continued exclusion or new forms of harm to others.

Reversibility and the Fragility Thesis

Perhaps the most unsettling challenge to progressive moral optimism is the evidence that moral gains can be reversed. The Weimar Republic preceded Nazi Germany. The legal emancipation of Black Americans after the Civil War preceded a century of terror, disenfranchisement and enforced racial hierarchy. The post-World War II international human rights architecture has been consistently eroded by the states that nominally endorse it. Rights once formally recognized have been de facto annulled through selective enforcement or the legal construction of new categories of exception.

Samuel Moyn's 2010 book "The Last Utopia" argued that modern human rights as a political project dates not to 1948 but to the 1970s, and that its apparent triumph in the late twentieth century was a response to the failure of more ambitious transformational politics rather than a fulfillment of long-building moral progress. On Moyn's account, human rights became the dominant moral framework precisely when socialist and anti-colonial movements lost momentum, making human rights less a pinnacle of moral achievement and more a floor established after higher ambitions collapsed. This framing makes the post-1990s erosion of human rights norms look less like an anomalous reversal and more like the inevitable unmasking of a framework that was never as secure as its advocates claimed.

The philosopher John Gray, consistently skeptical of progress narratives, argues in "Black Mass" and related work that the belief in moral progress is itself a form of secular eschatology inherited from Christian theology. Just as Christianity promised historical culmination in redemption, Enlightenment liberalism promises historical culmination in universal rights and rational governance. Gray's argument is not that moral improvements never happen but that there is no underlying trajectory guaranteeing they will persist. Without that guarantee, moral gains are always contingent on the maintenance of the political, economic and cultural conditions that produced them, and those conditions can change.

The Moral Telescope Problem

One of the most psychologically challenging aspects of thinking about moral progress is what we might call the moral telescope problem: it is much easier to see moral failures in the past than to identify them in the present. This is partly a structural feature of retrospection. We have the benefit of knowing how past debates ended, which gives us access to the arguments that won, the evidence that accumulated and the suffering that became impossible to deny. For contemporary issues, we are still in the middle of the argument, the evidence is still coming in and the suffering is still distributed in ways that make it easy for those not experiencing it to minimize.

Peter Singer's work on animal ethics is perhaps the clearest contemporary application of this logic. His 1975 book "Animal Liberation" argued that the capacity to suffer, not species membership, is the morally relevant criterion for consideration, and that factory farming inflicts enormous suffering on tens of billions of sentient beings annually for reasons of human convenience. The scale of this suffering vastly exceeds the suffering addressed by many of the historical moral advances we celebrate. Yet most people who readily condemn historical slavery continue to participate in systems that Singer argues are morally analogous. Whether Singer is right about this is genuinely contested, but the willingness to look honestly at the question requires the same kind of uncomfortable self-examination that moral progress has historically required.

"I teach ethics and every year I ask students to imagine they're historians from 2150 writing about the moral failures of our time. Factory farming and prison conditions come up constantly. But so does climate change, and the way we've knowingly continued causing catastrophic harm to future people because it's inconvenient to stop. The exercise is genuinely disturbing if you take it seriously." r/ethics • 22,847 upvotes

Climate change raises a version of the moral telescope problem with particularly high stakes. We currently have strong scientific consensus that our consumption patterns are imposing severe costs on future generations who have no voice in current political processes. The structural situation is not entirely unlike the situation of enslaved people whose suffering was externalized by those who benefited from their labor. Future historians, if there are enough of them and if they have honest access to what we knew and when we knew it, may view the period from roughly 1988 (when NASA's James Hansen testified to Congress about global warming) to the present as a case of morally culpable collective failure on a civilizational scale.

The Autonomy of Moral Reasoning

Against the more sociological or materialist accounts of moral change, there is persistent evidence that ideas themselves have causal force. The abolitionist movement in Britain preceded and shaped British economic interests rather than simply reflecting them, and the moral arguments of figures like Frederick Douglass, William Wilberforce and Harriet Beecher Stowe clearly moved public opinion in ways that created political pressure for legal change. The women's suffrage movement operated through decades of sustained argument and organizing that eventually shifted what counted as common sense about women's capabilities and rights. The gay rights movement succeeded partly through the strategic decision to make its arguments in the language of equality that mainstream American culture already professed to value.

This suggests that moral progress is neither purely rational (arguments automatically winning on their merits) nor purely material (norms mechanically following economic interests), but involves something more like a negotiated process in which arguments, interests, suffering, organizing and historical contingency all interact. Which arguments gain traction at which moments depends on complex factors, including who is making them, what existing moral frameworks they can appeal to and what countervailing interests they threaten. Moral progress on this account is genuinely produced by human effort rather than delivered by historical forces, which makes it both more achievable through deliberate action and more vulnerable to the failure to sustain that effort.

What Progress Looks Like From Inside

One deeply underappreciated feature of genuine moral progress is how unrecognizable it looks to most people living through it. The abolitionists of the early nineteenth century were widely considered fanatics and social destabilizers. The early suffragettes were frequently dismissed as mentally unhinged. Civil rights activists in the 1950s faced not only violent opposition but the considered judgment of many moderate intellectuals that they were moving too fast, using the wrong tactics and threatening social stability. This pattern repeats so consistently that it has become something like a diagnostic tool: positions that were once considered radical and are now considered obviously correct were, at the time, held by small minorities against the confident opposition of the mainstream.

The historian of philosophy Kwame Anthony Appiah explores this mechanism in "The Honor Code: How Moral Revolutions Happen" (2010), arguing that many historical moral shifts occurred not primarily through rational persuasion but through changes in what counted as honorable behavior. Dueling did not end primarily because people became convinced it was logically wrong but because the class of people who dueled began to find it embarrassing rather than prestigious. Foot-binding in China ended faster than almost anyone predicted once educated women began to see it as a marker of backwardness rather than beauty. Atlantic slavery became morally untenable partly because its practitioners began to find it shameful rather than simply profitable.

Appiah's account is sobering in one respect and encouraging in another. The sobering part is that rational argument plays a more limited role than moral philosophers tend to assume. The encouraging part is that social norms can shift faster than structural analysis suggests, because the mechanism of change is partly emotional and reputational rather than purely argumentative. The question of what current practices are most susceptible to rapid norm shift driven by changes in social honor is genuinely open and probably worth more attention than it receives.

The Question We Can't Yet Answer

The most honest position on moral progress accepts several uncomfortable things simultaneously. First, that genuine moral improvements have occurred and that denying this out of postmodern skepticism is itself a form of bad faith. The world in which women cannot vote and slavery is legal is worse in real moral terms than a world in which these things are prohibited. Second, that the record of moral progress is uneven, partial and often reflects the interests of those most advantaged within previously excluded groups rather than the most vulnerable. Third, that moral gains are genuinely reversible and that sustaining them requires ongoing political and cultural effort rather than trusting historical momentum. Fourth, that we are almost certainly currently wrong about something important in ways we cannot fully see, and that intellectual humility about this should inform how confidently we dismiss moral dissidents rather than simply confirming our existing views.

The hardest version of the question "does moral progress happen?" is not the historical question, which has a defensible affirmative answer. It is the forward-looking question: can we produce moral progress deliberately, reliably and quickly enough to address the harms we are currently inflicting on future generations, on animals, on the incarcerated and on the global poor whose suffering remains largely invisible to the populations whose consumption patterns produce it? That question is both the most urgent and the most genuinely uncertain, and it is the one that serious engagement with the history of moral progress should lead us toward rather than away from.
