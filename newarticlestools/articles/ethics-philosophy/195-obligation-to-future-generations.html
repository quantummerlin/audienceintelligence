What Do We Owe People Who Don't Exist Yet?

13 min read • Ethics & Philosophy
▲ 86,492 found this thought-provoking

Somewhere around 2075, assuming current demographic projections hold, a person will be born who will spend their childhood in a world significantly shaped by decisions being made right now. The climate they inherit, the institutions they live under, the public debt they are implicitly assigned at birth, the species that remain and the ones that have been extinguished, the nuclear materials that will require safe storage for centuries, the soil carbon levels, the antibiotic resistance profiles of the bacterial world: all of these will reflect choices made by people who will be long dead before that future person draws their first breath. The question of what obligations, if any, we have toward that person is one of the most philosophically challenging and politically consequential questions in contemporary ethics. It is also one of the least settled.

The intuitive answer for most people is that we obviously have some obligations to future generations. We should not poison the groundwater, exhaust the topsoil, collapse the fisheries or bequeath a debt load that cripples the economies they inherit. Most people feel this intuitively in the way they feel most moral requirements, as a kind of immediate moral perception rather than a conclusion of explicit reasoning. But when philosophers try to articulate the basis of this intuition and examine its implications, they run into a cluster of genuinely puzzling problems that have no agreed solutions and that have significant implications for how seriously we take obligations like climate mitigation.

The Non-Identity Problem

The most technically challenging problem in this area was identified by the philosopher Derek Parfit in his landmark 1984 work "Reasons and Persons," widely considered one of the most important philosophy books of the twentieth century. Parfit called it the non-identity problem, and it goes roughly like this. Suppose we adopt energy policies today that produce more carbon emissions than necessary, warming the climate substantially over the next century. The people alive in 2150 will live in a worse world as a result. It seems straightforward to say we have harmed them. But here is the complication: the particular people who will be alive in 2150 would not exist if we had adopted different policies. Different policies would have meant different economic conditions, different social networks, different meeting opportunities, different romantic relationships and ultimately different conception events. The specific individuals who will actually exist in 2150 owe their particular existence, in some sense, to the specific sequence of decisions that led to their being conceived. If we had made better decisions, different people would have been born.

This generates a paradox. We cannot have harmed a particular person by bringing them into existence in conditions worse than the alternative, if the alternative for that specific person was non-existence. Most of us believe that a life worth living, even a difficult one, is not a harm to the person living it compared to never having existed at all. So on standard accounts of harm that require making someone worse off compared to their counterfactual situation, our profligate present choices cannot harm the specific future people whose existence depends on those choices. Yet it still seems clearly wrong to adopt policies we know will make future lives much harder. We seem to be generating an obligation we cannot ground in individual harm.

"I spent three days working through Parfit's non-identity problem in grad school and it broke something in my head. The conclusion seems to be that we can make the world much worse for future people without harming any particular future person. That can't be right. But I've never found a response that fully resolves it." r/philosophy • 19,847 upvotes

Parfit himself found the non-identity problem deeply troubling and proposed several partial responses, none of which he considered fully satisfactory. One approach is to abandon person-affecting principles entirely, those moral principles that say only actions that make specific people worse off are wrong, and adopt impersonal principles that care about the quality of outcomes regardless of which particular people experience them. This move preserves our intuition that bad future climates are bad regardless of who lives in them, but it also opens the door to conclusions many find counterintuitive, including the "Repugnant Conclusion" Parfit himself named: that a world with an enormous number of barely-worth-living lives could be better than a smaller world of much happier people, because the aggregate wellbeing might be higher.

The Political Philosophy of Intergenerational Justice

Even setting aside the non-identity problem, political philosophy faces the question of how to incorporate future generations into frameworks designed for contemporaries. John Rawls's enormously influential theory of justice, presented in "A Theory of Justice" (1971), included an explicitly intergenerational element: the "just savings principle" required that each generation save a share of its resources for subsequent generations. But Rawls's veil of ignorance thought experiment, in which parties choose principles of justice without knowing their place in society, had difficulty accommodating intergenerational questions because it was unclear how to represent people not yet born behind the veil.

Edmund Burke's conservative tradition offers a different framework that takes intergenerational obligations seriously from a non-liberal direction. Burke famously described society as "a partnership between the dead, the living, and those who are yet to be born," implying obligations to preserve institutions, traditions and resources for successors. This framework grounds obligations to future generations not in their interests as individuals but in the nature of civilizational inheritance: we received our institutions, languages, accumulated knowledge and infrastructure from previous generations and are obligated to maintain and transmit them. The conservative framing is often dismissed by progressive philosophers but it captures something the individual-rights frameworks miss, namely that much of what makes individual lives possible is a collective inheritance that no generation produced alone.

Climate Change as the Test Case

Climate change has made obligations to future generations practically urgent in a way that has forced philosophers and legal scholars to engage with questions that might otherwise have remained academic. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's assessment reports document in detail the costs that will be borne by people living in the second half of the twenty-first century and beyond as a result of emissions choices being made now. These costs include heat mortality, agricultural disruption, coastal flooding, intensified storms, mass displacement and potential civilizational destabilization if warming exceeds 3-4 degrees Celsius. The people who will bear these costs are not yet born. The people making the decisions that produce them are alive now and will mostly be dead before the worst effects materialize.

Henry Shue's concept of "subsistence emissions" draws a distinction relevant here: all people have a right to the emissions necessary for bare subsistence (cooking, heating in cold climates, basic transportation), but luxury emissions beyond subsistence needs can be legitimately constrained by the interests of others, including future others. This framework has been influential in climate justice debates, particularly around the differential obligations of wealthy and poor nations. But it assumes that future people's interests have standing that can constrain present people's luxury choices, which requires grounding the obligations we are trying to articulate.

"What gets me about climate inaction is that it's not even a hard philosophical case. We're not talking about unknowable future consequences. We have detailed models. We know roughly what we're doing. The only honest description of continuing on the current path is: 'I prefer my convenience to significant harm to people I'll never meet who happen not to exist yet.'" r/collapse • 23,492 upvotes

The philosopher Stephen Gardiner has argued in "A Perfect Moral Storm" (2011) that climate change represents a uniquely difficult ethical problem not primarily because of philosophical puzzles about future people but because of the convergence of three "storms": an intergenerational storm (current people benefit, future people pay), a global storm (emitting nations differ from most affected nations) and a theoretical storm (our ethical frameworks were not designed for this kind of problem). This convergence creates what Gardiner calls "the perfect moral storm": a situation where every structural feature creates pressure toward rationalizing inaction, and where the fragmentation of responsibilities makes it easy for each actor to avoid feeling fully accountable.

Constitutional and Legal Approaches

Some legal systems have begun to institutionalize obligations to future generations in ways that attempt to operationalize the philosophical intuitions that moral frameworks struggle to fully ground. The Welsh Future Generations Act of 2015 established the world's first Future Generations Commissioner, an independent government officer charged with acting as a "guardian for the interests of future generations." Hungary's constitution includes a provision for a Parliamentary Commissioner for Fundamental Rights with specific responsibilities for future generations. The Netherlands' Urgenda case (2019) established that the Dutch government had a legal obligation to reduce greenhouse gas emissions based partly on its responsibilities to those who would bear future climate costs. In 2021, the German Federal Constitutional Court ruled that Germany's Climate Protection Act was partially unconstitutional because it pushed too much of the emissions reduction burden onto future generations, explicitly recognizing that current constitutional rights include protection from excessive climate burdens.

These legal developments are philosophically interesting because they represent attempts to create institutional representation for those who cannot represent themselves in current democratic processes. Representative democracy is built on the premise that those who bear the consequences of decisions should have a voice in making them. Future generations bear enormous consequences of current decisions but have no democratic voice. One response is to create proxy representation through commissioners or ombudsmen. Another, explored by the political theorist Alain Zerbib, involves weighted voting systems that give additional votes to younger voters on the grounds that they will live longer with the consequences of long-term decisions. Neither solution is philosophically clean, but both reflect recognition that the democratic legitimacy problem of intergenerational decision-making is real and not automatically solved by existing institutions.

The Population Ethics Tangle

Obligations to future generations cannot be fully separated from questions about how many future people there will or should be, and this enters territory that is philosophically perilous. If future people matter morally, do we have obligations to ensure there are more of them? The philosopher David Benatar has argued in "Better Never to Have Been" (2006) for a position he calls antinatalism: that coming into existence is always a harm, since existence brings suffering that would otherwise not occur, and non-existence brings no deprivation since there is no subject to be deprived. This view is philosophically challenging to refute without begging the question, though very few people find it intuitively acceptable.

More mainstream population ethics grapples with the question of whether a world with more people in it is better than a world with fewer, assuming both are above the threshold of lives worth living. The Repugnant Conclusion, generated by straightforward utilitarian calculation, says yes: ten billion barely-happy people are better than one billion very-happy people because the aggregate utility is higher. Almost no one accepts this conclusion, but avoiding it without abandoning utilitarian premises has proved extraordinarily difficult. Parfit spent decades trying to find a satisfactory population ethics and concluded before his death in 2017 that the field remained unresolved.

The Practical Upshot

Despite the philosophical difficulties, there is a practical upshot that most serious thinkers in this area endorse. The non-identity problem, the population ethics puzzles and the contested foundations of intergenerational justice do not add up to the conclusion that we have no obligations to future people. They add up to the conclusion that those obligations are philosophically complex to articulate, not that they are weak or absent. The convergence of intuitions across very different philosophical frameworks, including utilitarian ones focused on aggregate welfare, rights-based ones focused on protecting future people from severe deprivations and conservative ones focused on civilizational stewardship, suggests that the obligations are real even if their precise grounding is contested.

What is philosophically clear is that the standard political defense of inaction, that we cannot be confident enough about long-term consequences to justify short-term costs, becomes harder to sustain as the evidence about consequences becomes more certain. In the case of climate change, where the evidence is now extraordinarily strong, the argument from uncertainty is largely a rationalization. What is also clear is that institutional frameworks built entirely around the interests of the currently living are structurally biased toward under-weighting future people's interests, and that correcting this bias probably requires institutional innovation rather than simply better individual reasoning. The philosophers have not resolved the foundational questions, but they have identified the real structure of the problem clearly enough that continuing to ignore it is a choice rather than an inevitability.
