The Trolley Problem Is Real: Ethics in the Age of Autonomous Systems

12 min read  |  Ethics & Philosophy  |  AI Ethics
91,847 upvotes

Philippa Foot introduced the trolley problem in a 1967 paper in the Oxford Review and Judith Jarvis Thomson refined it into its now-famous form in 1985. A runaway trolley is headed toward five people tied to a track. You can pull a lever to divert it to a side track where only one person is tied. Do you pull the lever? Most people say yes. Thomson then varied the scenario: now you must push a large man off a bridge to stop the trolley with his body before it reaches the five. Most people who said yes to pulling the lever now say no to pushing, even though the arithmetic is identical. The divergence reveals that our moral intuitions are tracking something other than simple arithmetic, but the debate about what that something is has been running for more than fifty years without resolution.

The trolley problem was designed as a tool for probing moral intuitions in controlled, artificial conditions. Its usefulness was understood to be philosophical rather than practical because the situations it described were understood to be essentially impossible to encounter in real life. Nobody was actually going to be standing at a lever with a decision of exactly this structure.

And then we started programming machines to make exactly these kinds of decisions. Self-driving cars must encode responses to scenarios in which some harm is unavoidable and the choice is between different distributions of that harm. Military autonomous weapons systems must be programmed with rules of engagement that determine when and how deadly force is applied. Medical triage algorithms must rank patients for limited resources when all patients need treatment that only some can receive. Organ allocation systems must decide which of many candidates receives a scarce transplant. The trolley problem, long dismissed as a philosophical toy, has become an engineering specification.

The MIT Moral Machine and What It Found

In 2018, researchers at MIT published results from the Moral Machine experiment, an online platform that had presented millions of users in 233 countries with variations on trolley-problem-style scenarios involving self-driving cars. The platform asked users which outcome they would prefer when a self-driving car's brakes failed and it had to choose between hitting pedestrians in different groupings: old versus young, healthy versus less mobile, law-abiding versus law-breaking (pedestrians crossing on a red light), more people versus fewer people, humans versus animals.

The aggregate results confirmed some patterns that philosophers had theorized and revealed others that were surprising. Across most cultures, respondents preferred saving more lives over fewer, preferred saving younger people over older and preferred sparing humans over animals. But the magnitude of these preferences varied substantially across cultural clusters. A "Western" cluster including the United States, Europe and much of Latin America showed stronger preferences for sparing younger people and for preferential treatment of pedestrians following traffic rules. A "Southern" cluster showed stronger preferences for sparing women. An "Eastern" cluster showed weaker age-based preferences. These cultural variations are significant because any autonomous vehicle system deployed globally would have to make choices that embody some version of these preferences, and there is no culturally neutral option available.

The Moral Machine results also revealed an uncomfortable reality: the people most affected by autonomous vehicle decisions, pedestrians, had no role in specifying the algorithms that would make those decisions. The algorithms would be specified by engineers, approved by regulators and sold by manufacturers, all of whom have interests in the outcome that are not identical to the public's interests. This is a democratic deficit in the design of systems that make life-and-death decisions, and the field of machine ethics has not produced a satisfactory answer to it.

"What bothers me about autonomous vehicle ethics isn't the trolley problem itself. It's that whoever programs the car has already made the decision, once, in an office, before any accident happens. There's no human judgment in the moment. There's just someone's pre-committed utilitarian calculation running in code." r/philosophy  |  27,492 upvotes

Military Autonomous Weapons

The most consequential real-world deployment of trolley-problem-style algorithmic decision-making is in military autonomous weapons systems, where the decisions involve lethal force and the stakes of getting the ethics wrong are measured in human lives. The International Committee of the Red Cross has documented more than fifty countries with some form of autonomous weapons capability, and the legal and ethical frameworks governing their use remain deeply unsettled.

International humanitarian law requires that attacks distinguish between combatants and civilians, that the harm caused to civilians be proportionate to the anticipated military advantage and that all feasible precautions be taken to minimize civilian casualties. These are judgments that depend on context, intention, knowledge and human assessment in ways that current AI systems cannot replicate reliably. The machine that fires at a target classified as a combatant with 80% confidence cannot exercise the judgment that a human soldier exercises when they see a child being used as a human shield, or when the combat situation changes in ways the training data did not anticipate, or when the lawful order conflicts with a clear ethical violation.

The Campaign to Stop Killer Robots, a coalition of NGOs, has been calling for a preemptive international ban on fully autonomous weapons since 2012, arguing that weapons that select and engage targets without meaningful human control violate both existing international law and fundamental ethical principles about accountability and human dignity. The counterarguments, primarily from military establishments in the United States, Russia, China, Israel and South Korea, focus on the potential advantages of autonomous systems in terms of reaction speed, reduced risk to military personnel and potentially more consistent application of rules of engagement than human soldiers under combat stress. Neither argument has carried the day in international forums, which have so far produced extensive discussion but no binding treaty.

Medical Triage and Resource Allocation

The COVID-19 pandemic brought trolley-problem-style resource allocation decisions into hospitals across the world in a way that was no longer theoretical. Intensive care units faced scenarios in which more critically ill patients needed ventilators than ventilators were available, and medical staff and institutional protocols had to determine how to allocate the scarce resource. Many hospitals developed crisis standards of care protocols that used algorithmic scoring systems to make these allocations, attempting to systematize decisions that would otherwise be made inconsistently by individual clinicians under enormous stress.

The ethical debates about these protocols illuminated the deep tensions between different approaches to triage ethics. Utilitarian approaches favor allocating resources to maximize lives saved or life-years saved, which typically means prioritizing younger patients with better prognoses. These approaches conflict with equity-based arguments that people who have historically received less access to healthcare, including Black and Indigenous patients who often have comorbidities reflecting generations of discrimination rather than personal choices, should not be further disadvantaged by triage systems that treat their comorbidities as evidence against allocation. They also conflict with first-come-first-served principles that treat arrival time as a fair allocation mechanism, which they are not, given that ability to access care quickly also reflects existing inequalities.

Organ allocation presents a longer-standing version of the same problem. The United Network for Organ Sharing allocation system in the United States uses a complex scoring algorithm that weighs urgency of need, time on waiting list, geographic proximity to the donor and various compatibility factors. The system has been revised multiple times to address documented inequities, including inequities that disadvantaged Black patients who had higher rates of certain complications that the original formula penalized. Each revision involves explicit choices about which values to prioritize, and there is no version of the algorithm that does not embody contestable moral choices.

The Deontological Limits of Algorithmic Ethics

One of the most significant insights that emerges from the practical deployment of trolley-problem-style algorithms is the inadequacy of purely consequentialist ethics for governing autonomous systems. The utilitarian approach, program the machine to minimize expected harm, runs into two serious problems. The first is epistemic: the probability distributions over outcomes that expected value calculations require are often unavailable or unreliable in real situations, and the appearance of mathematical precision conceals enormous uncertainty. The second is that strong and widely shared moral intuitions resist the utilitarian framework in ways that suggest it is missing something morally important.

The footbridge variant of the trolley problem, in which pushing someone to their death is required to save five others, provokes near-universal revulsion even among people who endorse the utilitarian calculus in the lever variant. This asymmetry, which has been replicated in neural imaging studies by Greene and colleagues at Harvard, suggests that morally important distinctions are being tracked by the revulsion that the utilitarian framework fails to capture. The distinction between using someone as a means to an end and allowing harm as a side effect of achieving a different end, articulated in Kantian ethics as the prohibition on treating persons merely as means, provides a principled account of the asymmetry that has practical implications for how autonomous systems should be designed.

A fully articulated ethics for autonomous systems would need to incorporate not just consequentialist considerations about outcomes but deontological constraints that function as hard limits on what the system may do regardless of the expected value calculation. It would need to include some mechanism for handling moral uncertainty, the recognition that our collective moral knowledge is incomplete and that algorithms should be designed with humility about their moral reasoning. And it would need to incorporate democratic legitimacy, some form of meaningful public input into the values being encoded, rather than treating the encoding of values in autonomous systems as a purely technical problem to be solved by engineers and ethicists working in isolation from the people most affected by their decisions.

Philippa Foot and Judith Jarvis Thomson created a philosophical tool for clarifying moral intuitions. They could not have foreseen that their thought experiment would become a practical engineering problem. The fact that it has should prompt not just better engineering but a wider societal conversation about which values we want encoded in the systems that are increasingly making consequential decisions on our behalf, and who gets to participate in that decision.
