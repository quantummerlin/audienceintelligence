Effective Altruism: The Good, the Flawed and the Dangerous

13 min read  |  Ethics & Philosophy  |  Moral Philosophy
84,293 upvotes

The core idea at the heart of effective altruism is genuinely difficult to argue against. If you want to do good in the world, you should think carefully about how to do the most good per unit of resources — time, money, attention — that you have available. You should look for evidence of what actually works rather than what feels good to support. You should be willing to follow the evidence wherever it leads, even if it points toward causes that are unsexy or unfamiliar. You should take seriously the moral weight of people who are far away and very different from you. These are all things that careful, honest ethical thinking supports.

The effective altruism movement, which grew out of the work of philosophers Peter Singer at Princeton and William MacAskill at Oxford and was institutionalized through organizations including GiveWell, the Centre for Effective Altruism and 80,000 Hours, has produced genuine goods. It has directed billions of dollars toward rigorously evaluated global health interventions, including bednet distribution, vitamin A supplementation and direct cash transfers, that have saved an estimated hundreds of thousands of lives. It has changed how many thoughtful people in wealthy countries think about their charitable giving, shifting attention from emotionally resonant but minimally effective causes toward interventions with documented large-scale impacts. At its best it represents what moral philosophy should look like when it is taken seriously as a practical guide to action.

At its worst it is something considerably more troubling: a framework that mistakes quantification for wisdom, that uses the language of rigor to smuggle in ideological assumptions, that has enabled some genuinely alarming reasoning and that was, in its most prominent institutional expression, associated with one of the largest financial frauds in recent history. Both the good and the bad deserve careful examination, because the movement is influential enough that its failures matter as much as its successes.

What the Rigorous Version Gets Right

GiveWell, the charity evaluator that emerged from early effective altruism thinking, has been genuinely transformative in the way it approaches charitable evaluation. Before GiveWell and similar organizations, the dominant model of charity evaluation was overhead-focused: charities were assessed primarily on what percentage of their budget went to "programs" versus "administration," a metric that was easy to measure and almost completely uninformative about actual impact. A charity could have very low overhead and be completely ineffective at its stated mission, or have relatively high overhead because it invested seriously in monitoring and evaluation and deliver extraordinary value.

GiveWell replaced the overhead model with impact measurement: how much good does a dollar to this charity actually do, measured in outcomes like lives saved, diseases prevented or income increased, and how confident can we be in that estimate? This is a much harder measurement task, and GiveWell is honest about the significant uncertainties involved, but it is the right question. The Against Malaria Foundation, which distributes insecticide-treated bednets and which GiveWell has repeatedly named as among its top-rated charities, has been evaluated by independent researchers as preventing one death for every $3,000 to $5,000 in donations, at a level of confidence that no comparable charity in wealthier country contexts could claim. Directing philanthropic dollars toward this kind of documented effectiveness rather than toward emotionally resonant but minimally impactful causes is a genuine improvement in how charitable resources are used.

Singer's 1972 philosophical paper "Famine, Affluence and Morality," which argued that if it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it regardless of how far away it is or how many others could also help, remains one of the most bracing and difficult arguments in contemporary ethics. The paper's core challenge, that wealthy people in rich countries have enormous power to prevent suffering at relatively modest cost to themselves and are largely not using it, is one that deserves to be taken seriously and that most of us, on honest reflection, cannot fully answer.

Where Quantification Becomes a Problem

The effective altruism framework's commitment to quantification and comparison across causes produces a specific set of distortions that become more pronounced the more ambitiously the framework is applied. The fundamental issue is that quantification requires commensurability: you can only compare apples and oranges once you have decided what unit to measure them in. The effective altruism movement has largely adopted "welfare" or "wellbeing" as its unit of moral currency, measured typically through metrics like quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) or disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) borrowed from public health practice. These are useful tools in their original contexts, but using them as the master currency for comparing all possible interventions imports a set of philosophical assumptions that are not universally accepted and that are not explicitly argued for within the framework.

The assumption that all morally relevant considerations can be reduced to a single welfare metric, and that interventions can therefore be straightforwardly rank-ordered, is a specific philosophical position, essentially a version of classical utilitarianism, that many serious ethical thinkers reject. Rights-based approaches to ethics hold that some actions are wrong regardless of whether they maximize welfare. Virtue ethics holds that the character of the agent matters morally in ways that outcome metrics do not capture. Contractualist ethics holds that what matters is what principles could be justified to everyone affected, not what maximizes aggregate welfare. Effective altruism's implicit adoption of a utilitarian framework is not intellectually dishonest, but it is a philosophical choice that is sometimes presented as if it were simply the rational approach to ethics rather than one specific theoretical tradition among several serious competitors.

Longtermism and Its Discontents

The most contested development within effective altruism has been the rise of "longtermism," associated primarily with MacAskill's 2022 book "What We Owe the Future" and with Oxford philosopher Nick Bostrom's work on existential risk. Longtermism holds that the most important consideration in ethical decision-making is the long-run trajectory of civilization, including the welfare of the vast numbers of people who may exist in the future, and that interventions aimed at reducing existential risks (risks of human extinction or permanent catastrophic reduction in human potential) are therefore far more important than interventions aimed at reducing current suffering.

The mathematical logic of longtermism, if its premises are accepted, is nearly irresistible. If the universe contains the potential for trillions of human lives in the deep future, and if their welfare matters equally to the welfare of people alive today, then almost any probability reduction in extinction risk produces an expected value that dwarfs the value of any intervention aimed at reducing present suffering. The math makes present people's needs look almost irrelevant by comparison.

The problems with this reasoning are multiple and serious. First, the uncertainty involved in predicting the long-run trajectory of civilization is so enormous that the expected value calculations on which longtermism rests are essentially fictional. A one-in-a-million reduction in extinction risk over a million-year time horizon sounds impressive in an equation and means almost nothing in practice, because we have no meaningful ability to estimate either the baseline risk or the effect of any specific intervention on it. As philosopher Émile Torres and others have argued extensively, the longtermist's willingness to act on calculations that involve essentially unconstrained speculation about the deep future, combined with the movement's claim that this speculation justifies redirecting resources away from present suffering, is not a feature of rigorous reasoning but a license for motivated reasoning dressed in mathematical clothing.

Second, longtermism has been used to justify a focus on AI safety and biosecurity research, often conducted by the same highly educated professionals who constitute the EA movement's core membership, at the expense of near-term global health interventions that primarily benefit people in low-income countries. The coincidence between the interests of affluent technocrats and the recommendations of a framework that claims to represent universal moral reasoning should at minimum trigger scrutiny about whose welfare is actually being prioritized.

Sam Bankman-Fried and the "Earn to Give" Problem

The collapse of FTX in November 2022 and the subsequent conviction of its founder Sam Bankman-Fried on fraud charges raised pointed questions about the movement with which he was prominently associated. Bankman-Fried had built his public identity partly around effective altruism, specifically around the "earn to give" strategy advocated by some within the movement, which holds that the most effective thing a highly capable person can do for the world is pursue the highest-earning career available and donate a large portion of the resulting income to effective causes. He had pledged billions to EA-aligned causes, was celebrated by prominent figures in the movement and sat on the board of the Future Fund, an EA-affiliated philanthropic vehicle.

The fraud that Bankman-Fried committed, which involved misappropriating customer funds, was not in any direct way caused by EA philosophy. But several commentators, including philosophers within the EA community, noted that the specific reasoning patterns that EA fosters, including a willingness to make large bets on uncertain expected value calculations, a tendency to treat conventional ethical constraints as obstacles to be weighed against larger goods and an intellectual culture that celebrates contrarian reasoning about ethics, may have contributed to the cognitive environment in which the fraud felt justifiable. Bankman-Fried himself suggested in post-conviction interviews that a version of EA reasoning had contributed to his decision-making, though the reliability of this self-accounting is obviously limited.

The broader problem the FTX collapse illuminated is what critics call "galaxy-brained" reasoning within EA: the tendency for the framework's permission to override conventional moral intuitions in service of calculated expected value to produce conclusions that most people would immediately recognize as wrong but that the reasoning has been used to justify. When a framework that claims to represent the most rigorous approach to ethics can be used to rationalize financial fraud, the framework has a structural problem that post-hoc disclaimers do not resolve.

What a More Honest Effective Altruism Would Look Like

The critique of effective altruism's excesses does not require abandoning the core commitment to doing the most good one can with the resources available. What it requires is more honesty about the philosophical assumptions being made, more humility about the limits of quantification and more willingness to treat strong moral intuitions as evidence that counts against a conclusion rather than as biases to be overcome.

The practice of earning significant amounts of money in potentially harmful industries on the grounds that the resulting donations will do more good than the harm caused is an example of the kind of reasoning that requires more scrutiny than the movement has typically given it. The hedging and gaming of conventional ethics using expected value calculations is an example of galaxy-brained reasoning that should trigger suspicion rather than admiration. The colonization of the philanthropic agenda by the concerns of an affluent, highly educated, predominantly male and Western demographic, even when conducted with apparently rigorous methods, deserves more critical examination than it typically receives from within the movement.

What remains genuinely valuable is the core empirical commitment: look at evidence, measure impact, be willing to follow the evidence toward unfamiliar conclusions, take seriously the interests of people who are far away and who cannot advocate for themselves within existing power structures. These are good principles for ethical practice that can be held without the full ideological apparatus of the movement that has grown up around them. The question of what to do with your resources and your time to make the world better is worth taking seriously. The confidence with which some EA thinkers have believed they have solved that question is not.
