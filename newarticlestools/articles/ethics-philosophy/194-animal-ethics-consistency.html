Animal Ethics and the Consistency Problem

13 min read • Ethics & Philosophy
▲ 88,293 found this challenging

Most people in Western societies hold the following combination of beliefs without apparent difficulty. They believe it is wrong to be cruel to animals. They believe dogs and cats deserve legal protection from abuse. They believe the people who run dogfighting rings are criminals who deserve prosecution. And they eat industrially farmed pork, chicken and beef produced under conditions that the philosopher Peter Singer and many animal welfare scientists argue inflict suffering on a scale that dwarfs anything the dogfighting industry could manage. This combination is not a fringe phenomenon. It describes the revealed preferences and stated values of a substantial majority of the population. And it represents one of the most philosophically interesting cases of moral inconsistency in contemporary life.

The philosophical literature on animal ethics has been building since Jeremy Bentham's famous 1789 footnote in "Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation," in which he argued that the relevant question regarding animals was not "Can they reason?" nor "Can they talk?" but "Can they suffer?" Singer's 1975 book "Animal Liberation" built a systematic case on utilitarian grounds that species membership is no more morally relevant than race or sex, and that inflicting equivalent suffering on an animal for less weighty reasons than we would require for inflicting that suffering on a human is a form of prejudice Singer named speciesism. Tom Regan's "The Case for Animal Rights" (1983) reached similar conclusions from a rights-based framework, arguing that animals are "subjects of a life" with inherent value that cannot be traded off against human convenience. These arguments have been developed, challenged and refined over fifty years. The philosophical case against factory farming in particular is now quite robust. And factory farming has, if anything, intensified and expanded in the decades since.

"The thing that broke through for me was realizing I had completely different moral frameworks running in parallel. For dogs I had the 'can they suffer' framework, automatically. For pigs I had the 'they're food' framework, automatically. Both were emotional rather than reasoned. The question was which one, on reflection, I could actually defend." r/philosophy • 16,847 upvotes

The inconsistency Singer identifies is not hard to locate. Pigs score higher than dogs on most measures of cognitive complexity, problem-solving ability and social intelligence. Their capacity to experience pain, fear and stress is biologically comparable. There is no principled distinction between a dog and a pig that would justify the radically different moral treatment they receive in practice. The distinction is cultural, historical and economic rather than grounded in any feature of the animals themselves. We have relationships with dogs. We eat pigs. The moral frameworks follow the relationships rather than the facts.

The Scope of the Problem

Understanding why animal ethics is philosophically urgent requires some engagement with the actual scale of industrial animal agriculture. Approximately 80 billion land animals are slaughtered for food annually worldwide, with roughly 99 percent of US farm animals raised in concentrated animal feeding operations (CAFOs) according to data from the Sentience Institute. A standard commercial broiler chicken lives for 42 days in conditions of severe crowding, often unable to walk by the end of its short life due to the selective breeding that produces rapid weight gain. A gestating sow in a gestation crate lives in a metal stall barely larger than her body for most of her reproductive life. Layer hens in battery cages have less space than a sheet of paper. These conditions are not disputed by the industry. They are simply considered economically necessary.

The aggregate suffering involved is almost impossible to reason about intuitively because the numbers are too large. But consider a single comparison. The annual slaughter of chickens in the United States alone exceeds nine billion birds. The total number of humans killed in all wars across the entire twentieth century, the deadliest in recorded history, is estimated at somewhere between 70 and 150 million. We produce and kill more chickens in the United States in a single year than were killed in every war of the bloodiest century. Whether this comparison is philosophically meaningful depends entirely on whether you accept that animal suffering counts morally at all. If you accept even a weak version of that claim, the numbers become difficult to dismiss.

The Standard Defenses and Their Problems

The philosophical defenses of current practice are worth examining seriously. The most common are: animals are not rational agents and therefore lack moral standing; animals would not exist at all if we did not farm them; killing is not harmful if done painlessly; and humans have always eaten animals, making it natural and therefore acceptable. Each of these arguments has been analyzed extensively in the philosophical literature, and none holds up particularly well under scrutiny.

The rationality argument faces the problem that it would also exclude infants, people with severe cognitive disabilities and many other humans we do not think it permissible to harm. If the capacity for rationality is not required for moral standing in those cases, it is unclear why it should be required for animals. This is sometimes called the "argument from marginal cases," and while philosophers have proposed various responses, none has achieved consensus. The "they wouldn't exist otherwise" argument proves too much: it would seem to justify creating humans in order to kill them, which strikes almost everyone as clearly wrong. The painless killing argument ignores that factory farming involves enormous suffering well before death, and also runs into the philosophical puzzle of whether death itself is a harm to a being with interests and a future experience to lose. The "naturalness" argument has problems that are almost too numerous to list, beginning with the fact that many things natural to humans are deeply harmful and many things beneficial to humans are thoroughly artificial.

"I had a philosophy professor who pointed out that every defense of eating meat I could come up with either proved too much (justified things I clearly didn't want to justify) or relied on a distinction that dissolved when examined. Not saying everyone needs to be vegan but the honest position is 'I do this because I want to and it's convenient,' not 'I have a principled philosophical justification.'" r/ethicsvegan • 12,394 upvotes

The philosopher David DeGrazia makes a useful distinction between the question of whether eating animals is morally permissible and the question of whether participating in factory farming specifically is morally permissible. He argues that even people who are unconvinced by the strongest arguments for animal rights can recognize that the conditions of industrial animal agriculture impose suffering on a scale that is impossible to justify by appeal to the modest pleasures of human taste preference. This is a more limited claim that asks only for proportionality between the suffering inflicted and the benefit obtained, and it is much harder to resist than the full Singer argument.

The Psychology of Moral Disengagement

If the philosophical arguments are as strong as their proponents claim, the persistence of widespread participation in factory farming requires psychological explanation. The social psychologist Melanie Joy has studied what she calls "carnism," the invisible belief system that conditions people to eat certain animals while caring for others. Her analysis draws on Albert Bandura's concept of moral disengagement, the psychological mechanisms through which people suspend their own moral values in particular domains. Joy identifies several mechanisms that enable carnism: invisibilization (keeping slaughterhouse realities hidden from consumers), numbing (desensitizing people to information that would otherwise produce distress), normalization (treating current practice as simply what people do), and the three N's that people invoke to justify meat eating: it is Normal, Natural and Necessary.

The invisibilization mechanism is perhaps the most institutionally significant. Most people who consume factory-farmed animals have never visited a commercial chicken shed, a gestation crate facility or a slaughter line. The conditions are almost never shown on packaging. Video footage taken inside these facilities has been so disturbing when released that several US states have passed "ag-gag" laws criminalizing the recording of conditions inside agricultural facilities, laws that have been challenged in federal court as violations of the First Amendment. The active legal protection of invisibilization is itself philosophically interesting. If the practices were morally unproblematic, there would be no reason to criminalize their documentation.

The Wild Animal Suffering Extension

For philosophers who take animal ethics seriously, the logic does not stop at domesticated animals. The animal welfare philosopher Oscar Horta and others have argued that if animal suffering matters morally, then the suffering of wild animals in natural ecosystems also matters, and there is an enormous amount of it. Most animals born in nature die very young through predation, disease, parasites or starvation. The philosopher David Pearce has argued for a long-term project of redesigning ecosystems through biotechnology to reduce suffering throughout the animal kingdom, a position known as compassionate intervention or "wild animal welfare."

This extension strikes most people as absurd or deeply misguided, and critics including Martha Nussbaum have argued that it misunderstands the nature of animals as beings with species-specific ways of flourishing that include natural behaviors that may involve suffering. But the extension is philosophically honest in one respect: if suffering is bad because it is experienced as bad, then the location of that suffering in a forest rather than a factory does not obviously reduce its moral weight. The discomfort most people feel with the wild animal suffering argument is worth reflecting on. It may indicate a genuine limit of utilitarian reasoning about animals, or it may indicate that our intuitions have been calibrated primarily by the settings in which we have historically encountered animals rather than by consistent moral reasoning.

"The wild animal suffering argument was the point where I realized I was in genuinely uncharted philosophical territory. My intuition said it was wrong to interfere with nature even to reduce suffering. But I couldn't articulate why in terms that didn't also imply a lot of other things I wasn't willing to accept. I still don't have a clean answer." r/philosophy • 9,847 upvotes

Rights Frameworks vs. Welfare Frameworks

Within animal ethics, there is a significant disagreement between rights-based and welfare-based approaches that has practical implications. Tom Regan's rights view holds that animals have inherent value that cannot be traded against human interests: they should never be used merely as means to human ends, and gradual welfare improvements within a system that treats them as property are morally insufficient. Singer's utilitarian view holds that what matters is the aggregate experience, and welfare improvements that reduce suffering are genuinely valuable even if they fall short of full animal liberation. Regan criticized the welfare approach as stabilizing rather than challenging a fundamentally unjust system. Singer argued that Regan's perfectionism sacrificed real welfare gains for moral purity.

This debate maps onto practical disagreements about campaigns for welfare reforms like larger cage sizes, better slaughter practices and enriched environments within industrial agriculture. From a rights perspective, these campaigns make consumers feel better about participating in a system they should exit. From a welfare perspective, each incremental improvement matters because it reduces actual suffering experienced by actual animals regardless of whether the system is eventually abolished. The debate has not been resolved and probably cannot be resolved on purely logical grounds because it reflects different underlying values about what moral progress requires.

The Emerging Landscape

Several developments in the past decade have changed the practical landscape in ways the philosophical debates of the 1970s and 1980s could not anticipate. Cultivated meat, grown from animal cells without slaughter, has moved from laboratory novelty to limited commercial availability. GOOD Meat received the first USDA approval for cultivated chicken in June 2023. Plant-based protein technology has improved substantially, with companies like Beyond Meat and Impossible Foods producing products that satisfy many consumers who found previous alternatives unpalatable. Neither technology has yet achieved the cost parity or market penetration that would make it transformative, but the direction of travel suggests that the economic argument for maintaining current animal agriculture is more contingent than its defenders typically acknowledge.

More significantly, the scientific evidence for animal sentience and cognition has accumulated substantially. The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, signed by a prominent group of neuroscientists at a conference attended by Stephen Hawking in 2012, stated that non-human animals possess the neurological substrates that generate consciousness. Fish pain research has established that fish have nociceptors and respond to noxious stimuli in ways that strongly suggest conscious pain experience, challenging the widespread assumption that fish welfare can be largely disregarded. Cephalopod intelligence research has produced remarkable evidence of problem-solving, play behavior and individual personality in octopuses and squid. The scientific boundary of creatures whose suffering is morally negligible has been pushed outward with each research advance, and the philosophical frameworks that treat capacity for suffering as the threshold of moral concern follow that boundary wherever it goes.

Living With the Inconsistency

The honest position for most people engaging seriously with animal ethics is probably not a clean resolution but a reckoning with genuine inconsistency. Very few people have examined the full philosophical case and concluded it is wrong. Many have examined it and concluded it is largely right but continued to participate in systems it condemns, for reasons of habit, convenience, social pressure, cultural identity, the difficulty of individual action against structural systems and the general human capacity to hold uncomfortable truths at arm's length. This is not an admirable position, but it is an honest one, and it is considerably more honest than the claim that the philosophical arguments are weak or that one has a principled defense of current practice that holds up under examination.

Singer himself has argued that the goal is not moral purity but harm reduction. Every meal that does not involve factory-farmed animal products is a genuine reduction in contribution to a system of suffering regardless of whether the individual making that choice is perfectly consistent. The perfect should not be the enemy of the good. But the "I do what I can" response is also available for every other moral issue, and it is worth noticing when it is deployed primarily as a way of not examining the gap between stated values and actual behavior. The consistency problem that animal ethics poses is uncomfortable precisely because it is not someone else's inconsistency. It is almost everyone's, and the animals bearing the cost of that inconsistency are unable to participate in the philosophical conversation about it.
