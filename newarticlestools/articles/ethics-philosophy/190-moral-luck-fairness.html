Moral Luck: Why the Circumstances of Your Life Are Not Your Fault

12 min read  |  Ethics & Philosophy  |  Justice & Responsibility
79,847 upvotes

Two drivers run a red light. One makes it across the intersection safely; the other strikes a child who ran into the road. The first driver is briefly startled and drives on, perhaps reflecting on their own carelessness. The second driver faces vehicular manslaughter charges, a lifetime of guilt and a public record of serious wrongdoing. Their actions were identical. Their culpability, in any rational moral accounting, should be identical. But they will be treated as radically different people by the law, by their communities and almost certainly by themselves. The only difference between them is luck.

This is the problem of moral luck, one of the most unsettling ideas in contemporary moral philosophy, and one that becomes more unsettling the more honestly you apply it. The concept was introduced in its modern form simultaneously and independently by philosophers Thomas Nagel and Bernard Williams in companion papers published in 1976 in the Aristotelian Society Proceedings. Nagel called it "moral luck." Williams called it, more bleakly, "moral residue." Both were pointing at the same gap between what we think moral responsibility requires, which is control, and what our actual practices of moral judgment presuppose, which is that luck is morally relevant.

The problem is not merely academic. It sits at the foundation of how criminal justice systems operate, how we think about inequality and poverty, how we evaluate our own lives and how genuinely fair a society committed to merit and individual responsibility can actually be. Working through its implications carefully produces conclusions that many people find deeply uncomfortable, not because the conclusions are wrong but because they are right.

Nagel's Four Types of Moral Luck

Nagel distinguished four categories of moral luck, each of which poses the problem from a different angle. Resultant luck is the type illustrated by the two drivers: the same action produces different moral assessments depending on outcomes that neither agent controlled. Circumstantial luck concerns the situations people find themselves in through no choice of their own. The person who was never tested by the specific circumstances that would have revealed a moral failing is morally fortunate in a way that is not fully to their credit. The German who came of age in the 1930s and the German who came of age in the 1970s faced radically different moral circumstances, and the second person's cleaner moral record reflects this difference in circumstance as much as it reflects any difference in character.

Constitutive luck is perhaps the most radical category. It refers to the luck involved in who you are, including your dispositions, inclinations, capacities and temperament. If you are fortunate enough to have been born with the genetic endowment, the early childhood environment and the neurological development that produce a person inclined toward empathy, impulse control, honesty and long-term thinking, your good character is not entirely to your credit. You did not choose these characteristics; they were given to you by a combination of genetics and early experience that you had no role in determining. The person who struggles with addiction, who has difficulty controlling their anger or who finds honesty effortful is not necessarily worse in any character-independent sense; they may simply have been less constitutively fortunate.

Causal luck concerns the degree to which one's actions are determined by causes outside oneself. This category shades into the free will problem discussed in a later article in this series, and Nagel was careful to note that it creates the deepest philosophical difficulty, because if determinism is true, or even if our actions are causally influenced by factors outside our control to any significant degree, the scope of genuine moral responsibility shrinks dramatically.

The Control Principle and Why We Ignore It

Nagel identified what he called the control principle as the intuitive basis for our ordinary moral thinking: people are morally assessable only for what is within their control. This principle seems unimpeachable. We do not blame people for being short, for having congenital conditions or for accidents they genuinely could not have avoided. The control principle is embedded in the criminal law doctrine of mens rea, the requirement of a guilty mind: genuine criminal responsibility requires that the harmful act was intentional, knowing or at least negligent, not purely accidental.

And yet our actual moral practices routinely and systematically violate the control principle in the ways that the moral luck examples illustrate. The driver who struck the child is prosecuted; the driver who didn't is not. The soldier who committed atrocities under orders faces a lifetime of moral judgment; the soldier whose unit was posted to a quiet sector does not face that test. The ambitious young executive who rose to the top during the right economic conditions is celebrated; the equally talented and hardworking person who entered the workforce during a recession is not. In each case the moral difference between the two individuals substantially reflects luck rather than character or choice, but our judgments treat the outcomes as character revelations.

Why do we do this? One explanation is epistemic: we cannot directly observe character, intentions or the counterfactual worlds in which different outcomes occurred, so we use outcomes as proxies for underlying character even though we know the proxy is imperfect. Another explanation is practical: holding people responsible for outcomes, even luck-influenced ones, creates incentives for caution and care that produce better aggregate social outcomes, regardless of whether the resulting individual judgments are always fair. A third explanation is psychological: outcomes produce emotional responses in us, particularly when they involve harm to others, that generate attribution regardless of what the rational accounting would say.

Moral Luck and the Meritocracy Myth

The implications of constitutive and circumstantial luck for the ideology of meritocracy are radical and uncomfortable for anyone who has invested substantially in that framework. Meritocracy, in its common usage, refers to a system that distributes rewards according to individual talent and effort. Its appeal is as a framework for legitimate inequality: if some people have more than others, the meritocratic justification is that they earned it through qualities and choices that are genuinely their own.

But if constitutive luck means that your talents are substantially a product of your genetic endowment and early environment, and if circumstantial luck means that your ability to apply those talents is substantially a product of the circumstances you were born into, the meritocratic justification for inequality becomes much thinner than it appears. The person who was born with higher cognitive ability, into a stable and educationally rich family, in a wealthy country, during a period of economic expansion, into a social network that provided professional connections and mentorship, did not earn these advantages. They were lucky. And the distribution of rewards that follows from acting on these advantages reflects, in large part, a distribution of luck rather than a distribution of desert.

Philosopher John Rawls, whose 1971 book "A Theory of Justice" remains the most influential work of political philosophy of the twentieth century, built his entire theory of distributive justice on something very close to the moral luck insight. Rawls argued that the distribution of natural talents was "arbitrary from a moral point of view," and that a just society would not allow these morally arbitrary factors to determine the distribution of social goods without significant redistribution. His famous "veil of ignorance" thought experiment, in which you design a society without knowing your own place in it, is designed precisely to bracket the luck of one's actual position and ask what principles of distribution could be justified to everyone regardless of where they end up.

Criminal Justice and the Luck Problem

The criminal justice system confronts the moral luck problem most acutely in its treatment of attempted versus completed crimes. The person who attempts murder and succeeds faces a dramatically harsher sentence than the person who attempts murder and fails because the victim survived or the weapon misfired. The additional harm that triggers the harsher sentence is, in many cases, entirely a matter of luck. From the perspective of the would-be murderer's character, culpability and moral desert, there is no obvious reason why the accidental effectiveness of their action should dramatically change the punishment they receive.

Legal philosopher Michael Moore has argued that outcome-sensitivity in criminal sentencing is actually morally appropriate because criminal law is not purely an instrument for expressing moral desert but also an instrument for making the defendant "square" with their victims, and the gravity of what must be squared depends on what actually happened. This is a coherent position but it does not resolve the tension with the control principle; it reframes it as a different purpose for criminal law rather than eliminating the gap between our punishment practices and any purely desert-based justification for them.

The deeper criminal justice application of moral luck concerns the circumstances that produce criminal behavior in the first place. Research on the developmental antecedents of criminal behavior, including adverse childhood experiences, poverty, exposure to violence, parental incarceration, educational deprivation and neighborhood effects, consistently finds that a very large proportion of the individual characteristics that lead to criminal behavior are products of circumstances that the individual did not choose. The constitutive luck critique, applied to criminal justice, produces an argument that the current system of punishment is substantially a system for punishing people for having been unlucky in their circumstances of formation. This argument is not that crime should be unpunished or that individual responsibility is entirely a fiction; it is that the degree of individual moral blame we typically attribute to criminal behavior is systematically inflated by our tendency to discount the luck involved in the formation of the character that produced it.

The specific decision they made that put them inside could have been made by hundreds of people in similar circumstances. Living With the Problem

Nagel concluded his original paper with the observation that moral luck creates a genuine antinomy: an apparent contradiction between the control principle, which seems rationally inescapable, and our actual moral practices, which violate it so comprehensively that abandoning them entirely would require rebuilding moral life from scratch. He did not resolve this antinomy, and neither has anyone since. It remains one of the genuinely open problems of moral philosophy.

But acknowledging the problem, even without resolving it, changes something important. It produces a humility about one's own moral achievements: the recognition that the good person one has managed to become is substantially the product of fortunate circumstances that one did not earn. It produces a different orientation toward the failures of others: the recognition that the circumstances that produced their failures may have been very different from the circumstances that produced one's own relative successes. And it produces a different view of inequality: the recognition that the distribution of social outcomes reflects, in very large part, a distribution of luck that has no independent moral authority.

Bernard Williams argued that the recognition of moral luck should lead to what he called "agent-regret," a form of appropriate sorrow for what one has caused even when one could not have fully controlled it. The driver who struck the child owes something to the victim that the driver who didn't strike anyone does not owe, not purely because they are more blameworthy but because they are causally connected to harm in a way that the other driver is not. This is a more nuanced position than either "you are fully responsible for everything you cause" or "you are responsible only for what you fully control," and it may be the most honest position available in a moral life that has to contend with a world that distributes circumstances, capacities and outcomes in ways that no one chose.
