Free Will, Determinism and What We Actually Mean by Responsibility

13 min read • Ethics & Philosophy
▲ 94,847 found this mind-bending

In 1983, the neuroscientist Benjamin Libet published an experiment that has unsettled philosophers and cognitive scientists ever since. Participants were asked to flex their wrist whenever they felt like it and to note the position of a clock hand at the moment they became aware of the urge to move. Meanwhile, EEG electrodes measured electrical activity in their brains. What Libet found was that a brain signal associated with preparing for voluntary movement, called the readiness potential, began building around 550 milliseconds before the movement occurred. But participants reported becoming consciously aware of their intention to move only about 200 milliseconds before the movement. The brain, it appeared, was initiating the action about 350 milliseconds before the person consciously "decided" to act. The decision seemed to follow the neural preparation rather than cause it.

This experiment became a touchstone in popular discussions of free will, often cited as evidence that conscious decision-making is an illusion, a story we tell ourselves after the fact to explain choices that were already determined by unconscious neural processes. The neuroscientist and writer Sam Harris has argued in his 2012 book "Free Will" that this and related evidence establishes that the kind of free will most people believe they have, the sense of being an uncaused cause, the originator of one's own choices, does not exist. The physicist and writer Brian Greene, in "The Fabric of the Cosmos" and related work, makes a similar argument from physics: if the laws of nature are deterministic (or, adding quantum mechanics, probabilistic but not responsive to conscious choice), then the entire future of the universe including every human decision is fixed by the prior state of matter and energy. In neither case is there room for the kind of free will that grounds the moral intuition that people truly deserve punishment or reward for what they do.

"Reading about Libet's experiment at 19 sent me into a genuine existential crisis for about six months. The thing that eventually pulled me out wasn't a philosophical argument — it was realizing that I was still deliberating, still experiencing myself as choosing, and that this experience itself needed explaining even if the metaphysics of will was messy." r/philosophy • 21,493 upvotes

The philosophical stakes of this question are unusually high. The concepts of moral praise and blame, desert, punishment, gratitude and resentment all seem to presuppose that people could have done otherwise than they did. If they could not, if every action was the inevitable output of prior causes stretching back through the agent's history and the entire history of the universe, then the entire apparatus of moral responsibility seems to be built on a mistake. Criminal punishment in particular becomes philosophically problematic: if a person who commits a violent act could not have done otherwise given their brain state, genetics, upbringing and circumstances, then punishing them seems more like kicking a malfunctioning machine than like a morally justified response to genuine wrongdoing.

Three Positions and Their Problems

Philosophers have staked out three main positions on the relationship between determinism and free will. Hard determinists (or, in a version incorporating quantum indeterminacy, hard incompatibilists) argue that determinism is true, free will requires the ability to have done otherwise in the same circumstances, determinism excludes that ability, and therefore free will does not exist. This is the position Harris defends and it is philosophically coherent, but it has implications that most people find difficult to accept: that no one has ever deserved anything, that gratitude and resentment are systematically misdirected emotional responses and that the entire edifice of criminal justice premised on desert needs to be rebuilt from scratch.

Libertarians about free will (a technical philosophical term with no connection to the political movement) hold that free will does exist and is incompatible with determinism, but that the universe is not fully deterministic in the relevant way. Some invoke quantum indeterminacy as the gap in which free will operates. Others argue for forms of agent causation in which persons as unified entities can initiate causal chains not fully determined by prior physical states. The physicist and philosopher Roger Penrose has controversially proposed that quantum processes in neural microtubules might be relevant to consciousness and will, a position sharply criticized by mainstream neuroscience. The libertarian position preserves free will but requires either finding a gap in physical causation or postulating a kind of agent causation that is difficult to reconcile with the causal closure of the physical world that modern science presupposes.

Compatibilists, who include many of the most influential contemporary philosophers including Daniel Dennett, P.F. Strawson, Susan Wolf and John Martin Fischer, argue that the whole debate between the first two camps rests on a mistaken understanding of what free will actually requires. On the compatibilist view, "free will" does not and never did mean the ability to have done otherwise in an absolute metaphysical sense. What it means, and what matters for moral responsibility, is the ability to act in accordance with one's own reasons, values and desires without external compulsion or internal dysfunction. A person who acts freely in this sense is one whose action flows from their own reasoning and character rather than from physical force, addiction, psychosis, coercion or other bypassing mechanisms. Whether the universe is ultimately deterministic is irrelevant to this kind of freedom, because this kind of freedom was never about being uncaused. It was always about the right kind of causation.

P.F. Strawson and the Reactive Attitudes

The most influential single paper in the contemporary free will debate is probably P.F. Strawson's 1962 essay "Freedom and Resentment," which approached the question not through metaphysics but through reflection on ordinary moral psychology. Strawson observed that human moral life is structured by what he called "reactive attitudes": emotions like resentment, indignation, gratitude, love, hurt feelings and moral approval that are responses to the perceived quality of will behind others' actions. We resent someone who steps on our foot intentionally in a way we do not resent someone who steps on it accidentally, because resentment is a response to what the action reveals about the other person's regard for us rather than merely to the physical event.

Strawson argued that these reactive attitudes are not optional features of human life that could be discarded if determinism turned out to be true. They are constitutive of human relationships and of what it means to take oneself and others seriously as persons. The "objective stance," in which we view another person purely as a causal mechanism to be managed or treated rather than engaged with as a moral agent, is appropriate in specific circumstances: when someone is severely mentally ill, when they are very young, when they are in the grip of an overwhelming compulsion. But the objective stance, adopted universally because of abstract theoretical considerations about determinism, would represent the dissolution of human relationships as we know them. Strawson's argument was that the metaphysics of determinism, whatever its ultimate truth, cannot touch the human reality of reactive attitudes, and that this reality is itself what grounds our practices of responsibility.

"Strawson's paper is the one I assign when students come in convinced that free will is obviously an illusion and therefore nothing matters. It doesn't prove free will exists in any metaphysically robust sense. It asks: given that resentment, gratitude and moral regard are real features of your actual life, what exactly are you proposing to replace them with, and why would that be better?" r/academicphilosophy • 11,293 upvotes

The Libet Experiment Revisited

The popular interpretation of Libet's experiment as proof that conscious will is an illusion has been challenged substantially in subsequent decades. Several methodological problems have been identified, including the difficulty of retrospectively reporting the moment of becoming aware of an urge, the fact that the "readiness potential" may represent neural noise or preparation rather than a specific decision signal, and the finding by Aaron Schurger and colleagues in 2012 that the readiness potential Libet measured may be an artifact of averaging brain signals over many trials rather than a real precursor of individual decisions. More recent neuroimaging research using fMRI has produced more nuanced results, with some studies suggesting that while some information about a decision can be decoded from brain activity before conscious awareness, the conscious deliberation that occurs afterward still influences the final outcome.

Dennett has argued in "Freedom Evolves" (2003) that the Libet results, even taken at face value, do not establish what their popularizers claim. The question of free will is not whether consciousness is the very first neural event in a decision chain, but whether the whole system of deliberation, reflection and value-responsive reasoning that characterizes mature human cognition constitutes a genuine form of self-determination. Even if unconscious processes begin the motor preparation, the question of whether the resulting action reflects the agent's own values and reasoning in a way that makes attributions of responsibility appropriate is not answered by measuring the temporal order of neural events.

Criminal Justice and the Implications of Hard Incompatibilism

The criminal justice implications of the free will debate are genuinely significant. Robert Sapolsky's 2023 book "Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will" makes the most thorough recent case for the view that biological, developmental and social determinants fully explain human behavior in ways that should lead us to dismantle retributive justice entirely. Sapolsky, a neuroendocrinologist, traces the causal antecedents of behavior through neurobiology, hormonal effects, childhood experience, socioeconomic conditions and evolutionary history. His conclusion is that no one deserves punishment in the retributive sense, and that justice systems should be redesigned entirely around incapacitation of the dangerous and rehabilitation of the changeable.

This position has significant appeal, particularly given evidence of systematic biases in how punishment is distributed. Black defendants receive longer sentences than white defendants for equivalent crimes. People convicted of crimes with sympathetic personal histories receive lighter sentences than those without them, even when the legal facts are identical. Mandatory minimum sentencing laws remove judicial discretion in ways that produce manifestly unjust outcomes. If retributive punishment is philosophically unjustifiable anyway, these inequities become simply unjustified harm rather than unjust variations on a justified practice.

But critics of Sapolsky's position, including the philosopher Manuel Vargas and the legal scholar Stephen Morse, argue that the practical implications do not follow straightforwardly from the metaphysics. Even if no one metaphysically "deserves" anything in an ultimate sense, practices of holding people responsible through social sanctioning and consequences still serve important functions: they provide reasons for potential wrongdoers to deliberate before acting, they express the community's values and they maintain the social norms on which cooperation depends. A purely consequentialist justice system that jails people not for what they did but for what they are predicted to do in the future raises its own severe civil liberties problems. The question of how to structure a justice system given deep uncertainty about the metaphysics of free will is not answered simply by establishing that retributivism is hard to ground philosophically.

What Actually Changes If You Accept the Hard View

The philosopher Derk Pereboom, who defends "hard incompatibilism" more carefully than most, has argued that the practical implications are more modest than either enthusiasts or critics of determinism typically claim. We can still quarantine dangerous people to protect potential victims, in roughly the way we quarantine people with infectious diseases, without that practice requiring retributive justification. We can still engage in moral criticism and moral education, since even determined beings can change in response to reasons. We can still feel and express attitudes like disappointment, concern and moral disapproval, even if not resentment in the blame-laden sense. What we should give up, on Pereboom's view, is the emotional satisfaction of believing that suffering imposed on wrongdoers is deserved and just rather than merely necessary and regrettable.

Whether most people could actually sustain this psychological transformation is a different question. When someone you love is murdered, the response that their killer was a deterministic machine doing what their neurobiology and history made inevitable is philosophically defensible but experientially nearly impossible. Strawson's point about reactive attitudes being constitutive of personhood is relevant here: the question may not be whether the hard determinist view is true, but whether it is livable, and whether the asymmetry between its theoretical truth and practical inaccessibility itself tells us something important about the kind of creatures we are and the kind of moral frameworks appropriate for creatures like us.

The most honest position at the end of this argument is probably something like: the metaphysics of free will is genuinely uncertain, the popular understanding of free will as an uncaused cause is almost certainly wrong, the concept of moral responsibility does not require that strong a metaphysical foundation to be meaningful, and the practical conclusion most warranted by what we know is not nihilism about responsibility but a significant reduction in the confidence with which we impose the harshest punishments on those least privileged by the circumstances that shaped them. The universe may or may not be deterministic. But the brute fact that where you are born, to whom, in what circumstances, with what neurobiology, shapes who you become in ways you did not choose is real regardless of how the metaphysics resolves. Humility about luck, both good and bad, is warranted even before the free will question is answered.
