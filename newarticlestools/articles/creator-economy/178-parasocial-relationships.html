The Parasocial Trap: When One-Sided Relationships Feel Real

12 min read  |  Creator Economy  |  Psychology of Media
93,847 upvotes

You feel like you know them. You know their dog's name, you remember the story about their difficult childhood, you were there for the breakup — or at least you watched the tearful video about it the morning after. When they go through something hard you feel a genuine pang of concern, and when something great happens in their life you feel something close to pride. You have never exchanged a single word with this person. They do not know you exist. And yet the feeling in your chest is real.

This is the parasocial relationship, one of the stranger products of mass media and one that the internet has turbocharged into something the researchers who first named it could barely have imagined. The word sounds clinical and slightly accusatory, and many people bristle at having their attachments described that way. But understanding what parasocial bonds actually are, why human brains form them so readily and what the platforms and creators do with them once they exist is not about pathologizing fandom. It is about being honest with yourself about a dynamic that has been engineered to function in ways that do not always serve your interests.

Where the Idea Came From

The term was coined in 1956 by sociologists Donald Horton and Richard Wohl in a paper published in the journal Psychiatry titled "Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction." Horton and Wohl were watching early American television and noticing something curious about how audiences related to performers. Hosts like Arthur Godfrey and Dave Garroway spoke directly into the camera, used colloquial language, acknowledged the viewer's domestic situation, and created what Horton and Wohl called the "illusion of intimacy." Viewers began discussing these performers the way they discussed people in their actual social circles, worrying about them, defending them in conversation, feeling personally slighted when they behaved badly.

Horton and Wohl were careful not to treat this as a disorder. They noted that the capacity to form such relationships was an extension of completely normal social abilities, not a deficiency. The same cognitive and emotional machinery that helps you read another person's intentions, empathize with their situation and feel genuine warmth toward them operates when you watch a compelling performer on screen. The brain does not come equipped with a separate, degraded processing pathway for mediated relationships. It uses what it has, which turns out to be the same hardware it uses for everyone.

For decades parasocial relationships were studied primarily in the context of soap operas, late-night hosts and sports figures. Then the internet arrived, and with it a fundamental change in the texture of the illusion. The old parasocial relationship was inherently asymmetric in an obvious and acknowledged way. Walter Cronkite did not read your letters on air. Johnny Carson did not follow you back. The distance was legible. What the creator economy has done is introduce an artificial reciprocity that blurs that distance without actually eliminating it.

The Architecture of Manufactured Intimacy

Modern content creators, whether they are conscious of the strategy or simply following incentives, have become extraordinarily skilled at deploying what researchers call parasocial intimacy cues. These are communicative signals that trigger the social cognition system and make a mediated relationship feel more mutual than it is. Looking directly into the camera simulates eye contact, one of the most powerful triggers of social bonding in primates. Addressing the audience as "you" and speaking in the second person creates the linguistic structure of a direct conversation. Self-disclosure, the sharing of personal information, accelerates the perceived depth of a relationship because in ordinary social life deeper disclosure typically indicates that trust has been established.

The YouTuber or podcaster who tells you about their anxiety, their complicated relationship with a parent, the thing that happened in middle school that still bothers them, is activating social norms that govern real relationships. When someone discloses something personal to you in real life, you typically feel that a bond has been established, that you now know each other better, that there is a small obligation of reciprocal trust. Your brain does not automatically generate an asterisk noting that this disclosure was made simultaneously to several hundred thousand people and was likely planned, scripted or at least strategically shaped.

"I've watched this one creator for like four years. I know his whole family, his history, his values. When he got married I cried watching the video. Then I realized I've never once been in the same room as this person and they have no idea I exist. It was such a strange feeling to sit with." r/youtube  |  31,847 upvotes

The comment section and the reply function add another layer. A creator who reads a comment and responds, even once, has introduced the possibility of actual reciprocity into a relationship that otherwise has none. For the millions of viewers who never receive a response, the visible evidence that responses sometimes happen is psychologically significant. The relationship now contains the possibility of acknowledgment even if acknowledgment never arrives. This is structurally similar to what psychologists call a variable ratio reinforcement schedule, the same mechanism that makes slot machines so difficult to stop playing. The occasional reward is more motivating, and more habit-forming, than a consistent reward would be.

Memberships, Patreon tiers, Discord servers and live streams with super chats deepen this architecture. They create apparent proximity and the perception of special access. The creator who gives their Patreon subscribers a peek behind the scenes, reads out names during a live stream or responds to member-only comments has not actually established a relationship with those individuals, but has dramatically amplified the sense that one exists. The subscriber is now a participant, or at least feels like one, and the emotional investment that follows is genuine even if the reciprocity is largely theater.

What Parasocial Relationships Actually Do for People

It would be easy to write a purely critical account of parasocial relationships as manipulation or delusion, but that would be incomplete and somewhat condescending. People form these bonds because they deliver real psychological benefits, and the research is fairly clear on that point.

The most robust finding in the literature is that parasocial relationships can partially compensate for deficits in social connection. Studies conducted by researchers including Sherry Corb Stever, Jaye Derrick and Gabriel and Gardner have found that people who feel lonely or socially excluded show measurable psychological relief after parasocial engagement. In a series of studies published between 2008 and 2012, Derrick and colleagues found that people used parasocial relationships as a form of social snacking, a way of sating some of the need for belonging when actual social interaction was unavailable. After a social rejection, watching a familiar television character reduced negative affect more effectively than watching an unfamiliar one, which suggests the effect depended on the established relationship rather than simply on distraction.

For people with social anxiety, autism, depression or any condition that makes initiating and maintaining real relationships difficult, parasocial bonds can provide a low-stakes environment for practising the feelings of connection without the cognitive and emotional demands of mutuality. You can engage at your own pace, retreat whenever you need to and return without having caused any offence. The intimacy cues that the creator deploys do not ask you to respond with intimacy of your own. They offer the warmth without the vulnerability.

There is also a genuine identity function. Parasocial relationships with creators or characters who model certain ways of being in the world can be important to the formation of values, aesthetic sensibility and self-understanding, particularly during adolescence. The teenager who comes out to themselves partly through the experience of following a queer creator, or who discovers a passion for science through years of watching an enthusiastic communicator explain the world, has been shaped by that parasocial relationship in ways that are not trivial or delusional. People learn from the figures they observe closely, regardless of whether those figures know they are watching.

"I have social anxiety that's been really bad at different points in my life. During one of those stretches I had a podcast I listened to every day that made me feel like I was spending time with people who got me. I know that sounds pathetic but it genuinely kept me functional when real social stuff felt impossible." r/socialanxiety  |  44,293 upvotes

When the Bond Distorts

The psychological literature on parasocial relationships distinguishes between what researchers call functional parasocial engagement and what might be described as parasocial entanglement. The former is flexible, held lightly, compatible with and supplementary to real relationships. The latter is rigid, demands that the creator remain consistent with the imagined relationship, and can crowd out real connection rather than supplement it.

One of the most reliably painful experiences in parasocial relationships is what has been called parasocial breakup, a term coined by researchers in the early 2000s and studied more extensively since. When a creator stops making content, changes significantly, behaves badly or is revealed to be different from the persona the audience believed they knew, the resulting distress is measurably similar in structure to the distress of a real relationship ending. Studies by Eyal and Cohen published in 2006 found that parasocial breakup produced grief responses including sadness, anger and rumination, and that the intensity of the grief correlated with the strength of the original parasocial bond. People did not simply feel disappointed that a show they liked was cancelled. They felt something closer to loss.

This dynamic plays out visibly in public every time a prominent creator is exposed as having concealed an important aspect of their personality or history. The audience reaction is rarely "I was deceived about someone I never actually knew." It is typically far more personal, closer to betrayal, which reveals how fully the brain had processed the relationship as if it were real. The logical response and the emotional response diverge sharply because the emotional processing happened in systems that do not have access to the meta-level knowledge that the relationship was mediated and curated.

There is also a documented tendency for strong parasocial relationships to produce a sense of ownership over the creator. Audiences who have followed someone for years, who feel that they were there from the beginning, who have supported through memberships and merchandise, sometimes develop what researchers have described as parasocial possessiveness. The creator's personal life choices, creative decisions and professional moves become subject to audience approval in a way that the audience experiences as legitimate. The sense that "they owe their success to us" is not entirely unfounded as an economic observation, but it carries into territory that is psychologically unhealthy for both parties. Fan entitlement, parasocial possessiveness and the toxicity that sometimes characterizes fandom communities are downstream of the same mechanism that makes parasocial bonds useful in the first place.

The Celebrity Worship Scale and Clinical Concern

Psychologists John Maltby and Lynn McCutcheon developed the Celebrity Attitude Scale in the early 2000s as a research instrument for studying the full range of parasocial engagement with public figures. Their work identified three levels that they described as entertainment-social (following a celebrity's life because it is fun and interesting to discuss with others), intense-personal (stronger feelings of connection, thinking about the celebrity often, feeling that a special bond exists) and borderline-pathological (taking extreme actions for the celebrity, believing the celebrity knows you, inability to distinguish the parasocial relationship from reality).

The first level is extremely common and most of the research suggests it is essentially benign. The second level shades into territory that correlates, in Maltby and McCutcheon's research, with higher anxiety, depression and dissociation scores, though the causal direction is unclear. People who are already struggling with mental health may be more drawn to intense parasocial engagement, or intense engagement may exacerbate existing difficulties, or both may be true. The third level is rare but clinical in its profile, overlapping with conditions like erotomania and delusional disorder.

It is worth being clear that the vast majority of parasocial engagement, even when it feels very strong and personal, stays well within the first two levels and does not approach clinical concern. The point of the scale is not to suggest that caring about a creator is a warning sign, but to acknowledge that the same basic mechanisms exist on a continuum and that understanding where you are on that continuum is useful self-knowledge.

At some point I had to ask myself why someone I've never met had that kind of power over my emotional state. Turns out I was going through a period where I had basically no other consistent social contact." r/self  |  28,591 upvotes

How Platforms Monetize the Bond

Understanding parasocial relationships without understanding how they are monetized misses the most important dimension of the phenomenon in the current media environment. The intimacy that audiences feel for creators is not an incidental byproduct of the content; it is the product. The parasocial bond is the mechanism through which audience attention is converted into revenue, and the entire architecture of recommendation algorithms, engagement metrics and monetization tools has been designed to cultivate and harvest it.

The more deeply a viewer is parasocially bonded to a creator, the longer they watch, the more reliably they return, the more likely they are to click on merchandise links, to subscribe to membership tiers, to buy sponsored products and to defend the creator against criticism. Every one of these behaviors is economically valuable, and the measurability of digital engagement means that platforms have been able to identify with precision which content formats, which communicative styles and which intimacy cues drive the most of each behavior. The result is a feedback loop in which creators who successfully trigger parasocial bonding receive algorithmic rewards that expand their reach, which in turn demonstrates to other creators what works, gradually homogenizing the style of a very large amount of content toward maximally intimate self-disclosure.

Sponsored content sits at the intersection of parasocial trust and commercial transaction in a way that has attracted significant regulatory attention. When a creator you feel you know personally recommends a product, the recommendation is processed through a different cognitive framework than a billboard or a television commercial. It activates the social trust heuristic rather than the advertising skepticism heuristic. Research conducted by Campbell and colleagues published in the Journal of Marketing found that influencer recommendations receive significantly higher credibility ratings than traditional advertising from the same source, even when consumers are aware that the influencer has been paid. The awareness that it is an advertisement does not fully neutralize the parasocial trust effect.

This is why influencer marketing has grown from a minor novelty to a multi-billion dollar industry. Business Insider Intelligence estimated global influencer marketing spending at $15 billion in 2022, up from $1.7 billion in 2016. The FTC's 2023 updated endorsement guidelines, which require clear disclosure of material connections and extend to de facto endorsements and AI-generated content, represent an attempt to restore consumer protection in a space where the standard advertising literacy frameworks no longer fully apply. The effectiveness of those disclosures is debated; research suggests that the parasocial trust effect persists even when disclosure is present and clearly understood.

The Merch Table and Beyond

Merchandise has always been part of the celebrity economy, but the parasocial mechanics of the creator economy have given it a particular character. When a creator sells a hoodie or a mug or a skincare product, the purchase is rarely purely utilitarian. It is partly an act of affiliation, a way of expressing the relationship and making something that is essentially imaginary visible and material. The buyer is not just buying an object; they are buying a talisman of the connection.

This is not inherently exploitative. People have always made purchases partly for their social and symbolic meaning, and there is nothing wrong with wanting to support a creator you genuinely appreciate. The concern arises when creators or their management teams systematically optimize merchandise and product lines to maximize the conversion of parasocial attachment into purchase behavior, using techniques that exploit the emotional intensity of the bond rather than simply offering something genuinely useful or appealing. The $500 exclusive meet-and-greet package, the limited-edition drop designed to create artificial scarcity and urgency, the premium community tier that promises closeness while delivering a slightly larger Discord server, these are all exercises in monetizing parasocial attachment at its most emotionally charged.

The most troubling version of this dynamic involves parasocial relationships with public figures who are themselves struggling or in crisis. The creator who shares mental health struggles in detail, documents a difficult personal situation over many months or builds a narrative around ongoing vulnerability is accessing the deepest registers of audience empathy. The parasocial bond that forms around shared suffering is particularly intense. When that vulnerability is then monetized through donations, membership drives or merchandise campaigns, the ethical lines become genuinely difficult to draw. The creator may be sincerely sharing their genuine experience, and the audience's desire to help may be genuinely felt, but the commercial infrastructure converts compassion into revenue in ways that should at minimum prompt honest reflection.

Fictional Characters and the Parasocial Dimension

Parasocial relationships are not limited to real people. The same mechanisms operate with fictional characters, and in some ways the fictional version raises fewer ethical concerns because it is more obviously one-directional. You know Hermione Granger is not going to be hurt if you put down the book. The relationship is structurally identical to the parasocial bond with a real creator, but the fictional character cannot be exploited by their own fan base and cannot exploit their fans in return.

The phenomenon of character attachment has been extensively studied and is closely related to what literary scholars call narrative transportation, the cognitive and emotional absorption in a story world that temporarily displaces attention from external reality. Research by Green and Brock, and later by Mar and Oatley, found that narrative transportation produces measurable changes in beliefs, attitudes and empathy, and that these effects persist after the story ends. Characters who are well-constructed function as social partners for the cognitive systems involved in theory of mind and social cognition. Reading about another person's inner life, whether real or fictional, exercises the same mental muscles as navigating actual social relationships.

The intense grief that audiences sometimes feel at the end of a long-running series, the resistance to series finales, the disorientation of saying goodbye to characters who have been present in one's life for years, these are not trivial or embarrassing reactions. They reflect the genuine psychological reality of the bond that was formed. What is worth noting is that the most commercially successful fictional universes in the current media landscape have been explicitly designed to cultivate and extend this attachment indefinitely, through sequels, prequels, spinoffs and extended universe content, because the commercial value of the parasocial bond with fictional characters is just as real as with living creators.

The Creator's Perspective

Parasocial relationships are not only a phenomenon experienced by audiences. Creators live inside the asymmetry from the other direction, and that position has its own distinctive psychological pressures that received sustained attention in the previous article on creator burnout.

The creator who is deeply aware of the parasocial bond their audience has formed with them is in a peculiar position. They feel, or at least observe, the emotional investment of hundreds of thousands of people. They receive messages that describe their content as life-saving, that attribute major life decisions to their influence, that express love in terms that far exceed what the actual relationship warrants. They know the persona their audience is attached to and they know how much of that persona is curated or constructed. The gap between how they are perceived and how they actually experience themselves can be vertiginous.

Many creators describe a specific kind of loneliness that comes with parasocial fame: surrounded by apparent intimacy and attachment while experiencing profound isolation, because none of the thousands of people who feel they know you actually do, and the people who actually know you are often being displaced or strained by the demands of maintaining the parasocial relationship with everyone else. The YouTuber who says "I have millions of subscribers and no real friends" is describing something that many creators recognize.

There is also a manipulation trap that creators must navigate honestly. The more a creator deploys intimacy cues, the more they share personal information, the more they construct a narrative of closeness with the audience, the stronger the parasocial bond becomes and the better the commercial outcomes typically are. This creates an incentive structure that rewards the performance of authenticity regardless of whether genuine authenticity is present. The creator who is most effective at making you feel you know them is not necessarily the most honest; they may simply be the most skilled at operating the levers of parasocial bonding.

What Honest Self-Examination Looks Like

None of this analysis is meant to suggest that you should feel ashamed of the parasocial bonds you have formed or that you should try to sever them. The goal is something more modest and more useful: developing enough self-awareness to understand what these relationships are doing for you, what they might be costing you and where the line sits between a psychologically healthy attachment and one that is beginning to function as a substitute for something you actually need.

A few useful questions are worth sitting with. Does your interest in a creator or character feel like an addition to your social and emotional life, something that enriches a foundation of real connection, or does it increasingly feel like a replacement for real connection that feels too difficult or costly to pursue? Are you able to hold the relationship lightly, to maintain enjoyment while also maintaining the awareness that it is one-sided, or do you find yourself resisting that awareness and reaching for a sense of the relationship as more mutual than it is? When the creator does something you dislike or disagree with, do you respond with the kind of mild disappointment appropriate to losing interest in someone's content, or with something more like personal betrayal?

The capacity to enjoy parasocial engagement while maintaining accurate knowledge of what it is represents a kind of media literacy that is genuinely worth cultivating. It allows you to get the real benefits, the sense of companionship, the learning, the entertainment, the sense of finding people who share your values and aesthetic sensibility, without being as vulnerable to the ways these bonds can be monetized, manipulated and used against your better judgment.

Horton and Wohl, writing in 1956, concluded their original paper with a note that remains relevant. They observed that the parasocial relationship is not itself a problem. The problem arises when it is systematically cultivated for purposes other than the wellbeing of the person experiencing it. The media landscape they were describing in 1956 was, by any measure, a far less sophisticated machine for engineering that cultivation than what we navigate today. The principle they identified, though, has not changed: the bond itself is human and natural; what matters is who benefits from it.
