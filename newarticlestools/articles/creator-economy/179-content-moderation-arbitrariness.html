The Arbitrary Gavel: How Content Moderation Fails Everyone

13 min read  |  Creator Economy  |  Platform Governance
87,293 upvotes

A Palestinian journalist documents a bombing from her apartment building. The video is removed within hours. Across the platform, footage of equally graphic violence from a different conflict remains up for months before anyone notices. A breast cancer survivor posts a post-mastectomy photograph to raise awareness. Her account is suspended. Somewhere else on the same platform, accounts selling counterfeit goods and coordinating harassment campaigns operate without interruption for years. A climate scientist's peer-reviewed thread is flagged as misinformation because an automated system matched a phrase in it against a database of known hoaxes. The actual hoax post it resembled stays up and accumulates shares.

These are not cherry-picked anomalies. They are the normal operating condition of content moderation at scale on the major platforms, and the people who have studied the system most closely, including the former employees who built it, are nearly unanimous on this point: the system is arbitrary, inconsistent, structurally biased in predictable directions and largely unaccountable to anyone it harms. The question is not whether content moderation is broken. The question is whether it could be fixed, and what fixing it would actually require.

The Scale Problem Is Real but It Is Not the Whole Story

The first line of defense for platform companies when moderation failures are raised is the scale argument. Facebook processes more than 100 billion messages per day. YouTube sees roughly 500 hours of video uploaded every minute. TikTok serves content in over 150 countries and 75 languages. No human review system could evaluate all of this content in anything close to real time, so automation is necessary, and automation makes mistakes. This is true as far as it goes, and it is worth acknowledging before moving on.

But the scale argument is frequently invoked to deflect responsibility for problems that are not primarily problems of scale. The inconsistency in how rules are applied to powerful accounts versus ordinary users is not a volume problem. The tendency to suppress legitimate speech about marginalized communities while allowing hateful speech targeting those communities is not purely a machine-learning error. The opacity of the appeals process, the absence of meaningful recourse and the business incentives that shape which errors the system is designed to minimize, these are choices, not inevitabilities forced upon companies by the sheer number of posts to be reviewed.

The scholars who study this most rigorously, including Sarah Roberts at UCLA, whose 2019 book "Behind the Screen" was based on extensive fieldwork with commercial content moderators, and Tarleton Gillespie at Microsoft Research, whose 2018 book "Custodians of the Internet" remains the most thorough institutional analysis available, both emphasize that moderation is a fundamentally political activity dressed up in neutral-sounding procedural language. Every rule about what is allowed and what is prohibited embodies a particular set of values. Every decision about which harms to prioritize and which errors to tolerate reflects a distribution of power. Pretending that these are technical problems amenable to technical solutions obscures the degree to which they are questions about who decides what speech is legitimate.

The Human Cost Behind the Algorithm

Content moderation at scale is not actually performed primarily by algorithms. The algorithms make initial decisions, but the enormous volume of borderline content, appeals and complex cases is reviewed by human moderators, most of whom are contract workers employed by third-party vendors operating in the Philippines, Kenya, India and other countries where labor costs are a fraction of what they would be in Silicon Valley. Facebook's largest moderation operations are run by companies including Accenture, Cognizant and Teleperformance.

Roberts's research documented working conditions that have since been corroborated by investigative reporting from The Verge, Time and other outlets. Moderators are typically required to review hundreds of pieces of content per hour, leaving seconds per item for decisions about material that is sometimes extremely disturbing. They review beheadings, child abuse imagery, graphic torture, graphic self-harm and suicide, and they do this repeatedly, for hours a day, with inadequate psychological support. Studies conducted by researchers at the University of Arizona found measurable PTSD symptoms in a significant percentage of moderators after exposure to graphic content at work. In 2020, a group of former Cognizant employees who had moderated content for Facebook filed a class action lawsuit alleging that the company failed to provide adequate mental health support; the case was settled for $52 million.

"I moderated content for about two years. I have never talked publicly about what I reviewed because I'm not allowed to under my NDA. What I can say is that the idea that these decisions are being made with careful deliberation is a fantasy. You're making judgment calls at speed, all day, about things that most people couldn't look at once." r/TrueOffMyChest  |  38,492 upvotes

The conditions under which these decisions are made matter enormously for the quality of moderation outcomes. A worker reviewing their four hundredth item in the last hour, after several hours of graphic content, operating under quotas that penalize slowness and in a system that provides minimal context for many decisions, will make different judgments than a thoughtful policy expert reviewing the same content with time for reflection. The person harmed by the resulting decision rarely has any way of knowing that the removal of their content happened under these circumstances, much less any recourse that reaches the person who actually made the call.

Automated Systems and Predictable Bias

The automated systems that make the initial moderation decisions on most platforms are trained primarily on English-language content from North American and Western European contexts. This is not incidental. The companies that built these systems are concentrated in these regions, their earliest and largest user bases were in these regions and the labeled training data that teaches automated systems to recognize policy violations was generated primarily in these contexts.

The consequence is that automated systems perform markedly worse at identifying policy violations in other languages and cultural contexts, both in the direction of missing violations that are obvious to native speakers and in the direction of flagging legitimate speech as violations because the system lacks the linguistic and cultural context to interpret it correctly. During the early weeks of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, Facebook's systems were temporarily set to allow calls for violence against Russian soldiers because the company determined that the context justified an exception. The rollout and rollback of that policy produced documented errors in both directions, with content incorrectly allowed and incorrectly suppressed, partly because the systems had not been trained on the relevant contextual distinctions.

The Arabic language moderation problem has been documented in detail by researchers and human rights organizations for years. Human Rights Watch published a 2021 report documenting systematic suppression of Arabic-language content documenting human rights violations in conflict zones in the Middle East and North Africa, while content inciting violence in Arabic against minority communities in the same regions was frequently permitted. The report attributed the disparity partly to inadequate investment in Arabic-language moderation infrastructure relative to the platform's Arabic-speaking user base and partly to structural differences in how the automated systems performed across languages.

The problem is not only linguistic. Moderation systems trained primarily on majority-community speech also tend to flag minority-community speech at higher rates in ways that are not about content but about pattern recognition. Research by Maarten Sap and colleagues published at the ACL conference in 2019 found that automated hate speech detection systems were 1.5 times more likely to flag African American Vernacular English as offensive or hateful compared to standard American English, even when the semantic content was comparable. The system had learned to associate the linguistic patterns of AAVE with policy violations because the training data reflected a world where this speech was disproportionately reviewed and flagged by human moderators who carried the same biases.

Whose Speech Gets Suppressed

The pattern that emerges from the research, from investigative reporting and from systematic documentation by digital rights organizations is fairly consistent: content moderation systems, both automated and human, suppress speech about and by marginalized communities at higher rates than comparable speech about and by majority communities, while simultaneously being less effective at removing content that harms those same communities.

This is not a paradox. It reflects the way that content policy rules are written, trained and enforced in contexts where the people making the decisions are not members of the affected communities and often lack the cultural context to evaluate the speech accurately. A rule against "graphic violence" that is applied to documentation of violence against Black people while not being applied to comparable documentation of violence against white people does not require anyone to be consciously racist. It requires only that the people reviewing content find the first category more disturbing, more graphic or more controversial than the second, which is a function of proximity and representation rather than conscious intention.

LGBTQ content has been systematically suppressed on Instagram and TikTok in ways that have been partially documented through creator reports and confirmed through internal investigations. Instagram acknowledged in 2020 that its systems had been suppressing content with certain LGBTQ-related hashtags as part of its anti-spam and anti-nudity systems. TikTok's internal moderation guidelines, leaked to The Guardian in 2019, revealed instructions to suppress content featuring LGBTQ people, people with disabilities and people classified as "ugly," ostensibly to prevent cyberbullying but with obvious disparate impact on the creators being described.

"I've had accounts documenting discrimination I faced get removed. I've had posts about my own experiences flagged as 'hateful.' Meanwhile the people I was documenting the discrimination from had no issues with their accounts. At some point you stop being surprised and start treating your whole online presence as temporary." r/blacktwitter  |  41,847 upvotes

The Asymmetry of Virality and Enforcement

One of the least-discussed structural problems in content moderation is the relationship between viral spread and enforcement timing. Content moderation systems, particularly automated ones, are primarily reactive rather than predictive. They respond to reports, to viral signals or to pattern matches, which means that a piece of harmful content typically reaches its maximum audience before it is removed. The damage, in the form of harassment, misinformation, radicalization or psychological harm, is done in the period between posting and removal.

At the same time, enforcement often falls most heavily on content that has not yet gone viral, because smaller creators have fewer resources to contest removals and less leverage with the platform, while large accounts with significant audiences have both the ability to escalate removals and, in some cases, informal relationships with platform trust and safety teams that smaller creators do not. The result is a system in which the most-seen content is the least likely to be removed while the least-seen content disproportionately bears the enforcement burden.

Several analyses of Twitter content enforcement before and after the platform's 2022 ownership change documented that accounts with large audiences were systematically less likely to face enforcement action for identical policy violations than accounts with small audiences. This was partly a function of the reporting system (more followers means more people to report violations by others) and partly a function of informal prioritization. The pattern predated the ownership change and reflected structural incentives that exist across all major platforms: high-follower accounts generate more engagement, more ad revenue and more platform prestige, creating implicit pressure toward lighter enforcement.

The Opaque Appeals Process

When content is removed or an account is penalized, the affected creator typically receives a notification that states a policy category was violated and provides no specific information about which content, what specific element of that content triggered the decision or who made the determination. The appeals process, where one exists at all, typically consists of submitting a form that is reviewed by a different (and equally anonymous) member of the moderation team, with no requirement to provide a substantive explanation if the appeal is denied.

This opacity is not accidental. Providing detailed explanations of moderation decisions would be operationally costly and would potentially expose the specific signals the system uses, which could help bad actors evade detection. But the practical consequence for legitimate creators is that there is no meaningful way to understand why a decision was made, no way to identify what specifically needs to change to avoid a recurrence and no avenue for substantive challenge. The process has the formal structure of due process without any of the substance.

Meta established its Oversight Board in 2019 as a purportedly independent body with the authority to reverse Facebook content moderation decisions and issue binding recommendations. The board is composed of academics, jurists, journalists and human rights figures, and its decisions are, in principle, binding on Meta. In practice, the board can review only a tiny fraction of the content decisions made on the platform (it handled fewer than 50 cases in its first full year of operation, against a backdrop of billions of daily decisions), can only review individual content decisions rather than systemic policy questions and has no enforcement mechanism beyond the reputational pressure of public rulings. When the board ruled in 2021 that the suspension of Donald Trump's account after January 6th was justified in principle but that the indefinite nature of the suspension without clear criteria was inconsistent with Meta's policies, the company acknowledged the ruling and changed nothing of substance for months.

Section 230 and the Political Fight Over Liability

The legal framework governing content moderation in the United States is built primarily around Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act of 1996, which provides that interactive computer services cannot be treated as publishers or speakers for content created by third parties. In plain terms, this means that platforms are not legally liable for what their users post, even if the platform was aware of the content and chose not to remove it. This immunity was essential to the development of the early internet and remains the legal foundation on which every major platform's moderation approach is built.

Section 230 has been attacked from both political directions for contradictory reasons. Conservatives have argued that platforms use their moderation powers selectively against conservative voices while hiding behind Section 230's protections; the proposed remedy has been conditioning immunity on viewpoint-neutral moderation, which would effectively require platforms to host content they currently prohibit. Liberals and progressives have argued that platforms use Section 230 to avoid responsibility for the harms their recommendation algorithms cause, particularly in spreading extremist content, health misinformation and content facilitating trafficking; the proposed remedy here has been narrowing immunity for algorithmic amplification even while maintaining it for passive hosting.

The Supreme Court addressed the algorithmic amplification question in Gonzalez v. Google in 2023, which asked whether platforms could be held liable under the Anti-Terrorism Act for algorithmically recommending ISIS content. The Court declined to rule on the Section 230 question directly, instead deciding the case on narrower grounds, leaving the core legal question unsettled. The practical consequence is that the legal framework for platform liability remains essentially unchanged from 1996, applying to a media environment that is almost unrecognizable from the one that framework was designed for.

"The Section 230 debate drives me crazy because both sides are arguing past each other. The platforms need it to exist as they do. Users need some version of it to protect them from liability for sharing things. But the current version has basically zero accountability for predictable harms that the platforms know about and design around." r/technology  |  29,847 upvotes

The Creator's Lived Experience of Moderation

For creators whose livelihoods depend on platform access, the arbitrariness of content moderation is not an abstract policy problem. It is a material threat to their income, their audience relationships and in some cases their ability to do work that has genuine social value. A demonetization event or channel strike does not just affect the individual video flagged; it can suppress distribution across an entire channel, freeze monetization for months and trigger a recalibration of the creator's risk tolerance that pushes their content toward safer, blander territory.

The phenomenon of self-censorship in response to unclear or inconsistently applied rules is well-documented in the research on free expression. When people do not know precisely where the line is, they draw their own line further back from where they think the actual boundary lies, as a margin of safety. Across millions of creators, this effect produces a systematic bias toward the center, a de facto homogenization of content in the direction of whatever seems least likely to attract enforcement attention. The content that never gets made because a creator preemptively decided it was too risky is invisible in any analysis of what was removed, but it represents a real restriction on the range of ideas and experiences the platform surfaces.

The creators who are most affected are disproportionately those whose work is inherently more likely to be flagged by pattern-matching systems or reported by hostile audiences: journalists documenting conflict and human rights violations, educators discussing historically sensitive topics, LGBTQ creators whose existence is itself offensive to a segment of the platform's users who file mass reports, sex educators whose work involves clinical or practical discussion of topics the platform considers sexually explicit. The tools of mass reporting, designed to help communities identify genuine policy violations, function equally well as tools for targeted harassment of minority creators, and platforms have been slow to develop robust defenses against coordinated misuse.

What Better Would Actually Look Like

The critics of content moderation as currently practiced are not, for the most part, arguing for no moderation. Child sexual abuse material, coordinated inauthentic behavior, direct incitement to imminent violence, the case for removing these categories of content is strong and widely shared. The argument is about how the vast grey area is handled and by whom, with what transparency and with what recourse.

Several reform frameworks have been proposed by researchers, digital rights organizations and former platform employees. The most substantive of these involve a set of interconnected changes rather than a single fix. Meaningful transparency would require platforms to publish detailed, regularly audited data about enforcement actions: how many pieces of content were removed by category, language and country, what the appeal rates and outcomes were, and what the error rates were in both directions. The DSA, the EU's Digital Services Act which came into force for very large platforms in August 2023, requires this kind of transparency reporting and makes it enforceable, though the quality of compliance so far has been uneven.

Procedural fairness would require that creators receive specific, actionable information when their content is removed and have access to a meaningful appeals process that includes the possibility of human review by someone with relevant cultural and linguistic context. It would also require that platforms bear some burden of justification when they restrict speech, rather than treating the default of restriction as requiring no explanation. The Oversight Board model represents an attempt in this direction, but its limited scope and lack of enforcement power render it more symbolic than functional as currently structured.

The deeper reform, which the platforms have most consistently resisted, involves some form of external accountability for moderation systems. The current framework allows platforms to make rules, enforce those rules, handle appeals from those rules and report selectively on how well they are following their own rules. Independent auditing of content moderation systems, by bodies with genuine access to the data rather than curated summaries, would provide a basis for holding platforms accountable that does not currently exist. This is broadly analogous to the financial audit model, imperfect as that model is, and the resistance to it from platform companies is itself informative about how confident they are that independent scrutiny would confirm their public accounts.

None of this would make content moderation easy or error-free. The genuinely hard cases would remain genuinely hard, and the scale at which these systems operate would continue to make comprehensive human review impossible. But acknowledging that moderation cannot be perfect is different from accepting that it can be arbitrary, inconsistent and structurally biased without consequence. The people who bear the costs of those failures deserve better than the current system's combination of opacity and indifference.
