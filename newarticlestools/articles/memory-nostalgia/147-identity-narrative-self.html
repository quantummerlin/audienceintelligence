The Story We Tell About Ourselves

68,293 upvotes · by u/narrative_selfhood · 13 min read
▲ 68,293 people found this relatable
Advertisement

At some point in late childhood or early adolescence, something changes in how we relate to our own experience. We begin to understand our lives as stories. Not just sequences of events but connected narratives with themes, turning points, characters who matter, a sense of where things have come from and perhaps where they are going. The question "who are you?" becomes answerable not just with a list of attributes but with a story: here is where I came from, here is what happened to me, here is what I did with it, here is what it means. The story is not simply something we tell to others. It is something we tell to ourselves, continuously, as a primary mechanism for organizing experience into an identity that feels coherent and meaningful.

Dan McAdams at Northwestern University has spent his career studying what he calls "narrative identity" -- the internalized and evolving story of the self that each person constructs from their autobiographical memory. His central claim is that identity in the fullest sense is narrative: we are not just bundles of traits, values and beliefs but authors of personal myths that give our lives purpose, coherence and meaning. The narrative identity framework draws on the work of philosophers including Paul Ricoeur, Charles Taylor and Alasdair MacIntyre, who have argued that human self-understanding is fundamentally temporal and narrative in structure -- that to be a self is to have a story, to understand one's present in terms of one's past and one's anticipated future.

What makes this framework empirically interesting rather than merely philosophical is that the specific qualities of people's life stories have measurable relationships to psychological wellbeing, life satisfaction, resilience and how people respond to adversity. The story you tell about your life is not just a narrative. It is a determinant of how you experience the future moments you haven't lived yet.

The Structure of Personal Myths

McAdams's research identifies several structural features of life narratives that predict psychological outcomes. Narrative tone (whether the story is predominantly positive, hopeful and meaningful or predominantly negative, chaotic and meaningless) is associated with overall life satisfaction and mental health. Narrative coherence (whether the story has an identifiable through-line, causally connected episodes and a sense of the narrator as the agent of their own life) is associated with identity consolidation and psychological maturity. Narrative complexity (whether the story represents experience with appropriate nuance, acknowledging multiple perspectives and holding contradictions) is associated with cognitive flexibility and interpersonal effectiveness.

Among the specific structural features McAdams has studied most extensively, two stand out for their particularly robust associations with wellbeing: contamination sequences and redemption sequences. A contamination sequence is a narrative movement from good to bad: something positive is undermined, ruined or tainted by a subsequent negative development. The afternoon was wonderful until she said that thing. Everything was going well until the diagnosis. A redemption sequence is the reverse: something bad is followed by something good that came from it, or was made possible by it, or was learned through it. The most difficult experience of my life taught me what I would never have learned otherwise. The failure opened a door that the success would have kept closed.

"McAdams's research on contamination vs. redemption sequences hit me differently after a difficult period. I started actually listening to how I talked about hard things in my life and realized my default was contamination framing -- everything was fine until this ruined it. I tried renarrating some of those same events in redemption terms. Not lying about them or pretending they were good, but finding what they actually had made possible. It was uncomfortable and it was useful." r/psychology · 19,847 upvotes

People whose life narratives are characterized by high proportions of redemption sequences show reliably better outcomes on measures of psychological wellbeing, generativity (care for the next generation), life satisfaction and resilience in the face of adversity than those whose narratives feature predominantly contamination sequences. The research does not establish that the narrative causes the wellbeing rather than simply reflecting it -- causality is difficult to establish -- but McAdams argues the relationship is bidirectional: people who have developed redemptive narrative frames are better equipped to find meaning in future adversity, which shapes how they experience and respond to it.

The Narrative Self as Construction

The philosophical and empirical case for narrative identity carries a philosophical implication that many people find both liberating and unsettling: if the self is a story, and if stories are constructed rather than simply discovered, then identity is not fixed. It is authored, continuously revised, and capable of being reauthored. The version of you that appears in your current self-narrative is not simply the objective truth about who you are. It is one possible story that could be told from the available material -- and different stories, emphasizing different events, drawing different causal connections, identifying different themes, would produce a meaningfully different sense of who you are and what is possible for you.

This is not an invitation to self-deception or the fabrication of a life that wasn't lived. The events themselves cannot be changed. But the meaning of those events, the connections between them, the conclusions drawn from them about who you are and what you are capable of -- these are genuinely open to revision in ways that have real consequences for present functioning. The person who understands their pattern of relationship failures as evidence that they are fundamentally unlovable has authored a specific narrative from available material. A different author, working with the same material, might tell a story about someone who had specific learning deficits in early attachment relationships, has been working with limited tools and has specific things to develop -- a story that leads somewhere different. Both stories are constructed. They do not have equal consequences.

Michael White and David Epston's "narrative therapy," developed in the 1980s and 1990s, was built on exactly this recognition. White argued that people who come to therapy are typically "problem-saturated" in their self-narratives: the problem has been so central for so long that it dominates the story, and alternative stories that might also be legitimately told from the same life material are invisible. The therapeutic work involves "externalizing" the problem (treating it as something distinct from the person rather than as defining of the person), finding "unique outcomes" (moments when the problem was present but the person responded differently, which the problem-saturated narrative has been ignoring) and using those unique outcomes as the foundation of an alternative story that gives the person more agency and more possible futures.

Cultural Narratives and the Master Plot

Individual life narratives are not created in a vacuum. They are assembled from materials that cultures provide: narrative conventions, plot structures, character types, theories of causation and meaning that constitute what James Phelan and others call "master plots" -- the culturally available story templates within which individual stories are told. The hero's journey, the fall from innocence, the immigrant success story, the recovery narrative, the coming-of-age story -- these are culturally circulating templates that individuals draw on in constructing their personal myths.

The cultural availability of certain master plots and the unavailability of others has real consequences for whose lives can be narrated with coherence and meaning. A culture that has robust narrative templates for the stories of straight white men and thin narrative resources for anyone else leaves members of other groups with the difficult choice between forcing their experience into ill-fitting templates or working without narrative support for their self-construction. The expansion of whose stories get told in literature, film, journalism and public discourse is not merely a question of representation in some abstract sense. It is a question of what self-narrative resources are available to people who need to make meaning of experiences that the dominant templates don't accommodate.

"I grew up not seeing people who looked like me in the stories that were supposed to be universal. It wasn't that there were no stories about people like me -- it was that the stories that were there were usually about suffering or failure. And you internalize the stories that are available for your life, whether you want to or not. Finding literature by and about people whose experience actually overlapped with mine changed my self-narrative in ways I'm still working out." r/books · 22,384 upvotes

Cultural narratives about what a life should look like at different ages and stages function as normative benchmarks against which people measure their own progress and feel successful or failed. The life-script of marriage by a certain age, children, career advancement, home ownership and the specific sequence of milestones that constitutes a "normal" life was never universal even when it was presented as such, and its normative force has diminished substantially in the last fifty years. But normative benchmarks don't disappear when they become less universal. They continue to shape self-assessment even when people consciously reject them, producing what researchers call "narrative timing violations" -- the sense of being off-script that many people experience when their lives diverge from the expected sequence.

Narrative and Resilience

The relationship between life narrative and resilience has been studied most thoroughly through the concept of "benefit finding" or "meaning-making" in the aftermath of adversity. Research by Crystal Park at the University of Connecticut and by Ronnie Janoff-Bulman on trauma and the shattering of "assumptive worlds" (the implicit beliefs about the world being benevolent, meaningful and controllable that most people carry until something shatters them) has documented the importance of narrative meaning-making for adaptation after significant negative events.

When something severely bad happens, it typically violates the narrative coherence of the person's life: the event doesn't fit the story they had been telling about themselves and their world. The work of adaptation involves revising the narrative to accommodate the event -- finding a place for it within a story that still has coherence and meaning rather than leaving it as a jarring intrusion that has no place in the narrative. This revision process is what many people describe as "coming to terms with" a loss or difficulty: not accepting that it was good or shouldn't have happened but finding a way to integrate it into a coherent understanding of one's life.

Research on posttraumatic growth -- the phenomenon documented by Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun in which some people report positive psychological changes following trauma -- is closely related to narrative meaning-making. Growth is not guaranteed by trauma and is not present in all or even most trauma survivors. But when it occurs, it appears to require narrative work: the construction of a story in which the trauma, while not welcomed, is understood as having contributed to something valued -- greater wisdom, stronger relationships, clarified priorities, expanded empathy, a more authentic relationship with life's impermanence. The trauma survivor who has done this narrative work is not in denial about what happened. They have found a way to tell a story about it that allows them to move forward with something rather than simply less.

Therapy as Narrative Revision

Much of what happens in effective psychotherapy, across widely different theoretical orientations, can be understood as narrative revision. Cognitive behavioral therapy challenges the specific interpretations and causal conclusions embedded in the patient's self-narrative ("I always fail," "people always leave me," "I am fundamentally flawed") and replaces them with more accurate and more functional ones. Psychodynamic therapy excavates the earlier chapters of the life story that are shaping present chapters in ways the person doesn't recognize -- the family patterns, early experiences and acquired relationship templates that are running in the background. Narrative therapy, as described above, explicitly works with alternative stories and the expansion of the narrative repertoire. ACT (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy) works with the relationship to one's own self-narrative, teaching the capacity to observe the story without being identical to it ("I notice I'm telling myself the story that I'm a failure," rather than simply "I'm a failure").

The ACT insight is particularly interesting from a narrative identity perspective: it is possible to have a self-narrative and simultaneously to be aware that it is a narrative -- to hold it lightly rather than experiencing it as transparent reality. This meta-cognitive relationship to one's own story is associated with greater psychological flexibility, lower rates of depression and anxiety, and better capacity to respond to new information rather than filtering it through a fixed story. The person who cannot take any distance from their self-narrative is its prisoner. The person who can observe it from a slight distance without abandoning it has more freedom to revise it when revision is warranted.

"The most helpful thing my therapist ever said was: 'You have a story about yourself that you've been telling for thirty years. That story served a purpose when you needed it. But you're still living in it as though it's the only possible account of who you are. What would a different story look like?' That question genuinely changed something." r/therapy · 36,847 upvotes

The story you tell about your life is the most important story you will ever tell, because it determines how you interpret new experience, what possibilities you can see, what you are capable of attempting, and how you respond to difficulty. It is not the truth about you in the simple sense -- no story about a human life captures the full truth of it. But it is the operating system for your sense of self, and like any operating system it can be upgraded. The work is not denial of what has happened but honest and sometimes painful renegotiation of what it means, what it makes possible, and what it forecloses. The material doesn't change. What changes is the authorship. And the authorship, the research consistently suggests, is something you have more of than your story has been telling you.
