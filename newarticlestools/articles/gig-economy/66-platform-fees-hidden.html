Platform Fees Are Eating Your Income: The Real Cost of Marketplace Work

19,847 upvotes · by u/fee_calculator · 11 min read
▲ 19,847 people found this relatable
Advertisement

When you sell a handmade item on Etsy for $40, Etsy takes a listing fee, a transaction fee, a payment processing fee, an offsite advertising fee if the sale came through their advertising and, if you are subscribed to Etsy Plus, a monthly subscription. By the time the platform is finished, roughly 15 to 25 percent of your sale price has been extracted before you have accounted for materials, your own labor, shipping costs or the taxes you owe on the income. The buyer paid $40. You received something meaningfully less than $40. The difference went to a marketplace you cannot effectively replace because it has the customers and you need the customers.

This is the basic structure of platform economics for independent workers and sellers, and it plays out with variation across every major marketplace: Upwork and Fiverr for professional services, Amazon for third-party sellers, Airbnb for hosts, DoorDash and Uber Eats for delivery workers and restaurants, Spotify for musicians, Apple App Store and Google Play for app developers, YouTube for creators through their revenue share structure. Platforms provide a genuine service, access to customers or audiences that independent workers could not efficiently find or serve on their own. They charge for that service through fees. The question is not whether the fees are legitimate but whether the fee structures are fairly calibrated to the value provided, and whether the market power dynamics that allow platforms to set and raise fees are constraining independent workers in ways that warrant attention.

The Compound Fee Problem

Individual platform fees are rarely presented in ways that make their total impact on income immediately visible. Platforms describe their fee structures through a combination of percentage rates, fixed amounts and optional premium tiers, expressed in terms that make comparison and total calculation non-trivial. A freelancer on Upwork working through the platform's standard fee structure pays 20 percent on the first $500 earned with any client, 10 percent on earnings between $500 and $10,000 and 5 percent above $10,000. This tiered structure means that early-stage client relationships, and all work with lower-volume clients, are taxed at rates that substantially compress net income. A freelancer who earns $300 from a short project receives $240 after Upwork's commission, from which they still owe self-employment tax, any business expenses and income tax.

Amazon's fee structure for third-party marketplace sellers illustrates the compound problem with particular clarity. Amazon charges a referral fee on each sale that typically runs between 8 and 15 percent depending on product category. Sellers using Fulfillment by Amazon, which the platform increasingly pressures sellers to use through algorithmic ranking advantages, pay additional fulfillment fees based on item size and weight, monthly storage fees, removal fees for inventory that does not sell quickly enough and, for sellers who want advertising visibility, pay-per-click advertising costs. Research by marketplace analysts has found that third-party sellers on Amazon effectively pay 40 to 50 percent of their revenue to the platform across all fees, a figure that leaves very thin margins on competitively priced goods and has driven many small sellers either out of the marketplace or into practices that degrade product quality to preserve margin.

"I ran a small Etsy shop for three years. I genuinely loved making the things I was selling. I quit in 2023 after Etsy raised their fees again and I did the math: I was working about 15 hours a week on it and netting less than minimum wage after all platform costs and materials. The platform got bigger and I got smaller proportionally every year. I wasn't running a business. I was working for Etsy." — u/etsy_exit_2023, r/EtsySellers · ▲ 21,304

Fee Creep: The Ratchet That Only Goes One Way

One of the most consistent patterns in the history of digital marketplace platforms is fee escalation over time. Platforms typically launch with low fees to attract a critical mass of sellers, workers or creators, establishing the supply side of their marketplace. Once a sufficient seller and buyer base has been established, the platform's market power increases, and the incentive to extract more value from the supply side through higher fees intensifies. The sellers who have built their practices around the platform's customer base are, at this point, in a poor position to resist fee increases: leaving the platform means losing access to the customer relationships and reviews they have invested years in building, which is often a cost greater than the cost of the fee increase.

Etsy raised its transaction fee from 5 to 6.5 percent in 2022, a 30 percent increase in the fee rate, implemented with limited notice and justified by platform investments that sellers did not necessarily agree were benefiting them. A significant seller protest followed, but Etsy did not reverse the increase, and the majority of sellers who threatened to leave did not leave, because the alternatives are either other platforms with their own disadvantages or the difficult work of building direct customer channels. This is the revealed preference that the platform counted on when it raised fees: sellers' dependence on the platform's customer base is sufficiently deep that fee increases can be implemented over significant resistance.

Spotify's royalty structure for musicians illustrates a different version of the same dynamic at the extreme end. Spotify pays rights holders approximately $0.003 to $0.005 per stream, a rate that requires a song to be streamed roughly 250,000 times per month for the musician to earn minimum wage from Spotify alone. The platform's original licensing deals with major record labels concentrated the streaming royalty pool in structures that disadvantaged independent artists. Spotify's initial public offering in 2018 valued the company at $26.6 billion; the total lifetime payout to the musician who wrote a moderately successful song in the same period might be in the hundreds of dollars. The platform extracted the value that music generates; the creators of that music received a fee the platform deemed sufficient.

The Lock-In Mechanism

Platform fees are sustainable in part because leaving the platform is more costly than it appears from the outside. The costs are not primarily financial; they are relational and reputational. A freelancer with a five-year Upwork history, a five-star rating from fifty clients, a Top Rated Plus badge and verified skill assessments has invested substantially in a reputation that lives on Upwork's servers and cannot be meaningfully transferred to another platform. Their history of successful engagements, which potential clients use as a primary trust signal, is not portable. Starting on a different platform means starting from zero reviews, losing algorithmic ranking advantages and rebuilding trust from scratch in a marketplace where established practitioners have incumbency advantages.

This lock-in is not unique to labor platforms; it characterizes platform economics broadly. Etsy sellers have customer bases that leave reviews on Etsy. Amazon sellers have product rankings built through Amazon sales history. Musicians on Spotify have playlist placements and algorithm positions that cannot be replicated on Bandcamp. The review, rating and ranking infrastructure that platforms provide is genuinely valuable and genuinely non-portable, which is precisely what gives platforms leverage to raise fees and change terms without triggering the mass exodus that competitive markets would generate.

The economics concept of switching costs captures this dynamic. When switching from one platform to another requires forgoing significant accumulated assets, the effective price of the incumbent platform is not just its stated fee but its stated fee plus the annualized value of the switching cost. A platform with a 20 percent fee that would cost the average worker two years of income building to replace is effectively charging more than a platform with a 25 percent fee where switching is costless, even though the nominal fee rate is lower. Platform companies understand this and invest in deepening switching costs through features like proprietary payment infrastructure, platform-specific reputation systems and exclusive distribution relationships that make the accumulated value of their ecosystem harder to leave.

"I have 200+ five-star reviews on Upwork built over four years. I tried to move clients off-platform and Upwork's terms of service technically prohibit this for clients I found through them. I tried starting a profile on a competitor. It took six months to get my first review and I never built momentum. My Upwork profile is worth something. I am effectively stuck with Upwork's fees forever unless I'm willing to write off four years of reputation building." — u/upwork_captive, r/freelance · ▲ 16,408

The Advertising Tax

Many platform marketplaces have evolved a secondary extraction mechanism that compounds the fee problem: paid advertising within the platform's own ecosystem. Amazon Sponsored Products, Etsy Ads, Upwork Boosted Proposals, Fiverr Promoted Gigs and Airbnb's enhanced visibility features all sell better algorithmic placement within the platform's search and recommendation systems to the sellers who pay for it. This creates a dynamic where the platform extracts value twice: first through transaction fees and second through advertising fees that sellers pay to maintain visibility against competitors who are also paying for visibility.

The pay-to-play dynamic in marketplace advertising is particularly corrosive for independent workers and small sellers because it favors larger sellers with more advertising budget. A small Etsy seller competing against a well-funded reseller who can spend heavily on Etsy advertising faces a visibility disadvantage that compounds over time: less visibility means fewer sales, fewer sales means fewer reviews and lower algorithmic ranking, lower ranking means more advertising is needed to compensate, and the cycle transfers income from the seller to the platform through an advertising mechanism that the seller cannot effectively opt out of if they want to maintain competitive visibility.

Building Away From Platform Dependence

The strategic response to platform dependence is not leaving platforms but building enough independent customer relationships and direct channels to reduce the leverage that platforms hold. This is easier to describe than to execute, particularly for sellers and workers in early stages of building their practices when platform traffic is the primary source of customers. But the direction of travel that sustainable platform-based businesses tend to follow involves using the platform as a customer acquisition channel while systematically building direct relationships that do not depend on the platform for maintenance.

Email lists are the most durable direct channel for most independent workers and sellers, because email access does not depend on any platform's algorithm or policy decisions. A musician who converts Spotify listeners to email subscribers owns that relationship in a way they do not own their Spotify following. An Etsy seller who converts buyers to email subscribers can communicate with them about new products without paying for Etsy advertising or relying on Etsy's search algorithm. An Upwork freelancer who builds genuine relationships with long-term clients and maintains direct contact with them, within the constraints of Upwork's terms of service, has a more durable business than one whose entire client relationship infrastructure lives on the platform.

The broader policy question is whether the market power dynamics of dominant platforms justify regulatory intervention on fee structures or competitive practices. The EU's Digital Markets Act, the UK Competition and Markets Authority's platform investigations and ongoing antitrust proceedings in the United States all reflect growing regulatory interest in whether the market power of dominant platforms is being exercised in ways that harm the sellers and workers who depend on them. The outcomes of these regulatory processes will shape the fee landscape for platform workers over the next decade, potentially more significantly than individual choices about which platforms to use or how to build around them.
