Side Hustle Culture Has Gone Too Far: The Problem with Monetizing Everything

26,554 upvotes · by u/just_a_hobby_please · 12 min read
▲ 26,554 people found this relatable
Advertisement

Someone will tell you to monetize it. Whatever the thing you enjoy, whatever the skill you are developing, whatever the interest you pursue on evenings and weekends, there is a genre of advice content that will tell you that the failure to turn it into income is a missed opportunity, a failure of entrepreneurial imagination, a refusal to play the game correctly. Can you cook well? Start a food blog. Do you run? Sell a training plan. Do you read widely? Summarize books for an audience. Are you good at something technical? Tutor people in it. Have opinions? A newsletter. Have a face and a reasonable Wi-Fi connection? Literally anything can be content.

This advice is not entirely wrong. Some hobbies do translate into supplementary income, and for people whose primary income is insufficient to cover their costs, the side hustle is not a lifestyle choice but a financial necessity. The critique of side hustle culture is not that earning money from your skills is bad. It is that the relentless cultural pressure to monetize everything, to treat income generation as the appropriate response to any skill or interest, is reshaping the relationship people have with their own non-work lives in ways that are worth examining critically before adopting as a personal operating philosophy.

What Hobbies Are For

The concept of a hobby, an activity pursued for its own sake, for pleasure, skill development, relaxation or social connection, without any instrumental purpose or expected return, is one that has existed in most cultures throughout recorded history. Play, leisure and creative activity that is not oriented toward production have been recognized by philosophers, psychologists and cultural commentators across widely different traditions as valuable components of a well-lived human life. They provide restoration, the replenishment of cognitive and emotional resources consumed by work and obligation. They provide identity outside of productive and economic roles. They provide the kind of intrinsically motivated engagement that research on flow and peak experience consistently associates with psychological wellbeing.

The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, whose research on flow states is among the most cited in positive psychology, found that people report their highest states of engagement and wellbeing not in passive leisure but in activities that are challenging enough to require skill but not so demanding as to produce anxiety. Many hobby activities sit in this sweet spot naturally: the difficulty level is self-selected, the feedback is immediate and the consequences of failure are low enough that the activity can be genuinely playful. Introducing economic stakes into this equation changes its structure. When your pottery is something you must sell to justify the time and materials, the activity that was intrinsically rewarding for its own imperfect process becomes an activity oriented toward the production of a sellable good, and the psychological experience of the two is different in ways that matter.

"I used to love writing. I wrote for years in journals and for online forums, just because I liked doing it. Then I started a newsletter to 'monetize my writing skills.' Now I hate writing. Not sometimes — I actually dread sitting down to write. The newsletter is moderately successful by any objective measure. I am miserable doing it. I didn't monetize my passion. I turned my passion into a job I didn't interview for." — u/newsletter_regret, r/writing · ▲ 23,841

The Overjustification Effect at Scale

The psychological mechanism through which external rewards reduce intrinsic motivation has been studied since the 1970s. The "overjustification effect," demonstrated robustly in dozens of experiments, occurs when an activity that was previously intrinsically motivating is brought under external reward contingency. Once the reward is present, the activity loses its intrinsic justification: you are doing it for the money rather than for itself. When the reward is removed, or when the activity becomes associated with the obligations and performance demands of paid work, the intrinsic motivation does not simply return to its pre-reward level. For many activities and many people, it is permanently reduced or eliminated.

Side hustle culture applies this dynamic at the population level. By persistently framing unpaid hobbies and interests as "leaving money on the table," it creates a cultural context in which choosing not to monetize a skill or interest requires justification while monetizing it is presented as the self-evidently correct response. The person who bakes excellent bread and gives it to friends is prompted to ask why they are not selling it. The person who does beautiful watercolors and keeps them for themselves is asked why they are not on Etsy. The person who maintains a rich personal journal is asked what platform they are building it on. In each case, the implicit message is that an activity without economic output is somehow incomplete, that the skill demonstrated requires monetization to be fully legitimate.

The aggregate effect of this framing on people's relationship with their own non-work activities is not trivial. When every interest becomes a potential revenue stream, the decision about how to spend non-work time becomes systematically evaluated through an economic lens. Is this the best use of my time? Could I be doing something more productive? Should I be building something? This evaluation is exhausting in a specific way because it never resolves: there is always, in the side hustle framework, something more productive you could be doing, and the choice to simply enjoy something without economic purpose becomes a choice that requires constant re-justification against the implicit alternative of monetization.

The Real Reason Side Hustle Culture Took Off

Side hustle culture did not emerge from nowhere. Its rise tracks almost precisely with the period of real wage stagnation, rising housing costs and increasing economic precarity that characterized the 2010s in the United States and several other wealthy countries. When wages do not cover costs, people look for supplementary income. When benefits are not provided by primary employers, gig work allows some people to patch together a more complete economic picture. When financial security is genuinely precarious, the advice to develop multiple income streams is not abstract hustle-culture ideology but a reasonable response to a real structural problem.

The problem is when the cultural response to structural economic failure becomes normalized to the point where it obscures the structural failure itself. When working two jobs is framed as entrepreneurial hustle rather than as evidence of wage suppression, when the need for multiple income streams is presented as a lifestyle choice rather than an economic necessity, when the individual response to collective economic problems is celebrated rather than questioned, the structural conditions that make multiple income streams necessary are naturalized rather than challenged. Side hustle culture, in its more ideological forms, is partly a mechanism through which the consequences of economic failure are transformed into evidence of individual ingenuity.

This framing has specific effects on people who cannot or do not want to maintain side hustles. People who work demanding primary jobs, who have significant caregiving responsibilities, who are managing health conditions, who lack the particular combination of skills and circumstances that side hustle income requires: these people are implicitly framed as failing to optimize their economic position, when what is actually happening is that the primary economic arrangements available to them are insufficient and the secondary arrangements that would supplement them are not accessible or sustainable. The failure is not individual. The framing that makes it look individual is worth resisting.

"I work 50 hours a week in healthcare and have two kids. Everyone keeps telling me I should start a side hustle. With what time? With what energy? The assumption that I have hours to spare that I'm wasting by not monetizing them is incredibly out of touch with what life actually looks like for a lot of people. A side hustle is a solution for a specific demographic. It's being sold as universal." — u/no_spare_hours, r/antiwork · ▲ 19,274

The Commodification of Rest and Social Life

Side hustle culture's most insidious expansion is into the domains of rest and social life. The transformation of leisure activities into monetizable content means that even time that was previously clearly non-productive is now evaluated through a productivity lens. The family vacation that generates Instagram content. The dinner party that is photographed for a food blog. The morning routine that becomes a YouTube series. The personal transformation that becomes a course. None of these are inherently problematic choices, but the accumulation of them describes a lifestyle in which the distinction between living and performing a life for commercial purposes has significantly blurred.

The specific cost of this blurring is difficult to measure but easy to recognize in experience. The meal shared with friends that is partly a content production opportunity is a different meal than the one shared without commercial purpose. The vacation experienced partly through the frame of what it will generate for an audience is a different vacation than the one experienced without that frame. The relationship maintained partly because it provides material is a different relationship than the one maintained without that consideration. The monetization of life's experiences does not leave those experiences unchanged; it introduces a layer of instrumentality that alters the quality of the experience for the person in it.

There is also the question of what unpaid activities do for the social fabric and community life that paid activities cannot replicate. Volunteering, community organizing, mutual aid, friendship maintenance, participation in religious or civic institutions, mentoring without payment: these activities generate social value that does not show up in anyone's income statement but that sustains the social infrastructure on which everything else depends. A culture that systematically redirects time and energy from unpaid social contribution toward monetizable activity is, in aggregate, consuming social capital while treating it as a free resource.

The Permission to Not Monetize

This article is, to some extent, permission literature. You do not have to turn your baking into a business. You do not have to build an audience around your photography. You do not have to create a course about your expertise. You are allowed to be good at things that you do not sell. You are allowed to enjoy activities whose entire value is the experience of doing them and the pleasure they generate in your own life. You are allowed to rest without it producing content. You are allowed to have a hobby that is just a hobby, that exists outside the economic frame, that you would do even if no one paid you for it and you would not particularly want them to.

This is not an argument against all monetization of skills and interests. People who genuinely enjoy building businesses around their skills, who find the commercial dimension adds meaning and motivation rather than subtracting it, who have found ways to maintain the intrinsic pleasure of their work while also earning income from it, have found something worth having. The research on optimal conditions for creative work suggests this is possible when there is sufficient autonomy, when the commercial demands are not so intense as to crowd out the intrinsic motivation, and when the person has a genuine orientation toward both the creative work and the business of making it sustainable. It is not impossible. It is also not universal, and the cultural pressure that presents it as the obvious correct choice for everyone should be examined rather than simply adopted.

The question worth asking is not "how can I monetize this?" but "what does this activity do for me and what would it do differently if I tried to monetize it?" The answer will be different for different people and different activities. For some, the answer is that monetization would add purpose and sustainability to an activity they already love. For others, the answer is that monetization would add obligation, audience judgment and commercial performance pressure to something whose value is precisely its freedom from those things. Both answers are valid. Only one of them is well-represented in the culture's default advice.
