The Standardized Testing Machine and the Anxiety It Creates

A third grader in New Jersey sits at a desk that feels too big for her, staring at a booklet of reading passages about things she has no context for, answering multiple-choice questions under timed conditions. She is eight years old. Somewhere in the building, her teacher is watching a clock and feeling something between worry and helplessness. In the evening, her parents will ask how it went, and she will not be able to explain the particular knot in her stomach that materialized around question fourteen and never quite went away. None of them will use the word "anxiety." But that is what it is.

Standardized testing is so deeply embedded in American education that most people have forgotten to ask whether the structure makes sense. We test children in elementary school to measure reading and math proficiency. We test them again in middle school for accountability benchmarks. We test them in high school for college admissions. We test them for gifted programs, for AP courses, for scholarship eligibility and for graduate school placement. The cumulative effect is a childhood and adolescence organized partly around preparation for assessment, in which being measured becomes one of the primary experiences of being a student.

The testing industry is enormous. The College Board, a nonprofit that administers the SAT and AP exams, reported revenues of roughly $1.2 billion in its last publicly available filing. ACT Inc. is comparable in scale. State testing contracts run into hundreds of millions of dollars annually. The test prep industry, including tutoring companies, prep books and online platforms, generates billions more. Around the central question of whether any of this accurately measures what it claims to measure, there is considerably more disagreement than the industry's self-confidence would suggest.

Where Standardized Testing Came From

The history of standardized testing in America is tangled with a history of eugenics in ways that testing advocates rarely lead with. The Army Alpha and Beta tests, developed during World War I to efficiently classify recruits, were among the first large-scale intelligence assessments administered to Americans. After the war, the psychologists who developed them brought their frameworks back to schools, where intelligence testing became a tool for sorting children into academic tracks. The underlying assumption, that intelligence was a fixed, measurable, largely heritable quantity, reflected the scientific consensus of the 1920s. It also reflected the social preferences of the researchers, most of whom were white, educated and professionally interested in theories that positioned people like them at the top of intellectual hierarchies.

The SAT was developed in the 1920s by Carl Brigham, who had worked on the Army tests and who argued in a 1923 book that immigration was causing American intelligence to decline. Brigham later repudiated those views, but the test he developed became the dominant college entrance assessment in America, shaping educational opportunity for generations. The College Board has made significant changes to the SAT over the decades, dropping analogy questions and other formats that showed particularly strong socioeconomic bias. But the fundamental structure of a timed, paper-based test designed to measure abstract reasoning has remained, along with the persistent socioeconomic correlations in scores that have characterized it since the beginning.

No Child Left Behind, signed into law in 2002, represented the most significant expansion of standardized testing in K-12 education in American history. The law required annual testing in reading and math for grades 3 through 8 and once in high school, tied school funding to test performance and created accountability mechanisms based heavily on standardized test scores. The Every Student Succeeds Act of 2015 gave states more flexibility in how they used test scores for accountability, but maintained the testing requirements. The result is that American students today take more standardized tests, earlier and more frequently, than any previous generation.

What the Tests Actually Measure

Testing proponents argue that standardized tests measure real academic skills, provide objective data free from teacher bias and offer a common metric for comparing students across different schools and demographic groups. Each of these arguments has a version that is true and a version that overstates the case considerably.

It's true that standardized tests correlate with academic performance. SAT scores predict college GPA at statistically significant levels, though the predictive power is modest, especially when high school GPA is included in the model. Studies consistently find that high school GPA is a better predictor of college success than standardized test scores, which raises the question of what the tests add beyond what grades already tell us. The answer, in short, is a measure that is less subject to grade inflation and school-to-school variation in grading standards, and a signal about performance on timed, high-stakes tasks specifically. Whether that signal is worth the enormous infrastructure and stress it requires is a different question.

The relationship between standardized test scores and family income is one of the best-documented patterns in education research. In 2023 SAT data, students from families earning over $200,000 per year scored an average of roughly 150 points higher on the combined SAT than students from families earning under $20,000. This correlation is often presented as evidence that tests measure preparation rather than intelligence, and that's partly correct. It's also evidence that the resources available to wealthy families, including test prep courses averaging $1,000 to $3,000 for commercial options, private tutors at $150 to $400 per hour and the time and cognitive bandwidth that comes with not growing up in financial scarcity, show up in scores in ways that make the test measure family wealth as much as it measures academic capability.

What Gets Displaced

Testing doesn't just consume time directly. It consumes curriculum indirectly through a mechanism called curricular narrowing. When schools are evaluated primarily on reading and math test scores, rational administrators respond by dedicating more instructional time to reading and math and less to everything else. Multiple studies have documented this effect after No Child Left Behind, finding significant reductions in time devoted to social studies, science, art, music and physical education in the years following the law's passage, particularly in lower-performing schools serving higher proportions of low-income students.

This creates an educational equity problem layered on top of the already significant equity problems in school funding. Affluent schools and districts, less likely to face the most severe accountability consequences, continue offering rich curricula with electives, arts programs and extracurricular activities. Lower-income schools, more likely to be labeled underperforming and facing consequences, narrow their curriculum toward the tested subjects and reduce or eliminate the enrichment activities that make school engaging and developmentally valuable. Students in the schools that most need enriching educational experiences get the most impoverished version of schooling, while the students in the most well-resourced schools get the broadest educational experiences. The accountability system designed to help struggling schools can, in practice, make them worse in ways that don't show up on the tests.

Social-emotional learning, civic education, creative thinking and collaborative problem-solving are all notoriously difficult to standardize and therefore difficult to test. They are also the skills that employers, researchers and educators consistently identify as essential for adult functioning. The emphasis on measurable academic skills has created a system that does reasonably well at what it measures and potentially undermines what it doesn't, a classic case of Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

Test Anxiety as a Clinical Reality

Test anxiety is a recognized clinical phenomenon with its own diagnostic characteristics and treatment research. It goes well beyond the ordinary nervousness that most people feel before any high-stakes event. Test-anxious individuals experience intrusive thoughts about failure, difficulty concentrating, physical symptoms including rapid heartbeat, sweating and nausea, and performance that is meaningfully worse than their actual knowledge and capability would predict under non-anxious conditions. Research suggests that somewhere between 10 and 40 percent of students experience test anxiety at clinically significant levels, with higher rates among high-achieving students in high-pressure environments.

The mechanism through which test anxiety impairs performance is relatively well understood. Anxiety activates the threat response system, redirecting cognitive resources toward threat monitoring and away from the working memory processes that academic performance requires. When you're anxious, you're partly running an internal threat detection program in the background, and that program consumes the same limited cognitive resources you need to read carefully, hold information in mind and reason through complex problems. High-stakes testing environments are specifically designed to be threatening, with time pressure, high consequences and a structure that makes students highly aware they are being evaluated. This design is essentially optimal for triggering anxiety in anxiety-prone individuals.

The inequality dimension of test anxiety matters here too. Research has found that students from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, students from stigmatized racial and ethnic groups, first-generation college students and students with certain mental health conditions experience higher levels of test anxiety on average. This means the populations that already face structural disadvantages in educational outcomes are also disproportionately likely to have their performance degraded by anxiety on the assessments that gatekeep their opportunities. The test is not a neutral measurement instrument. It interacts with the characteristics of the test taker in ways that systematically affect some groups more than others.

The College Admissions Industrial Complex

The SAT and ACT occupy a particularly charged position in American education because they sit at the gateway to higher education, which in the American imagination means they sit at the gateway to economic mobility, status and adult identity. This is not entirely irrational. For selective colleges, test scores have historically been significant factors in admissions decisions. And entry into selective colleges does correlate with better career outcomes, though researchers debate how much of that correlation reflects the value the education adds versus the selection of students who would have succeeded anywhere.

The test prep industry built around college admissions tests is a perfect illustration of how high stakes and unequal access interact. When scores matter for admissions and scores can be improved with preparation, families with resources invest in preparation. When wealthy families invest in preparation and scores improve, the colleges that use scores as admissions criteria see higher scores from wealthy applicants and lower scores from non-wealthy applicants. If the college responds by raising score expectations, the bar shifts upward in a way that the families with resources can meet and the families without resources cannot. The test that was supposed to give talented students from modest backgrounds a fair shot at elite schools has, in practice, often functioned as a mechanism for perpetuating the advantages of families that were already advantaged.

The COVID-19 pandemic forced a natural experiment when nearly all selective colleges adopted test-optional or test-blind admissions policies in 2020 and 2021. The results were revealing. Applications increased dramatically, particularly from first-generation students, students from lower-income families and students from underrepresented racial groups. Early research on outcomes suggested that test-optional students admitted during this period performed comparably to students who submitted test scores, undermining the argument that scores were essential for predicting academic success. As of 2024, many highly selective universities had returned to requiring test scores, including Yale, MIT and Dartmouth, though the debate about what tests actually contribute to admissions beyond what high school records already provide remains active.

The Elementary School Testing Treadmill

While college admissions tests get the most attention, perhaps the more consequential testing pressure operates at much younger ages in ways that shape children's relationship to learning before they have the cognitive tools to contextualize it. Children in American public schools begin taking standardized assessments in some states as early as kindergarten, with regular state assessments beginning in third grade under federal requirements. The message embedded in this structure, that your performance on this assessment matters and will be used to make judgments about you and your school, is communicated to children who are developmentally unable to understand it in any useful way.

Child development research is fairly clear that children in early and middle childhood are in a critical period for developing their identity as learners. Whether a child comes to see herself as capable or incapable, as someone who can figure things out or as someone who is bad at school, is shaped significantly by early academic experiences. Creating high-stakes assessment environments for young children, particularly when those assessments consistently produce failure experiences for students whose preparation levels don't match the test's expectations, can shape learning identities in ways that persist. The child who learns at age eight that she is a bad test-taker has learned something about herself that may be entirely untrue in a meaningful sense while remaining entirely true as a practical matter, because the assessments she'll face for the next decade will be similar enough to the ones she's already struggling with.

Teachers are often the best witnesses to this dynamic, and what they describe is painful. Teachers who taught before the heavy testing era and continued afterward frequently describe the shift in how children relate to school, with younger students showing anxiety responses, avoidance behaviors and loss of curiosity and playfulness that wasn't characteristic of their earlier student cohorts. This is subjective testimony, not clinical data, but it comes from people who have observed thousands of children learning over long careers and who are describing a real change they can see in the children in front of them.

International Comparisons and What They Show

American education policy frequently invokes international comparisons, typically in the context of anxiety about whether American students are falling behind their peers in high-performing countries like Finland, South Korea, Singapore and Japan. What these comparisons often leave out is that the education systems held up as models differ from the American system in ways that go well beyond which skills they prioritize.

Finland's education system, frequently cited as among the world's best, administers remarkably few standardized tests until students are in their late teens. Finnish students begin formal schooling later than their American counterparts, play more and have less homework. Teachers in Finland are highly trained, highly respected and given significant professional autonomy. The country has much lower child poverty rates than the United States, much less residential segregation and much more equal school funding. Attributing Finland's outcomes to its testing culture while ignoring these other variables is analytically suspect. The education system is not separable from the social system in which it operates.

High-performing East Asian education systems like South Korea and Japan do use more testing, but the picture there is also complicated. South Korean parents and students frequently describe the testing culture as intensely stressful, and the country has high rates of academic anxiety, sleep deprivation among students and spending on private tutoring, called hagwons, that consume enormous portions of family income. The outcomes in terms of standardized international test scores are impressive. The outcomes in terms of youth wellbeing are more contested. The question of whether "high test scores" and "good education" are the same thing is one that testing advocates tend to answer with a confident yes and that educators, psychologists and many parents would answer with significant qualification.

What the Research Says About Alternatives

Portfolio-based assessment, project-based learning, teacher-developed assessments and performance tasks are all alternatives to standardized tests that have their own research bases. Critics of these alternatives note that they are harder to standardize and therefore harder to use for large-scale accountability comparisons, that they may be more susceptible to teacher bias and that they require more sophisticated teacher training to implement well. These are legitimate concerns. But the current system's defenders sometimes present these concerns as if the alternative to imperfect alternatives is a perfect standardized test, when the actual alternative is different imperfections with different costs.

The Chicago Consortium on School Research has done some of the most rigorous work on what actually predicts college success and found that noncognitive factors including study habits, academic behaviors, mindset and social belonging matter substantially, in some analyses as much as or more than test scores. Researchers at the University of Chicago and elsewhere have developed measures of these factors that have predictive validity. The challenge is that these measures are harder to standardize, easier to game and require more resources to administer, making them poor candidates for mass accountability systems even if they're genuinely informative at the individual level.

The most honest position on standardized testing is that tests provide one kind of information, that information has value and limitations, and building an entire accountability and opportunity-allocation system around one kind of information produces predictable pathologies. The testing isn't the only problem with American education. It's one significant problem among several, and its costs are unevenly distributed in ways that consistently land harder on students and families who already have the least.

Surviving the Testing Culture as a Parent or Student

For parents raising children inside this system, the practical challenge is maintaining a healthy relationship with learning in a context that applies constant pressure to see learning as primarily instrumental and assessment performance as primarily important. Children learn not just from what schools teach them but from what they see their parents value. Parents who talk about grades and scores as the primary indicators of educational success communicate a message about what education is for. Parents who express genuine curiosity about what their children are learning, who model intellectual engagement and who treat a poor assessment score as information rather than verdict are communicating a different message.

Students who are prone to test anxiety benefit enormously from developing a cognitive reframing of what tests represent. A test is information about performance on this task on this day under these conditions. It is not a verdict on intelligence, on potential or on worth. This reframing is easier said than done in a culture that treats certain test scores as life-defining events, but even partial shifts in how students relate to tests can reduce the anxiety response. Mindfulness-based interventions have modest but real research support for reducing test anxiety specifically. Simply knowing that anxiety is normal, expected and manageable, rather than a sign of inadequacy, reduces its impact for many students.

The structural changes that would most help, restoring funding to arts and enrichment programs, reducing the accountability weight placed on single test scores and investing in teacher training for formative assessment, require political will that has been hard to sustain. But awareness of what the current system costs, the anxiety it creates, the curriculum it narrows and the inequities it reinforces, is a precondition for building that will. The child sitting at the too-big desk with the knot in her stomach is not the problem. The system that put her there, and tells her that her worth can be measured in bubbles filled correctly, is.
