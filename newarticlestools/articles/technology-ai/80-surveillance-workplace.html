Workplace Surveillance Is the New Normal

28,317 upvotes · by u/worker_dignity_matters · 12 min read
▲ 28,317 people found this relatable

When remote work became widespread in 2020, many employers faced a problem they had not previously needed to solve at scale: how to know whether employees were actually working when no manager could see them. The response, accelerated by an industry of workforce monitoring software that had existed in more limited forms before the pandemic, was surveillance. Within eighteen months of widespread remote work adoption, the employee monitoring software market had grown by over fifty percent. Tools that tracked keystrokes, took random screenshots, measured mouse movement, logged application usage, monitored email content and calculated productivity scores from behavioral metrics became widespread in ways that would have been considered dystopian in a different context.

What began as a response to the specific uncertainty of remote work has not receded as offices reopened. It has expanded. The data collection infrastructure built during the remote work period has been retained and in many cases extended. Employees returning to physical offices found surveillance systems that had not existed before. AI-powered tools now monitor retail worker efficiency at the level of seconds. Call center workers have their speech patterns analyzed in real time. Warehouse workers have every physical movement tracked. Knowledge workers have their digital behavior profiled continuously. The workplace has become one of the most surveilled environments in modern life, and the legal, ethical and psychological implications of that transformation are only beginning to be understood.

The Toolkit of Worker Surveillance

The specific technologies in use span a wide range of invasiveness. At the more transparent end are productivity measurement tools that log application usage and generate reports showing how much time workers spend in different programs. At the more invasive end are tools that take random screenshots of worker screens multiple times per hour, capture keystrokes including text that is typed but never sent, activate webcams to take periodic photos of the worker's face and analyze those photos for attentiveness, and generate algorithmic productivity scores that feed directly into performance management systems.

Hubstaff, Teramind, ActivTrak and Time Doctor are among the major players in the employee monitoring software market. Their marketing materials are revealing about the intended use cases. Teramind's website offers "insider threat detection" alongside productivity monitoring. ActivTrak describes itself as offering "workforce analytics." The language of analytics and productivity optimization sits alongside the language of security and threat detection, blurring the distinction between measuring productivity and managing workers as potential security risks.

For physical workers, the surveillance architecture is different but comparably extensive. Amazon warehouse workers have been monitored at a level of granularity that includes the time between package scans. Workers have received automated productivity warnings when their rates fell below algorithm-set thresholds, and have been subject to automated termination recommendations based on productivity metrics, with human management review apparently optional in some documented cases. A 2021 Vice investigation found that Amazon's system had generated hundreds of thousands of automated productivity warnings per year across its warehouse workforce. Delivery drivers have GPS-tracked routes, monitored driving behavior and in some cases had AI-powered cameras watching for distracted driving, yawning and seat belt non-compliance.

The Productivity Measurement Problem

The business case for worker surveillance is typically framed in terms of productivity measurement and improvement. The framing raises an immediate empirical question: does surveillance actually improve productivity? The research on this question is consistent and largely negative. Surveillance does not improve the kind of productivity that matters most in knowledge work and in most service work. It consistently degrades it.

The psychological mechanisms are well understood. Autonomy is one of the strongest predictors of intrinsic motivation in the workplace literature. Workers who have genuine control over how they do their work show higher engagement, lower turnover and, critically, better performance on tasks requiring creativity and problem solving than workers whose methods are controlled and monitored. Surveillance is the operational opposite of autonomy. It communicates distrust, removes discretion and creates an environment in which compliance becomes the goal rather than excellence.

Studies of monitored versus non-monitored workers consistently find that monitoring increases performance on the specific metric being measured while decreasing performance on unmeasured dimensions. A worker whose keystrokes are counted types more. They do not necessarily produce better work. A call center worker managing to average handle time metrics may rush customers off the phone before their problems are fully resolved. A warehouse worker pacing to algorithmic productivity scores may skip safety precautions that the system does not measure. Goodhart's Law, the principle that when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure, applies with particular force to the complex, multidimensional work that surveillance systems attempt to reduce to a productivity score.

A 2022 study published in Harvard Business Review found that workers who were aware of being monitored reported significantly lower job satisfaction, higher stress levels, lower trust in their employer and greater intention to leave the job than workers who were not monitored, controlling for other factors. The study also found no significant productivity improvement associated with monitoring for knowledge workers. The surveillance cost workers substantially in wellbeing while delivering no measurable benefit to employers in the one dimension employers used to justify it.

The Chronic Stress Architecture

Continuous monitoring creates a sustained stress response that has both psychological and physiological consequences. The human stress response evolved to handle acute, identifiable threats. It does not handle chronic, diffuse threats well. A worker who knows their keystrokes are being counted, their screen is periodically photographed and their productivity score is visible to management exists in a state of continuous low-level threat assessment that activates stress physiology without providing any mechanism for resolving it.

Research on chronic workplace stress consistently finds that it elevates cortisol levels, increases risk of cardiovascular disease, impairs immune function and contributes to both anxiety and depression. The specific stress of being watched adds the psychological dimension of self-consciousness, the phenomenon researchers call the panopticon effect, in which the mere possibility of being observed modifies behavior even when observation is not actually occurring. Workers under surveillance modify their behavior based on what they believe the monitoring system will see, regardless of whether they are actually being watched at any given moment. This modification is cognitively expensive and emotionally exhausting.

The emotional labor dimension is significant for customer-facing workers specifically. Performing emotional states on demand, being friendly, patient and helpful with customers regardless of personal emotional state, is a well-documented source of emotional exhaustion. AI monitoring of speech patterns, tone of voice and the use of specified phrases adds a layer of performed emotional authenticity on top of the basic emotional labor requirement. Workers must manage their actual emotional state, manage their performance of emotional states for customers and simultaneously be aware that an AI is evaluating whether their performed emotional state meets specified parameters. The cognitive load of this triple management is substantial and poorly recognized in the monitoring industry's productivity framing.

The Legal Landscape

Workplace surveillance operates in a legal environment that has not kept pace with the technology. In the United States, employers have broad legal authority to monitor employees on employer-provided equipment and networks, with minimal notice requirements in most states. The Electronic Communications Privacy Act of 1986, written before the internet, provides very limited protection for employee communications. Most states require only that employers disclose that monitoring occurs, not what specifically is monitored, how data is used or how long it is retained.

A small number of states have moved to provide additional protections. Connecticut and Delaware require more specific disclosure of monitoring practices. New York City passed a law in 2022 requiring employers to notify employees of electronic monitoring before it begins. California's Privacy Rights Act gives employees some rights to know what personal data is collected about them in the employment context, though the employment provisions are less robust than the consumer protections.

The EU's General Data Protection Regulation applies to employee data as well as consumer data and requires that employee monitoring be necessary, proportionate and transparent. EU data protection authorities have taken enforcement action against employers whose monitoring practices violated these principles. A German court found that the use of keylogger software without employee consent was illegal. A European court ruling established that employers cannot access employees' private communications on work devices without legitimate cause. The legal protections for European workers against surveillance are substantially stronger than those available to American workers, reflecting a different underlying philosophical framework about privacy as a right versus a commercial negotiation.

The Algorithmic Manager

The most significant development in workplace surveillance is not the monitoring itself but the degree to which monitoring data is feeding directly into management decisions through automated systems. Algorithmic management, the replacement of human judgment in management decisions by algorithmic systems, is progressing rapidly in sectors where work is easily quantifiable.

Uber and Lyft drivers experience algorithmic management almost purely: rating systems, fare calculations, route suggestions, and in some cases deactivation decisions, are made or heavily constrained by algorithm. Drivers whose ratings fall below threshold receive automated warnings and ultimately face automated deactivation. The appeal process for algorithmic decisions is typically opaque and slow, with human review difficult to access.

Algorithmic management is expanding into sectors where it was not previously present. AI systems that flag employees for performance review based on behavioral metrics, systems that generate disciplinary action recommendations based on deviation from productivity norms and systems that schedule workers based on demand predictions are all forms of algorithmic management. The common thread is that human judgment is displaced by algorithmic calculation, with the employee's relationship to their employer increasingly mediated by a system that has no capacity for the contextual understanding that human management, at its best, can provide.

The algorithmic manager does not know that you were less productive last Tuesday because your child was sick and you were distracted. It does not know that your lower-than-average handle time this week reflects the fact that you are dealing with unusually complex customer issues. It does not know that your output quality is substantially better than your output quantity metrics reflect. It knows what it measures. What it measures is not the complete picture of your work. The gap between the metric and the reality is where significant injustices occur.

Resistance and the Power Asymmetry

Workers have developed a range of responses to workplace surveillance that reflect both the genuine constraints they face and the creativity that constraint produces. Mouse jigglers, hardware devices and software programs that simulate mouse movement to prevent automatic screen lock, have become popular among remote workers whose monitoring software uses mouse inactivity as a proxy for being away from work. The existence and popularity of these devices reveals both the limitation of activity metrics as productivity proxies and the adversarial dynamic that surveillance creates.

Union organizing has become one of the most significant responses to algorithmic management in sectors where collective action is possible. Amazon warehouse workers in Staten Island organized a successful union campaign in 2022 partly on the basis of objections to the pace and surveillance conditions of warehouse work. The union organizing at Apple retail locations and Starbucks has included surveillance and monitoring conditions among the grievances driving organizing activity. The connection between algorithmic management and labor organizing is not coincidental. Algorithmic management creates consistent, documentable grievances that are shared across a workforce, which provides organizing material that personal management conflicts do not.

The power asymmetry between employers and workers in most surveillance disputes is substantial. Individual workers have very limited options: comply, attempt to negotiate through existing channels or leave. The legal protections are minimal in most US jurisdictions. The information asymmetry is large, with employers knowing exactly what data they collect and how they use it while workers often do not. The cost of leaving is high, particularly in a tight economic environment. The practical options for individual workers are therefore mostly limited to leaving the organization or accepting the surveillance.

The structural responses, labor organizing and legislative action, operate on different timescales but are more likely to produce lasting change. The legislative trajectory in Europe suggests what is possible when there is sufficient political will to regulate employer surveillance as a power relationship requiring legal constraints rather than a purely private business decision. The American legislative environment has moved more slowly, but state-level action is accelerating as the visibility of surveillance practices grows. Workers who understand both their current rights and the direction of regulatory change are better positioned to advocate for protections that do not yet exist but are increasingly achievable.
