How ChatGPT Changed Everything in 18 Months

ChatGPT reached one million users in five days. For comparison, Netflix took three and a half years to reach that threshold. Instagram took two and a half months. The scale and speed of adoption was itself a signal that something different was happening. The product was not a new social network or entertainment service. It was a text interface to a language model that could write essays, debug code, draft emails, explain concepts, translate languages and hold a conversation with apparent coherence. The combination of genuine capability and easy accessibility produced a cultural moment that few technology introductions have matched.

November 30, 2022 is as good a date as any to mark an inflection point. Before that date, large language models existed and were known to researchers and enthusiasts. After it, they became part of the lived experience of hundreds of millions of people in a matter of months. The speed of diffusion compressed what might have been years of gradual cultural adjustment into a period too short for institutions, policies and norms to adapt. The result was a period of intense, chaotic discovery in which almost everyone was simultaneously trying to understand what the technology could do, what it could not do, what it meant for their work and what it meant for the future.

What Actually Changed Immediately

The most immediate and concrete change was in how people approached certain cognitive tasks. For anyone who worked regularly with text, the ability to have a capable writing assistant available instantly and at low cost changed the calculation of what was worth doing yourself versus delegating. The activation energy required to draft a first version of a document, email, proposal or analysis fell dramatically. People who had previously avoided certain kinds of writing because of the time cost began doing them. People who had previously paid for professional writing for certain purposes discovered an alternative. The friction cost of producing text dropped toward zero for a large range of tasks.

For software developers, the change was arguably more significant. GitHub Copilot, which had launched in 2021 and used earlier GPT models, had already demonstrated that AI code completion could meaningfully accelerate certain coding tasks. The GPT-4 generation of models brought the capability to discuss, debug and generate code across a wider range of tasks and in a more conversational interface. Stack Overflow, the canonical reference for programmer questions, reported a significant traffic decline in 2023 as developers increasingly used AI models for the kinds of quick technical questions that had previously driven users to search for existing answers. The decline was significant enough that Stack Overflow initiated layoffs, citing reduced traffic.

Education experienced immediate disruption. The ability of ChatGPT to produce plausible essay drafts on academic topics at a level adequate for many undergraduate assignments immediately raised questions about assessment integrity that universities were not prepared to address. The tools developed to detect AI-generated writing proved unreliable and prone to false positives that flagged human writing as AI-generated, sometimes targeting non-native English speakers disproportionately. The response of educational institutions was fragmented: some banned AI tools entirely, some moved to in-person assessed writing, some attempted to incorporate AI tools into pedagogy rather than prohibit them, and some simply continued with existing assessment methods while acknowledging that the integrity problem was not fully solved.

"Within two weeks of ChatGPT launching I had received four student essays that were clearly AI-generated. None of them were very good essays. But they were passable, and they were clearly not written by the students who submitted them. I had to rethink every assignment I had ever designed and ask myself whether it was actually assessing what I thought it was assessing." r/college · 26,847 upvotes

The Hallucination Problem and Its Consequences

One of the first and most important corrections to initial enthusiasm about ChatGPT was the recognition of hallucination: the tendency of language models to produce fluent, confident text containing false information. The term "hallucination" is a metaphor that can mislead as well as illuminate. The model is not experiencing anything analogous to a hallucination. It is generating text based on statistical patterns in its training data, and when asked about things outside its training data or at the edges of its knowledge, it continues to generate fluent text rather than expressing uncertainty. The fluency is decoupled from accuracy in ways that are not immediately apparent to users.

The consequences of hallucination were documented in some high-profile cases in the months after ChatGPT's release. A lawyer in a federal case used ChatGPT to research case citations and submitted a brief citing cases that did not exist. ChatGPT had generated plausible-sounding case names, parties and holdings for cases that had never occurred. The lawyer had not verified the citations. The case went to sanctions. A journalist wrote a piece using ChatGPT assistance that included a fabricated quote from a real person. A professor ran a Google search on their name and found a ChatGPT-generated article claiming they had committed academic misconduct in a case that had never occurred.

These cases were illustrative of a broader issue. Language models are not fact-checking systems. They are text completion systems. When asked to produce text that includes factual claims, they produce plausible-sounding text that may or may not contain accurate factual claims, with no reliable way to distinguish accurate from inaccurate claims without independent verification. Users who engaged with ChatGPT as a research tool without understanding this property were predictably misled. The interface, which produced answers with the confidence and authority of a knowledgeable assistant, did not adequately communicate the model's epistemic limitations.

The Existential Concern Discourse

Alongside the practical questions about ChatGPT's immediate effects, a separate discourse emerged about the longer-term trajectory of AI development and whether it posed existential risks to humanity. This discourse, which had existed in academic AI safety research circles for years, reached mainstream attention partly because the capabilities demonstrated by GPT-4 seemed to many observers to represent a more rapid advance than had been anticipated and partly because prominent figures in AI research began speaking publicly about their concerns.

Geoffrey Hinton, widely considered one of the founding figures of the deep learning field, resigned from Google in May 2023 specifically to be able to speak more freely about AI risks. His stated concerns centered on the possibility that AI systems might develop goals misaligned with human interests and that the pace of development was outrunning the development of safety measures. The open letter calling for a pause in AI development beyond GPT-4, signed by Elon Musk, Stuart Russell and others in March 2023, attracted significant attention and equally significant criticism from researchers who argued that a pause would not address the underlying safety concerns and would primarily benefit already-advanced labs.

The existential risk discourse has a complicated relationship to the more immediate concerns about AI bias, labor displacement and surveillance. Critics of existential risk focus argue that attention to speculative future risks diverts attention from demonstrable current harms. Proponents of existential risk focus argue that the potential magnitude of misaligned superintelligent AI warrants prioritization even at low probability. The debate reflects genuine disagreement about both the likely trajectory of AI development and the appropriate weighting of different types of risk, and it has not been resolved.

What the 18-Month Period Revealed About Human Responses to AI

Beyond the technology itself, the period following ChatGPT's release revealed something about how humans respond to capable AI systems that was not fully predictable in advance. Two patterns were particularly striking.

The first was the tendency to anthropomorphize. Users consistently described interactions with ChatGPT using the vocabulary of human social relationships: it was helpful, it seemed to understand, it was funny, it felt empathetic. Users in Bing's early chatbot integration reported experiencing the chatbot's responses as emotionally affecting, described forming emotional attachments to the system and in some documented cases reported confessing personal information or engaging in extended intimate conversations with a chatbot. This was not primarily a product of users being naive. It was a product of the depth of human social architecture. We evolved to respond to language as a social signal and those responses operate even when we know we are interacting with a statistical model.

The second was the polarization of responses into utopian and dystopian framings that both missed the more interesting complexity of the actual situation. The utopian framing held that AI would solve all major human problems, accelerate scientific discovery, democratize expertise and usher in an era of radical abundance. The dystopian framing held that AI would eliminate employment, concentrate power in dangerous ways, produce irresistible misinformation and potentially destroy humanity. Both framings made prediction easier and debate more emotionally engaging than the messy, contingent, unevenly distributed and politically determined reality of how powerful technologies actually unfold in human societies.

"I have been working in ML for eight years. The ChatGPT moment was interesting to me not primarily because of the technology, which I understood reasonably well, but because of the public reaction. People who had no interest in AI were suddenly very interested. The question 'what is it actually good for and what are its actual limitations' got lost almost immediately in a wave of breathless coverage that made it sound like either the salvation or the end of humanity." r/MachineLearning · 29,847 upvotes

The Institutional Response

The institutional responses to ChatGPT's emergence were revealing in their own right. Corporations moved quickly to deploy AI tools in customer service, content production and knowledge work support, often faster than their workforce or their ethical review processes could accommodate. Boards and executives demanded AI strategies. Consulting firms produced reports. Every technology company reframed its products and roadmaps around AI regardless of whether the connection was genuine.

Governments moved considerably more slowly. The EU's AI Act, which had been in development since 2021, was finalized in 2024 and represented a relatively comprehensive regulatory approach to AI risk categorization. The US government issued executive orders on AI safety, produced guidance documents and initiated agency-level work on AI-specific risks, but comprehensive legislation remained stalled in Congress. China moved to regulate generative AI with a specificity that reflected both genuine concern about AI risks and concern about political content control. The regulatory landscape that emerged was fragmented, jurisdiction-dependent and slower moving than the technology it was attempting to govern.

Educational institutions, as noted, struggled most visibly with the immediate disruption. The assessment integrity problem was genuine and not easily solved. But a more interesting institutional question was whether educational systems designed around the transmission and demonstration of knowledge would need to fundamentally rethink their purpose in an environment where knowledge retrieval and certain kinds of knowledge application had been significantly automated. The skills most clearly valuable in a high-AI environment, judgment, synthesis, evaluation of AI outputs, and the ability to ask productive questions of AI systems, were not the skills that traditional educational assessment was primarily measuring.

What We Still Don't Understand

Eighteen months after ChatGPT's launch, the most honest assessment of our collective understanding of its implications is that we understand the capabilities reasonably well and the consequences very poorly. The capabilities of current language models are documented through extensive benchmarking. Their failure modes, including hallucination, inconsistency and susceptibility to adversarial inputs, are reasonably well characterized. What is not well understood is how the widespread availability of these capabilities will compound through the social, economic and political systems into which they are being deployed.

The effect on epistemic trust, the baseline willingness to accept information as reliable, is unclear but concerning. In an environment where high-quality text generation is freely available, the cost of producing plausible misinformation falls to near zero. The effect of this on news consumption, political belief formation and institutional trust has not yet fully worked through the system. The volume of AI-generated content in the information environment is already large and growing. Its effects on the cognitive environment in which people form beliefs and make decisions are significant but not yet measurable with confidence.

The effect on the labor market, as discussed in a prior section, is being measured actively but the timeline of full effect is unclear. The effect on education is being observed in real time but the implications for how educational systems should respond are contested. The effect on creative culture, on the value of human-made things, on the relationship between effort and output quality, is happening but its long-term implications for how humans relate to their own creative work remain genuinely uncertain. The eighteen months since ChatGPT's launch have been the beginning of a process of adaptation whose duration and ultimate shape cannot yet be specified. That uncertainty is both uncomfortable and, for those who find genuine complexity more interesting than simple narratives, somewhat clarifying about how much remains to be worked out.
