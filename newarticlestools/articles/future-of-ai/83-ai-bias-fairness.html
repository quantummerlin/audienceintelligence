AI Bias Isn't a Bug, It's a Feature of Bad Data

29,473 upvotes · by u/algorithmic_justice · 12 min read
▲ 29,473 people found this relatable
Advertisement

When an AI system produces a discriminatory outcome, the natural instinct is to describe it as a malfunction. The system is biased, which implies deviation from intended operation. Fix the bias and the system works correctly. This framing is not exactly wrong, but it obscures something important about where the problem actually lives. Discriminatory AI systems are typically performing precisely as designed, optimizing the objective they were given using the data they were trained on. The problem is not that the system is broken. The problem is that the objective was wrong or the data was contaminated with historical injustice, and those are much harder problems to fix than a software bug.

Understanding AI bias requires understanding how machine learning systems work at a basic level. These systems learn patterns from historical data. They identify features in training examples that correlate with target outcomes and use those features to make predictions or classifications on new examples. If the historical data reflects discriminatory patterns, the learned model will replicate those patterns. If Black defendants were historically given harsher sentences for comparable crimes, a model trained to predict "risk" based on historical outcomes will learn that being Black correlates with higher risk. If women were historically passed over for promotions, a model trained on past hiring data will learn that being a woman correlates with lower promotion probability. The model is doing exactly what it was asked to do. It is learning from history, and the history is unjust.

COMPAS and the Criminal Justice Case

The clearest and most studied example of consequential AI bias is COMPAS, the Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions tool. COMPAS is a risk assessment algorithm used by courts in multiple US states to assess the likelihood that a defendant will re-offend. Judges use COMPAS scores in bail, sentencing and parole decisions. The scores are presented with an air of scientific objectivity that contrasts with the acknowledged subjectivity of judicial discretion.

A 2016 ProPublica investigation by journalists Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Lauren Kirchner and Surya Mattu analyzed COMPAS scores for more than seven thousand defendants in Broward County, Florida. Their analysis found that Black defendants were nearly twice as likely as white defendants to be falsely flagged as high risk when they did not subsequently re-offend, while white defendants were more likely to be falsely flagged as low risk when they did go on to re-offend. The false positive rate for Black defendants and the false negative rate for white defendants were both substantially higher than for the other group.

Northpointe, the company that developed COMPAS, disputed the ProPublica analysis. A subsequent academic exchange revealed something important about AI fairness: multiple definitions of "fair" exist, they are mathematically incompatible with each other under most real-world conditions and satisfying one definition of fairness necessarily violates at least one other definition when base rates differ across groups. The company argued that COMPAS was fair under one definition, equal calibration across groups. ProPublica argued it was unfair under another definition, equal false positive rates across groups. Both were right about their respective metrics. The disagreement was not about math. It was about which definition of fairness matters most when making decisions about human beings' liberty.

"The thing that gets me about algorithmic risk assessment in the justice system is that nobody knows why a specific score was assigned. The defendant doesn't know. Their lawyer often doesn't know. The judge often doesn't fully understand the model. But the score is treated as objective. A number produced by an opaque algorithm carries more rhetorical weight than anything the defendant can say about their own life." r/legaladvice · 21,847 upvotes

Hiring Algorithms and the Amazon Experiment

Amazon developed a machine learning tool between 2014 and 2017 to automate the initial screening of job applicants. The system was trained on resumes submitted to Amazon over a ten-year period and patterns in who was hired from that pool. The training data reflected Amazon's historical hiring outcomes, which were predominantly male, particularly in technical roles. The system learned that resumes mentioning women's colleges or the word "women's" in phrases like "women's chess club" were associated with not being hired in the historical data. It penalized those terms accordingly. Amazon discovered the bias, attempted to fix it and ultimately scrapped the project when engineers concluded they could not guarantee the system was not making gender-based discriminatory decisions.

The Amazon case is instructive because the company was not trying to discriminate. It was trying to automate a tedious human process. The discrimination emerged from the training data without anyone intending it. The system learned a proxy for gender, the correlation between certain resume terms and historical hiring outcomes, and used that proxy to make predictions. This is the core technical mechanism of AI bias: the model learns to use proxies for protected characteristics when those proxies correlate with historical outcomes influenced by discrimination.

The proxy problem is particularly resistant to simple fixes. Removing explicit protected characteristics like race and gender from the model does not prevent discrimination if the model has access to features that are correlated with those characteristics. ZIP code correlates with race in geographically segregated societies. Educational institution correlates with race through historical access inequalities. First name correlates with race and ethnicity. Word choice in personal statements can correlate with gender or socioeconomic background. The correlations between apparently neutral features and protected characteristics are deep, pervasive and not fully removable from most real-world datasets.

Facial Recognition and the Accuracy Gap

Joy Buolamwini's research at the MIT Media Lab, published in 2018 with Timnit Gebru and known as the Gender Shades study, documented dramatic accuracy disparities in commercial facial analysis systems across demographic groups. The study tested three major commercial facial analysis systems on their ability to classify gender from photographs. The systems achieved error rates of less than one percent for light-skinned male faces. For dark-skinned female faces, error rates ranged from 20 to 35 percent.

The explanation was training data. Facial recognition systems are trained on datasets of labeled faces, and the benchmark datasets used in academic research and commercial development were heavily skewed toward lighter-skinned faces and male faces. A system trained primarily on faces that look a certain way will perform better on faces that resemble its training data and worse on faces that differ from it. The accuracy gap was not an intentional design choice. It was a predictable outcome of training on unrepresentative data.

The consequences are not abstract. Law enforcement agencies in the United States have used facial recognition systems in criminal investigations, and multiple wrongful arrest cases have been documented in which Black individuals were falsely identified by facial recognition systems and arrested based on that identification. Robert Williams in Detroit, Michael Oliver in New Jersey and Nijeer Parks in New Jersey were all wrongfully arrested after facial recognition identifications later determined to be erroneous. In each case the false identification involved a Black man being incorrectly matched to a suspect by a facial recognition system with documented accuracy disparities across demographic groups. These were not statistical abstractions. They were arrests, detentions and in some cases criminal charges based on AI systems that performed measurably worse on the demographic characteristics of the people they were used against.

"I was wrongfully arrested because a facial recognition algorithm said I was someone else. I was held for thirty hours before it got sorted out. The detective told me that the 'computer matched me.' As if that settles it. As if the computer can't be wrong. But the data on these systems is clear: they are substantially less accurate on Black faces. I happened to be on the wrong end of that statistic." r/news · 38,294 upvotes

Lending, Housing and Algorithmic Redlining

Algorithmic bias in lending and housing has received significant attention from regulators and researchers because of its direct connection to wealth accumulation and the specific history of racial discrimination in housing markets. The Fair Housing Act and Equal Credit Opportunity Act prohibit discrimination based on race, color, national origin and other protected characteristics in housing and lending. The challenge for regulators is that algorithmic systems can produce discriminatory outcomes through statistically neutral proxies in ways that are harder to document and prove than explicit discriminatory intent.

A 2019 analysis by the Markup found significant disparities in mortgage approval rates across racial groups when controlling for financial factors. Black and Latino applicants were denied mortgages at higher rates than white applicants with comparable financial profiles across multiple major lenders. The algorithms used for mortgage underwriting incorporate credit scores, debt-to-income ratios and property valuations that are themselves influenced by historical discrimination. Property in predominantly Black neighborhoods is systematically undervalued by appraisal processes that rely on comparable sales in areas that were historically redlined. A loan applicant whose property has been undervalued by discriminatory appraisal practices may be denied a mortgage not because of any decision about their race but because the downstream consequences of historical discrimination have contaminated the data their application is evaluated against.

Automated tenant screening tools used by landlords present similar issues. These tools incorporate credit history, eviction records and criminal history into tenancy decisions. Eviction and criminal records are distributed unevenly across demographic groups in ways that reflect both historical discrimination and current policing and enforcement disparities. An algorithm that uses these records without accounting for their discriminatory origins will produce discriminatory outcomes even when the intent is simply to assess tenancy risk from available data.

Healthcare and the Life-or-Death Stakes

AI bias in healthcare is perhaps the highest-stakes application because the consequences can involve direct harm to health outcomes. A widely cited 2019 study in Science by Obermeyer and colleagues examined a healthcare algorithm widely used by US hospitals to identify patients who need complex care management. The algorithm used healthcare spending as a proxy for health needs: the assumption was that sicker patients spend more on healthcare. The study found that the algorithm systematically underestimated the health needs of Black patients by a large margin, with the result that Black patients identified as equally needing care management as white patients were in fact substantially sicker by medical measures.

The bias arose from the proxy. Black patients and white patients with equivalent health needs do not generate equivalent healthcare spending, because structural barriers, including distrust of the healthcare system based on historical abuses, transportation barriers and insurance disparities, reduce healthcare utilization among Black patients independently of health status. A proxy that uses spending to estimate need will underestimate need for populations whose utilization is suppressed relative to their health status. The algorithm was not designed to discriminate. It was designed to identify high-need patients efficiently. The discrimination emerged from the inadequacy of the proxy for the population it was applied to.

The implications of automated diagnostic and treatment recommendation systems operating with demographic biases are significant and not fully mapped. AI systems are being deployed or piloted in clinical decision support across specialties. Their training data reflects the demographics of historical research populations, which have historically been skewed toward white male subjects. Drug dosing algorithms, disease risk calculators and treatment recommendation systems trained on these populations may perform differently across demographic groups in ways that have not been fully documented.

Accountability and the Path Forward

The regulatory response to AI bias has been developing but remains fragmented. The EU's AI Act, adopted in 2024, classifies high-risk AI applications including those in hiring, credit, law enforcement and healthcare, and requires conformity assessments, transparency documentation and human oversight mechanisms for these applications. The US has issued executive orders and agency guidance on AI but lacks comprehensive binding legislation specifically addressing AI bias.

The technical research on algorithmic fairness has produced several concrete approaches to reducing bias. Pre-processing approaches modify training data to reduce historical bias before model training. In-processing approaches build fairness constraints into the training objective. Post-processing approaches adjust model outputs to equalize metrics across demographic groups. Each approach has limitations: they address different definitions of fairness that may be mutually inconsistent, and they all involve trade-offs between fairness metrics and overall accuracy that require normative judgments about which trade-offs are acceptable.

The most important accountability mechanism in the near term is audit. Independent algorithmic auditing, in which researchers with access to model inputs, outputs and documentation assess whether a system produces discriminatory outcomes, is the primary mechanism available for detecting and documenting AI bias in deployed systems. The companies that develop and sell AI systems have limited incentives to conduct thorough independent audits that might reveal problems with their products. Regulatory requirements for mandatory third-party auditing of high-stakes AI systems, similar to financial auditing requirements for public companies, would create the external accountability that voluntary auditing cannot guarantee.

The deeper issue is that AI systems deployed in high-stakes domains are making consequential decisions about people's lives with limited transparency, limited accountability and limited recourse for people affected by errors. The fairness problem is not primarily a technical problem. It is a power problem. The people most likely to be harmed by biased AI systems are the people with the least power to challenge those systems and the least representation in the organizations that build and deploy them. Technical fixes to AI bias that are not accompanied by structural changes in who has power over AI development and deployment are likely to be insufficient to the scale of the problem they are addressing.
