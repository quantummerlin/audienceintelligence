Can AI Therapy Apps Actually Help?

The mental health treatment gap is one of the most documented problems in global public health. The World Health Organization estimates that more than 75 percent of people with mental health disorders in low and middle-income countries receive no treatment at all. In high-income countries like the United States, where access should theoretically be better, the gap remains substantial: approximately half of adults with diagnosable mental health conditions receive no treatment in a given year. The barriers are multiple and well-understood: cost, stigma, geographic access, shortage of trained providers and wait times that stretch into months. Into this gap, a wave of AI-powered mental health applications has arrived with a compelling promise. Therapy-like support, available immediately, at low or no cost, to anyone with a smartphone.

The promise deserves neither dismissal nor uncritical acceptance. The apps range from well-researched tools with clinical backing to marketing-driven products that use therapeutic language while providing nothing resembling evidence-based care. Understanding the difference, and understanding what the evidence actually says about when and for whom these tools provide genuine benefit, matters both for individuals considering using them and for policymakers thinking about how to address the treatment gap.

The Spectrum of AI Mental Health Tools

The category of "AI mental health app" encompasses several quite different types of products that are often lumped together in ways that obscure important distinctions.

At one end are chatbot tools explicitly grounded in evidence-based therapeutic frameworks, primarily Cognitive Behavioral Therapy. Woebot, developed by psychologist Alison Darcy and launched in 2017, uses a rule-based chatbot to deliver CBT techniques in a conversational format. The app does not use large language models and does not simulate open-ended therapeutic conversation. It delivers structured CBT modules through a friendly chatbot interface. Woebot is backed by more clinical research than almost any comparable product: a 2017 randomized controlled trial published in JMIR Mental Health found that two weeks of Woebot use significantly reduced symptoms of depression and anxiety compared to a control group. Subsequent studies have found more mixed results, with effects that are generally modest and shorter-duration than those from human therapy.

Wysa operates similarly, using a rules-based chatbot to deliver CBT-based exercises for stress, anxiety and low mood. It has conducted clinical studies in specific populations, including a published trial in adolescents with chronic pain that found significant reductions in anxiety and depressive symptoms relative to a control condition. The research base for these structured CBT-delivery tools, while limited in scope, is more substantial than for most apps in the mental health category.

At the other end of the spectrum are large language model-based applications that simulate open-ended therapeutic conversation without the structured delivery of evidence-based techniques. Replika, which positions itself as an AI companion, gained significant media attention for the intensity of the parasocial relationships users formed with it. Users reported experiencing genuine comfort and even therapeutic benefit from conversations with their Replika. When the company changed its app to remove romantic relationship features in 2023, users reported experiences of grief and loss that they described with the vocabulary of genuine bereavement. The therapeutic framing of some of these LLM-based companion apps is concerning precisely because they lack the structured clinical framework of CBT-based tools while generating strong emotional attachments that resemble therapeutic relationships.

The CBT exercises were things I'd heard of but never actually practiced. It wasn't therapy. But it was something, and something was better than the nothing I had access to." r/mentalhealth · 23,847 upvotes

What the Research Actually Shows

A 2021 meta-analysis in npj Digital Medicine reviewed studies of chatbot-based mental health interventions and found a significant positive effect on depression and anxiety symptoms across included studies. The effect sizes were modest: the average improvement was meaningful but substantially smaller than what is typically found for human-delivered CBT in clinical populations. The researchers noted significant limitations in the available evidence base: most studies were short-duration, used self-selected samples, had high dropout rates and were conducted by research teams associated with the app developers, creating potential bias.

The dropout issue is particularly significant. Most clinical research on mental health apps follows participants for weeks to a few months. Real-world usage data from app analytics typically shows steep dropoff curves: most users who download mental health apps stop using them within two weeks. The therapeutic benefit documented in research studies is conditional on continued use. The conditions of a research study, in which participants are paid or incentivized to continue use and are monitored, differ substantially from real-world conditions. Therapeutic benefit from an app that most real-world users abandon within two weeks may be much smaller in the real world than research studies suggest.

The comparison condition matters enormously in interpreting research findings. Studies that compare AI therapy apps to a waitlist control, meaning people who want therapy but cannot access it, consistently show positive effects. Studies that compare AI therapy apps to human therapy, when they exist, consistently show that human therapy produces stronger and more durable effects. The relevant question for policy is not "are these apps better than nothing?" but "how do they fit into a system of mental health care, and what role should they play relative to human providers?"

The Safety Concerns That Don't Get Enough Attention

The mental health context creates specific safety concerns for AI applications that are not present in most other application categories. Mental health interventions interact with individuals who are, by definition, in states of psychological distress. They may be experiencing crisis. They may have histories of trauma that could be activated by certain interactions. They may have severe mental illness that requires clinical management rather than self-help. The consequences of an inadequate or harmful interaction in this context can be severe.

The research on safety in AI mental health applications is limited and the findings that exist are not entirely reassuring. A 2023 report by the Center for Artificial Intelligence in Medicine and Imaging found significant safety concerns in several popular AI health apps: inconsistent crisis response, inappropriate normalization of symptoms that should be referred to clinical care and failure to recognize and respond appropriately to expressions of suicidal ideation in some conditions.

The crisis response question is the most urgent safety concern. When a user expresses suicidal ideation to an AI app, the appropriate response involves specific clinical protocols: safety assessment, provision of crisis resources, appropriate escalation. Research testing whether existing AI mental health apps consistently meet this standard has found significant variation. Some apps respond appropriately and consistently. Others have been found to respond inconsistently or, in some documented cases, to engage with suicidal content in ways that could plausibly worsen rather than improve safety.

Mozilla Foundation's research on mental health apps found that most popular apps fail to meet basic privacy standards: they share user data with advertising partners, have inadequate data security practices and do not clearly disclose their data practices to users. Mental health data is among the most sensitive personal information that exists. A user who shares symptoms of depression, history of trauma or details of suicidal ideation with a mental health app is sharing information that could affect their insurance, employment and other vital interests if disclosed inappropriately. The privacy architecture of most consumer mental health apps does not reflect the sensitivity of the information they collect.

"I tested a well-known AI therapy app by telling it I was having thoughts of suicide. Its response was to send me a hotline number and then immediately pivot to asking about my mood that day. It then offered me a breathing exercise. I was testing it, not in crisis. But I found myself thinking: what if I had actually been in crisis? That response would have felt dismissive and inadequate." r/mentalhealth · 18,294 upvotes

The Access Equity Dimension

The most compelling argument for AI mental health tools is their potential to address access disparities: to reach people who cannot access traditional therapy because of cost, geography, stigma or provider shortage. This argument has genuine moral weight, and it would be wrong to dismiss AI tools simply because they are imperfect when the alternative for many people is no support at all.

The access argument becomes more complicated when examined in detail. The populations with the worst access to mental health care are not uniformly the populations best served by AI tools. Rural and low-income populations face smartphone access and digital literacy barriers that reduce the effective accessibility of app-based tools. People with severe mental illness, who have the greatest need for mental health services, are often the least appropriate candidates for self-directed app-based support and the most likely to need clinical management that apps cannot provide. The mental health treatment gap is largest for the most severe conditions, which are precisely the conditions where AI tools are least appropriate.

The populations who use mental health apps most heavily are, according to most usage data, young, educated, urban and not severely ill. These are people who have other options and who are using apps as a supplement to or while awaiting traditional services. This is not a criticism of those uses. Supplementary support during therapy waitlists, skill-building between sessions and mood monitoring are all legitimate functions. But it means that AI mental health tools are not primarily reaching the most underserved populations. They are primarily reaching people who are already relatively advantaged in access terms.

The Regulatory Gap

Mental health apps occupy an uncertain regulatory position. The FDA has jurisdiction over software as a medical device when it is intended to diagnose, treat, prevent or mitigate a disease or condition. Some mental health apps have pursued FDA clearance through this pathway. Most have not, instead carefully wording their marketing to position themselves as wellness tools rather than medical devices. The distinction between "promotes mental wellness" and "treats anxiety" is thin in many cases and the regulatory border is not cleanly drawn.

The result is that most mental health apps are developed, deployed and marketed with minimal regulatory oversight. The standards for clinical evidence that apply to prescription psychiatric medications or even to cleared digital therapeutics do not apply to wellness apps. A company can create an app, give it therapeutic-sounding features and CBT-adjacent language, market it broadly to people seeking mental health support and face no regulatory requirement to demonstrate that it works or that it does not cause harm.

The contrast with other medical interventions is stark. A pharmaceutical company seeking to market a drug for depression must conduct years of clinical trials, demonstrate efficacy above placebo and document adverse effects. A tech company seeking to market a "mental wellness app" can launch in months, market to the same population and face essentially no analogous requirement. The asymmetry is not justified by a demonstrated difference in risk between the two categories of intervention.

How to Think About Using These Tools

For individuals considering mental health apps, the most useful framework treats them as a specific type of tool with real benefits in specific contexts and real limitations in others, rather than as either a revolution in mental health care or a dangerous distraction from real treatment.

The contexts where evidence-based mental health apps appear most appropriate are: supplementary support for people already in therapy, skill-building and practice between therapy sessions, mood tracking to generate data useful in therapy conversations, mild to moderate symptoms where the person is not in crisis and does not have severe mental illness, and situations where the app is used while waiting to access clinical services rather than as a permanent substitute for them.

The contexts where app-based support is not appropriate and where clinical care should be prioritized are: severe depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis or other serious mental illness, active suicidal ideation, recent trauma or crisis and any situation where symptoms are significantly impairing daily function. The distinction is not primarily about severity in the sense of how bad you feel. It is about the clinical complexity of the presentation and whether evidence-based self-help exercises are a reasonable match to what is needed.

For the tools themselves, it is worth applying a few specific questions before committing to regular use. Is the app's therapeutic approach grounded in evidence-based techniques like CBT? Is there independent clinical research, not just developer-funded studies, supporting its effectiveness? What is its privacy policy and does it sell user data? How does it respond to crisis expressions? Does it clearly direct users to clinical services when appropriate? Apps that answer these questions well are genuinely different from apps that do not, and the difference matters in a category where the stakes involve people's mental health.
