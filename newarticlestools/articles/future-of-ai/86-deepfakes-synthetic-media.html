Deepfakes and the Crisis of Synthetic Reality

31,847 upvotes · by u/authentic_media_watch · 12 min read
▲ 31,847 people found this relatable
Advertisement

In January 2024, sexually explicit AI-generated images of Taylor Swift circulated on X and other platforms, reaching tens of millions of views before being removed. The images were fabricated. Swift had no involvement in their creation and had not consented to their use. The episode received significant media attention partly because of Swift's cultural prominence and partly because it made viscerally clear something that researchers and advocates had been documenting for years: the technology to create convincing false images and video of real people was now cheap, accessible and being used at scale to cause harm.

Deepfakes, the generic term for AI-generated synthetic media depicting real people, are not new. The technology emerged from research into generative adversarial networks around 2017. What has changed is the threshold for production. Creating a convincing deepfake video in 2018 required significant technical skill and substantial computing resources. By 2024, dozens of consumer applications could produce convincing synthetic media in minutes with no technical expertise required. The production cost barrier that had limited the technology's harmful applications has effectively collapsed. The implications are working through multiple domains simultaneously.

The Nonconsensual Intimate Image Problem

The most prevalent harmful application of deepfake technology is the creation of nonconsensual intimate imagery, sexually explicit fake images and videos depicting real people without their consent. Research by the nonprofit organization Sensity AI estimated in 2019 that 96 percent of deepfake videos online were nonconsensual pornography. More recent analyses by the Stanford Internet Observatory and similar research groups have found that the absolute volume has grown enormously since 2019 while the proportion has diversified somewhat, but nonconsensual intimate imagery remains the dominant use case for deepfake technology.

The victims are overwhelmingly women. The harms are severe and documented: emotional distress, reputational damage, professional consequences when images reach colleagues or employers, relationship damage and in many documented cases the use of deepfake images as a tool of coercive control and harassment by abusers. The digital permanence of synthetic intimate images, the difficulty of comprehensive removal from the internet once an image has circulated and the ease of re-creation if the original is removed, means that the harm does not end when images are taken down.

Legal frameworks have lagged the technology significantly. As of 2024, roughly half of US states have laws specifically prohibiting nonconsensual deepfake intimate imagery, with significant variation in what is covered and how severely violations are penalized. Federal legislation has been proposed repeatedly without passage. The UK passed legislation in the Online Safety Act criminalizing the creation of sexually explicit deepfake images without consent. Australia and several EU member states have created or strengthened relevant laws. The global patchwork of laws creates significant enforcement gaps, particularly when creators are in jurisdictions without relevant laws and victims are elsewhere.

Political Deepfakes and Election Integrity

The electoral dimension of deepfake technology has received substantial attention from researchers, election officials and regulators, though the actual impact of political deepfakes on elections remains a subject of active research and genuine uncertainty.

The documented cases of deepfakes being used in electoral contexts are numerous. During the 2024 New Hampshire primary, robocalls using an AI-generated voice mimicking President Biden told Democratic voters not to vote in the primary. The calls were traced to a political consultant who was subsequently charged under federal law. The episode demonstrated both the real deployment of synthetic audio in electoral manipulation attempts and the existence of legal frameworks that can reach at least some perpetrators.

The broader concern about deepfakes and elections is partly about specific fabricated content and partly about the epistemological effect sometimes called the liar's dividend. The liar's dividend is the benefit that the existence of deepfake technology provides to people who want to deny authenticity of real media. When anyone can plausibly claim that a damaging video or audio recording of them is AI-generated, the evidentiary value of authentic recordings is undermined. The politician caught on video saying something embarrassing can plausibly claim it is a deepfake regardless of its authenticity. The liar's dividend may ultimately be more consequential for democratic accountability than specific deepfake deployments.

Provenance technologies, primarily the C2PA standard developed by a coalition including Adobe, Microsoft and the New York Times, aim to address the liar's dividend by cryptographically signing authentic media at the point of creation, allowing downstream verification of provenance. Cameras and phones that implement C2PA sign their outputs in ways that can be verified. The technology is promising but its effectiveness depends on widespread adoption by both creators and consumers, which is a coordination problem that technical standards alone cannot solve.

Voice Cloning and Financial Fraud

AI voice cloning has enabled a wave of fraud schemes that exploit the emotional trust people place in familiar voices. The "grandparent scam," in which scammers pose as grandchildren in distress to extract emergency funds from elderly targets, has been substantially amplified by voice cloning technology. With a few seconds of audio from social media posts, scammers can generate a convincing clone of a grandchild's voice. The emotional urgency of hearing what sounds like your grandchild's voice asking for help overwhelms the skepticism that written scam communications might trigger.

Business email compromise attacks have evolved into business voice compromise attacks using AI voice cloning. A 2019 case documented by the Wall Street Journal involved a UK energy company CEO who transferred approximately $243,000 after receiving a phone call from what he believed was his parent company's chief executive, requesting an urgent wire transfer. The call was an AI voice clone. This case predated the current generation of voice cloning tools, which are significantly more capable and more accessible. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center has documented substantial growth in reported voice fraud losses in the years since voice cloning tools became widely available.

Family emergency scams using voice cloning have produced some of the most emotionally affecting cases. Parents have received calls from convincing clones of their children's voices describing emergencies and requesting money transfers. The psychological impact on victims who realize they have been deceived, and who process what it means that technology can impersonate their child's voice, is substantial beyond the financial loss. The erosion of voice as a reliable authentication signal has practical consequences for personal security practices that most people have not yet incorporated into their daily lives.

Detection and the Arms Race

Significant research effort has gone into developing deepfake detection technology, and substantial progress has been made. Detection systems trained on known deepfake artifacts, specific patterns in how synthetic media differs from authentic media, can achieve high accuracy on test datasets. The practical effectiveness of detection in real-world deployment is considerably more limited.

The fundamental challenge is the arms race dynamic. Detection systems identify artifacts in current-generation synthetic media. Generative systems improve, producing media with fewer and different artifacts. Detection systems are updated. Generative systems improve further. The cycle repeats, with neither side achieving permanent advantage. The current generation of detection systems is relatively effective on deepfakes produced by lower-quality tools and less effective on deepfakes produced by the best available generation tools.

Human detection is even less reliable than automated systems. Multiple studies have found that human observers cannot reliably distinguish deepfake faces and audio from authentic media under realistic viewing conditions. A 2020 study at the MIT Media Lab found that humans identified deepfakes at roughly chance accuracy. A 2023 study by University College London found that with training, participants could reach approximately 80 percent accuracy, but that untrained participants performed barely better than chance. The forensic skills required to reliably detect sophisticated synthetic media are not widely distributed and cannot be assumed in the general population.

The Accountability Architecture Problem

Deepfake harms require responsive legal and platform accountability mechanisms, but the current architecture of both legal systems and platform governance is poorly suited to the speed and scale at which synthetic media can cause harm.

Legal remedies for deepfake harm are available but slow. Defamation, harassment and in relevant jurisdictions nonconsensual intimate image laws provide causes of action. The barriers to using them are significant: identifying anonymous creators, obtaining legal representation, navigating jurisdictional questions when creators and victims are in different places and waiting for court processes to play out while harm continues. The asymmetry between the speed at which synthetic media can be created and distributed and the speed at which legal remedies operate is severe.

Platform takedown mechanisms are faster but inconsistent. Major platforms have policies against nonconsensual intimate imagery and respond to reports with varying speed and completeness. The practical experience of victims reporting synthetic images is that removal is inconsistent, appeal processes are slow and re-upload is common after removal. The verification requirements that make platforms hesitant to remove contested content are less protective of authentic accused when the accuser has compelling synthetic evidence.

The legislative responses that have moved furthest are those addressing nonconsensual intimate imagery, where the harm is most concrete and the politics are most straightforward. Legislative responses to political deepfakes are more contested because of First Amendment considerations in the American context and equivalent protections in other jurisdictions. The line between prohibited political deepfakes and legitimate political satire and commentary is a genuine constitutional challenge that resists simple legislative solutions.

Living in a Post-Authenticity Information Environment

The deeper cultural consequence of synthetic media is not any specific deepfake but the general epistemological shift toward what some researchers call a post-authenticity environment: one in which the default assumption can no longer be that audiovisual media depicts real events. The implications for journalism, legal evidence, personal relationships and collective trust in institutions are significant and not fully worked out.

For journalism, the verification burden for audiovisual material has increased substantially. Standards that were sufficient when the primary concern was altered authentic images need revision for an era of wholly synthetic media. Major news organizations are developing synthetic media policies, investing in provenance verification tools and adding verification steps to visual media workflows. The practical effect is increased cost and time for responsible journalism in a sector already under financial pressure.

For legal evidence, courts in multiple jurisdictions are beginning to grapple with the admissibility and weight of audiovisual evidence in an era when any such evidence could in principle be synthetic. The forensic standards for authenticating digital media are being developed in real time. The risk that both authentic evidence will be excluded and synthetic evidence will be admitted because of inadequate verification standards is real.

For personal trust, the collapse of voice as an authentication signal has practical implications that most people have not yet implemented. Establishing family code words that would not appear in public social media audio, using two-factor verification before transferring money regardless of who is asking and maintaining healthy skepticism about urgent financial requests even from apparently familiar voices are practical responses to a landscape in which voice can no longer be trusted as identity verification. These are adjustments that the technology has made necessary, and the cost of making them, in terms of friction to legitimate communication and the erosion of basic interpersonal trust, is a real harm even for those who never encounter a synthetic media attack directly.
