Dark Patterns: How Apps Manipulate You

▲ 26,749 people found this relatable

You have experienced this. You try to cancel a subscription and the button that should complete the cancellation is pale grey while the "Keep My Subscription" button is bright and prominent. You sign up for a free trial and notice afterward that a box was pre-checked to add a premium tier at checkout. You try to delete an account and are routed through four confirmation screens, each one asking whether you are sure, each one reminding you of what you will lose, while adding a new account takes thirty seconds. You click "Accept All" on a cookie banner because finding the actual preference settings requires navigating a maze of nested menus, and you just want to read the article.

None of these experiences are accidents. They are designed. The field of user experience design has developed a vocabulary for these techniques: dark patterns, a term coined by UX designer Harry Brignull in 2010. Brignull defined dark patterns as user interface design choices that trick users into doing things they did not intend to do, or that prevent them from doing things they intended to do, for the benefit of the company deploying them. The field has grown considerably more sophisticated since 2010, and so has the sophistication of the patterns.

A Taxonomy of Manipulation

Dark patterns come in enough distinct varieties that researchers have developed formal classification systems. Understanding the categories helps you recognize them in the wild, which is the first step to not being captured by them.

Roach motels are among the most common. The pattern is named after the old commercial: you can check in but you can't check out. Signing up for a service is frictionless. Canceling is labyrinthine. The asymmetry is not accidental. Every additional step in a cancellation flow reduces cancellation rates by a measurable amount, and companies A/B test cancellation flows the same way they test sign-up flows. The "cancel anytime" language in subscription marketing technically means the company will eventually cancel your account if you persist. It does not mean cancellation will be easy.

Confirmshaming is the practice of labeling the decline option in a way designed to induce guilt or shame. The classic example is the newsletter sign-up that offers two options: "Yes, I want to save money!" and "No thanks, I prefer to pay full price." The technique works because declining a clearly beneficial offer is cognitively uncomfortable, and users will sometimes opt in to relieve that discomfort rather than because they genuinely want the service. A 2020 study in the Journal of Usability Studies found that confirmshaming increased opt-in rates by 35 percent compared to neutral decline options, and that users who opted in through confirmshamed interfaces reported lower satisfaction with the resulting service than users who opted in through neutral interfaces.

Hidden costs are a dark pattern with direct financial consequences. The Booking.com and hotel industry practice of displaying room prices without resort fees or cleaning fees, adding them only at the final checkout step, has been documented extensively and is the subject of ongoing FTC action. Ticket platforms that add service fees, delivery fees and facility charges totaling twenty to thirty percent of the face price at the last stage of checkout use the sunk cost of having already selected seats and entered payment information to reduce abandonment rates. A 2022 FTC study found that drip pricing, the gradual revelation of fees throughout a purchase flow, increased consumer spending by an average of 21 percent compared to all-in pricing disclosed upfront.

Pre-Checked Boxes and the Default Effect

Default settings are one of the most powerful and least obvious manipulation vectors available to interface designers. Behavioral economists call it default bias: people are far more likely to accept whatever option is presented as the default than to actively change it, even when changing it would take seconds and clearly serves their interests. Organ donation rates in countries that use opt-out systems are dramatically higher than in opt-in countries, not because people in opt-out countries value donation more, but because the default structure determines outcomes far more than conscious preference.

Companies understand this. Pre-checked boxes for marketing emails, for "optional" service add-ons, for sharing data with partners and for automatic renewal are all applications of default bias to extract value from users. The EU's GDPR explicitly prohibits pre-checked consent boxes for data processing, which is why European users see unchecked boxes in cookie consent interfaces. American users often see the opposite because no equivalent regulation exists.

The airline industry has industrialized pre-checked add-ons. Spirit, Ryanair and similar low-cost carriers pre-select seat assignments, travel insurance and baggage options during booking, presenting the de-selection of these items as requiring active effort from users who must notice each pre-selected option and uncheck it. Research published in the journal Marketing Science found that pre-checked options in airline booking generated between fifteen and twenty-five percent more add-on revenue than explicitly offered options at equivalent prices. The revenue comes entirely from users who did not notice the pre-selections.

Cookie Consent Theaters

Few dark pattern examples illustrate the gap between the letter and spirit of regulation as clearly as the cookie consent interface. GDPR requires informed, specific and freely given consent before placing non-essential tracking cookies. The regulation intended to give users meaningful control over their data. What emerged in practice was an ecosystem of deliberately confusing consent interfaces designed to minimize the percentage of users who actually restrict tracking while technically complying with the requirement to obtain consent.

A 2022 study by researchers at MIT, Cornell and the University of Michigan analyzed 1,500 of the most popular websites and found that 56.5 percent used dark patterns in their cookie consent interfaces. The most common patterns were making "Accept All" buttons prominent and easy to find while making "Reject All" or granular consent options difficult to locate, requiring multiple clicks to decline cookies compared to a single click to accept, and using misleading language to blur the distinction between "functional" and "tracking" cookies. The study found that the combination of these patterns reduced the rate of consent rejection by up to 75 percent compared to interfaces with neutral design.

The dark irony is that the consent banner proliferation that GDPR produced created more friction and annoyance for users than the pre-GDPR state, while ultimately delivering less actual privacy protection than the regulation intended. The banners became a dark pattern delivery mechanism. Users learned to click "Accept All" reflexively to make the banner disappear, which produces even higher tracking consent rates than existed before the regulation. The industry effectively used the regulatory requirement for consent as an opportunity to extract consent at scale through interface manipulation.

Social Proof Manipulation

Urgency and social proof are legitimate psychological principles that describe real phenomena. Items that are genuinely scarce become more desirable. Choices that many other people have made carry real informational value. Dark patterns in this category involve fabricating or exaggerating scarcity and social proof signals to pressure purchase decisions.

The "Only 3 left!" warning on a product page that never seems to sell out is a straightforward fabrication. Booking platforms that display messages like "23 other people are viewing this hotel right now" or "Booked 12 times in the last 24 hours" use real-time urgency to create pressure that benefits conversion rates. The FTC took action against Hotels.com in 2022 for displaying false urgency messages that did not accurately reflect actual booking activity. Investigations by Norwegian consumer protection authorities found that Booking.com displayed urgency messages even when inventory was not actually limited, and the company paid fines totaling millions of euros across multiple European jurisdictions.

Fake reviews are a related dark pattern operating on the same social proof mechanism. The FTC's 2023 rule against fake reviews made it explicitly illegal to purchase fake reviews, pay for reviews without disclosure or suppress negative reviews. The enforcement challenge is significant. Amazon has documented and removed hundreds of millions of fake reviews and has filed numerous lawsuits against fake review farms. The problem persists because the economics are favorable: a product boosted from 3.8 stars to 4.4 stars through fake reviews generates substantially more sales, and the per-review cost in offshore markets can be as low as a few cents.

The Attention Dark Patterns

The dark patterns discussed so far operate at the level of specific transactions. A separate and arguably more consequential category operates at the level of attention itself. These patterns do not trick you into a specific purchase or consent. They trap your attention and emotional engagement in service of advertising revenue models, using behavioral psychology at an architectural level.

Infinite scroll was invented by engineer Aza Raskin, who later became a prominent critic of the technology he helped popularize. Traditional pagination, the requirement to click "next page" to see more content, creates a natural pause point at which a user can make a conscious decision about whether to continue. Infinite scroll eliminates that pause, replacing a periodic decision with continuous frictionless consumption. Raskin has estimated that infinite scroll generates 200 billion extra minutes of daily social media use globally that would not occur with paginated interfaces. He has publicly apologized for creating it.

Variable reward schedules are the mechanism underlying notification addiction. Slot machines use variable reward schedules, the unpredictable intermittent delivery of rewards, because they produce the strongest and most persistent engagement behavior of any reinforcement schedule. Social media notifications operate on the same principle. Most notifications are low-value, but the occasional high-value notification, a message from someone you care about, recognition from a peer, an unexpectedly strong response to something you posted, keeps the checking behavior alive through intermittent reinforcement. This is not an analogy. It is a deliberately applied behavioral design principle, and its effectiveness is continuously tested through A/B experiments on users.

Autoplay was designed to reduce the friction of choosing the next piece of content. It also eliminates the decision point at which you might decide to stop watching. Netflix's autoplay feature, which queues the next episode ten seconds after the current one ends, has been credited with a significant fraction of binge-watching behavior. Netflix's own research found that the autoplay countdown screen kept users watching through 80 percent of recommended next episodes. The feature is now opt-out rather than opt-in in many jurisdictions following regulatory pressure, but the default remains on and most users never change it.

Children as Targets

The ethical dimensions of dark patterns become considerably more serious when the targets are children. Child-directed products and games have been documented using some of the most aggressive dark patterns in the ecosystem. Loot boxes in video games, random reward packs purchased with real money or in-game currency, apply variable reward schedules to create purchase behavior that researchers have consistently found to be functionally similar to gambling. A 2019 study published in Nature Human Behaviour found that loot box spending was significantly correlated with problem gambling in adults and that the correlation was stronger in adolescents.

The FTC's investigation of children's apps found widespread use of virtual currency systems designed to obscure the real-money cost of purchases, countdown timers that expire unless children convince parents to spend money, and social mechanics that pressure children into purchases by making their in-game status visible to peers. The investigation resulted in enforcement actions against several companies but did not resolve the underlying regulatory gap. COPPA, the primary federal law governing children's online data and marketing, was written before the era of app-based gaming and its protections do not cleanly address many current practices.

Regulatory Responses and Their Limits

Regulatory attention to dark patterns has increased substantially since 2020. The FTC has published reports, issued guidance documents and brought enforcement actions. The EU's Digital Services Act and Digital Markets Act both contain provisions addressing dark patterns, with the DSA explicitly prohibiting platforms from designing interfaces that deceive or manipulate users. GDPR enforcement has resulted in fines specifically for dark pattern cookie consent interfaces.

The limits of regulation in this domain are real. The design space is enormous. For every specific practice that becomes prohibited, designers iterate to new patterns that accomplish similar goals within the letter of the prohibition. Enforcement actions take years to complete while the practices they target continue. Fines, even large ones, can represent small fractions of the revenue generated by the pattern they penalize. The FTC's enforcement capacity relative to the scale of the digital economy means that only the most egregious and visible practices receive regulatory attention.

Class action litigation has produced some of the most concrete accountability. LinkedIn paid an $8 million settlement over a feature that sent connection invitations from users without clear disclosure. Amazon paid $61 million to the FTC in 2023 to resolve allegations that it enrolled consumers in its Subscribe & Save program without clear consent and made cancellation unreasonably difficult. Ticketmaster parent Live Nation has faced numerous suits over drip pricing practices. These settlements are meaningful but they operate retrospectively and cannot address the ongoing proliferation of new patterns faster than litigation can catch them.

Developing Your Recognition Toolkit

Understanding the taxonomy of dark patterns is its own defense against them. The researchers who study this field consistently find that explicit knowledge of manipulation techniques reduces their effectiveness. When you recognize a confirmshaming opt-out for what it is, the induced guilt loses most of its power. When you know that "Only 2 left!" is statistically likely to be fabricated urgency, the pressure it was designed to create dissipates.

A few practical habits build on that recognition. Slowing down at checkout is the single most valuable practice. Dark patterns are most effective when users are moving quickly and making fast decisions. The moment you notice your attention narrowing and the impulse to just click and get through the process, that is precisely when friction is serving the company and not you. Taking an extra thirty seconds to read what you are agreeing to and to notice what boxes are pre-selected costs very little and can save real money.

Downloading an app and immediately going to its settings before using any features is a habit that pays dividends. Default notification settings in most apps are set to maximum. Default data sharing settings are set to maximum sharing. The settings that matter for your privacy and attention are buried, but they are usually there. Finding them at the beginning, before habits form around the default experience, is more effective than trying to change behavior later.

For subscription services specifically, using a dedicated payment method with a spending limit or a virtual card number service means that even if a roach motel cancellation flow defeats you temporarily, the company cannot continue charging if the card details change. Services like Privacy.com generate disposable virtual card numbers tied to specific merchants with spending limits you set. This converts the cancellation friction problem from a behavioral manipulation challenge into a technical non-issue.

The broader context for all of this is that the design of digital environments is not neutral. Every interface reflects choices made by someone about what behavior to encourage and what to discourage. Those choices are usually made in the interest of the company operating the service rather than the user experiencing it. Approaching digital interactions with the assumption that interface design is advocacy rather than neutral presentation is not paranoia. It is an accurate model of how these systems actually work.
