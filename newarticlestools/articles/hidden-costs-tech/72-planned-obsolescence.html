Planned Obsolescence Is a Business Model

28,813 upvotes · by u/right_to_repair_now · 12 min read
▲ 28,813 people found this relatable
Advertisement

Something that most people accept as an irritating but unavoidable fact of modern life deserves a harder look. Your phone, which worked perfectly well two years ago, now feels slow and laggy running the latest apps. Your washing machine failed at year seven, roughly two years after the extended warranty expired. Your laptop's battery has degraded to forty percent of its original capacity and the design makes replacement so difficult that a repair shop quoted you more than half the cost of a new machine. Your printer has stopped accepting third-party ink cartridges after a firmware update.

None of these experiences are coincidences. They are outcomes of deliberate engineering decisions made by companies whose revenue models depend on you buying new products at regular intervals. The concept has an official name, planned obsolescence, and a longer history than most people realize. Understanding how it works, how it has evolved and what levers exist to push back against it changes the relationship you have with the objects you buy.

The Phoebus Cartel and the Birth of Engineered Failure

The most documented early example of planned obsolescence is also one of the most brazen. In 1924, the major lightbulb manufacturers of the world, including Philips, General Electric, Osram and several others, formed a cartel called the Phoebus Cartel with the explicit purpose of reducing the lifespan of incandescent lightbulbs. At the time, lightbulbs were already capable of lasting two thousand hours or more. The cartel agreed to standardize bulb lifespan at one thousand hours and to impose fines on member companies whose bulbs lasted longer.

Documents from the cartel's internal meetings are preserved and make for remarkable reading. They show engineers explicitly identifying the problem as bulbs that lasted too long and discussing technical modifications to reduce filament quality just enough to hit the target lifespan without being obviously detectable. The cartel operated for decades before being dismantled. The one-thousand-hour bulb standard it established became so entrenched in manufacturing that it persisted long after the cartel dissolved.

The Phoebus Cartel is famous because it left a paper trail. But the underlying logic, that durable goods are bad for manufacturers because customers who buy less frequently are customers who generate less revenue, applies to essentially every category of consumer product. The incentive structure has not changed. Only the sophistication of the engineering has.

Hardware Obsolescence: The Physical Layer

Modern smartphones are engineering marvels whose physical design choices increasingly seem aimed at shortening their useful life. In 2016, Apple settled a class action lawsuit after acknowledging that iOS updates deliberately throttled the performance of older iPhones. The company's stated justification was that throttling prevented unexpected shutdowns in phones with degraded batteries. Critics noted that Apple could have instead notified users of battery degradation and offered affordable battery replacements, and that its retail pricing for battery service before the scandal was $79, dropping to $29 during the settlement period.

The battery situation is structural rather than incidental. Modern smartphones use lithium-ion batteries that degrade with each charge cycle and are typically rated for somewhere between three hundred and five hundred full charge cycles before degrading to eighty percent of original capacity. At typical usage patterns, most phones reach this threshold in two to three years. Manufacturers have consistently made battery replacement progressively more difficult, moving from user-replaceable batteries to glued-in cells that require professional tools and significant disassembly to access. The Right to Repair movement has extensively documented this transition. A 2021 iFixit analysis of flagship smartphone repairability showed scores declining across major manufacturers over the preceding five years even as repair-friendliness became an occasional marketing talking point.

Non-replaceable components extend beyond batteries. Soldering RAM and storage directly to mainboards in laptops, a practice Apple pioneered with the 2016 MacBook Pro, means that users who would previously have upgraded these components to extend a device's life now have no such option. A laptop purchased with 8GB of RAM that feels insufficient three years later cannot be upgraded. It must be replaced. The environmental and financial implications are both significant. The average American now replaces their smartphone every 2.5 years, generating roughly 151 million old phones annually in the US alone. The global e-waste stream was estimated at 53.6 million metric tons in 2019 and is growing faster than any other waste category.

Software Obsolescence: The Digital Layer

Physical degradation is only one mechanism of planned obsolescence. Software-driven obsolescence has become equally significant and arguably more insidious because it converts perfectly functional hardware into electronic waste through policy decisions rather than material failure.

Microsoft's decision to end support for Windows 10 in October 2025 will affect hundreds of millions of PCs that are physically capable of running modern software but fail to meet Windows 11's hardware requirements, specifically its requirement for TPM 2.0 chips and recent-generation processors. Estimates suggest that between four hundred million and nine hundred million PCs currently running Windows 10 are ineligible for the free Windows 11 upgrade. Many of these machines are three or four years old and perform their intended tasks without difficulty. They will become security liabilities when Windows 10 reaches end-of-life, effectively compelling users to buy new hardware through the security update withdrawal mechanism rather than any physical necessity.

App developers follow platform support decisions downstream. When iOS drops support for older iPhones, app developers face a choice between maintaining compatibility with older versions or targeting only the current platform. Most choose the latter because maintaining backward compatibility adds cost. The result is that perfectly functional older phones gradually become unable to run apps that once worked on them, not because the phone lacks processing power for those apps but because the apps have been rewritten to target newer operating system features that the older phone cannot run.

This dynamic creates a cascading effect that effectively sets a ceiling on how long any device remains fully functional regardless of its physical condition. The phone you bought in 2019 and have maintained carefully may be physically pristine, but if app support for your operating system version has declined and you cannot update to the current OS, it is functionally obsolete in ways that have nothing to do with its hardware. The manufacturer has made a business decision that your device's continued use is not in its interest.

The Appliance Industry's Reliability Regression

The appliance industry presents a different but equally documented version of the same phenomenon. Consumer Reports reliability data, compiled over decades of subscriber surveys, shows a consistent decline in appliance longevity since the 1990s. Refrigerators that routinely lasted twenty to twenty-five years in the 1970s and 1980s now have average lifespans closer to thirteen to fifteen years. Washing machines that lasted fifteen to twenty years now average around ten. Dishwashers, which typically lasted fifteen years a generation ago, are now averaging nine.

The decline in longevity is not primarily driven by increased complexity, though complexity does create more failure points. It is driven by a shift in manufacturing philosophy. In the repair industry, this shift has a colloquial name: cheap-out design. The specific manifestation changes by product category, but the underlying pattern is consistent. Manufacturers identify the component most likely to fail and use the lowest-cost version that will survive past the warranty period. Extended warranties, sold at high margins by both manufacturers and retailers, became a major revenue center roughly concurrent with the decline in product reliability. The business logic is not subtle: a product that fails reliably around year two or three, just past the standard one-year warranty, creates a lucrative market for both replacement units and extended coverage.

Appliance repairability has declined in parallel with reliability. The repair industry trade association iReason has documented steady increases in repair costs relative to replacement costs across most appliance categories. When a specific component fails, manufacturers increasingly offer only proprietary replacement parts at high prices, require factory-authorized service for warranty-covered repairs, and in some cases program appliances to detect non-OEM parts and refuse to operate normally. John Deere tractors, which are not consumer appliances but represent the same logic, have been one of the most visible cases. The company's software locks prevent independent repair of key components, forcing farmers to pay dealer service rates or wait days for a dealer visit during time-sensitive harvest windows.

Ink, Toner and the Razor-and-Blades Trap

The printer industry has refined planned obsolescence into something approaching an art form. Printers are frequently sold at or below cost because the real margin comes from ink and toner cartridges, which are sold at extraordinary markups. HP's printer ink has been analyzed as costing somewhere between $2,700 and $8,000 per liter, depending on the cartridge, making it one of the most expensive liquids sold at retail. The business model works only if customers are locked into purchasing proprietary consumables, which requires a multi-layered system to prevent customers from using cheaper alternatives.

The mechanisms for enforcing consumable lock-in have grown progressively more aggressive. Early printer chips simply tracked ink levels. Current chips authenticate the cartridge against the printer's firmware and refuse to operate with unrecognized cartridges. Firmware updates, pushed automatically through internet-connected printers, have been used to disable printers that were working fine with third-party cartridges by updating the authentication protocols mid-product-life. HP executed this maneuver in 2016, disabling printers across its installed base through an update that changed how the firmware validated cartridges. The FTC subsequently investigated HP's practices and the company has faced ongoing regulatory attention, but the practice continues in evolved forms.

Coffee makers have adopted the same logic. Keurig's K-Cup system uses DRM-style authentication to prevent compatible pods from third parties from working in its machines. Nespresso's original patent system created a proprietary capsule ecosystem and the company has fought legal battles in multiple countries to maintain its consumable monopoly. The initial machine price is often subsidized by the expectation of ongoing consumable revenue that makes the economics work. This is not hidden. It is openly described as the razor-and-blades model in business school curricula. The consumer harm occurs when the terms of the ongoing relationship are not clearly communicated at the point of sale and when the company uses technical measures to prevent consumers from choosing lower-cost alternatives.

The Right to Repair Movement

The political response to planned obsolescence has coalesced around a framework called Right to Repair. The core demand is straightforward: consumers and independent repair technicians should have legal access to the documentation, tools and parts needed to repair products they own. The current situation in many product categories is the opposite: manufacturers use intellectual property law, software licenses and trade secret protection to restrict repair access in ways that favor their own service revenue.

The Right to Repair movement won a significant victory in Massachusetts in 2020, where voters passed a ballot initiative requiring automotive manufacturers to make repair data accessible to independent shops. The initiative passed with 75 percent of the vote, reflecting how broadly the concern resonates across political demographics. The automotive industry subsequently engaged in aggressive lobbying against expansion of similar requirements and challenged the Massachusetts law in federal court.

At the federal level, the FTC issued a report in 2021 finding that manufacturer repair restrictions harm consumers and that existing laws provide insufficient protection. The report recommended both FTC enforcement action and legislation to address the gap. The EU has moved further, with its Ecodesign Regulation requiring manufacturers in multiple product categories to make spare parts available for minimum periods and to provide repair documentation. Several EU countries now offer tax incentives for repair services as part of circular economy initiatives.

Apple, which had previously been one of the most aggressive opponents of independent repair, announced a Self Repair Program in 2021 following intense regulatory and public pressure. The program makes genuine Apple parts and repair manuals available to consumers. Independent technicians who examined the program noted that it was structured in ways that remained cumbersome and expensive compared to simply allowing open access to parts. But it represented a meaningful shift in the company's public position, driven entirely by the credible threat of legislation.

What You Can Actually Do

Individual responses to planned obsolescence exist on a spectrum, and the realistic range is broader than either doing nothing or becoming a full-time repair advocate. A few specific practices meaningfully change the financial relationship you have with consumer technology.

Buying refurbished or certified pre-owned devices is one of the highest-leverage choices available. A two-year-old flagship smartphone, refurbished and certified, costs roughly forty to sixty percent of a new model and provides essentially identical performance for most use cases. The refurbishment market has matured significantly. Programs like Apple Certified Refurbished and third-party sellers like Back Market offer warranties and return policies comparable to new device purchases. Choosing refurbished extends the useful life of existing hardware, reduces e-waste and substantially reduces the per-year cost of device ownership.

Learning to replace batteries yourself, for devices where it is physically possible, is one of the better investments of an afternoon. A replacement battery for most smartphones costs between fifteen and forty dollars. iFixit publishes detailed repair guides for hundreds of devices and sells the specialized tools needed for under twenty dollars. For phones where battery replacement is physically accessible, the labor involved is thirty to sixty minutes and the result is a device that performs like new on the metric that matters most to daily usability.

For appliances, the single most useful shift is buying older models from manufacturers with documented reliability histories rather than new models at the current frontier. A new washing machine model has not yet accumulated the failure data that would reveal its weak points. A model that has been on the market for five years and appears consistently in Consumer Reports reliability surveys has a known track record. Paying slightly more for a brand with strong reliability data typically saves money over the ownership period by avoiding early replacement.

Supporting Right to Repair legislation is not purely idealistic. The Massachusetts automotive result demonstrated that legislative action moves manufacturers significantly. When companies face the credible prospect of being required to provide repair access, they often offer voluntary programs preemptively. The most direct action available is contacting state and federal representatives to support pending Right to Repair bills, which exist in dozens of state legislatures. The opposition is well-funded and organized. Consumer voices are the comparative advantage of the other side.
