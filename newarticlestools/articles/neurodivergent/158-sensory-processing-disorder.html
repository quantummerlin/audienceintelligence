Sensory Processing Disorder: When the World Is Too Much

12 min read  ·  Neuroscience  ·  Daily Life
63,847 upvotes
Advertisement

Imagine that you are in a moderately busy restaurant. The lights are slightly brighter than in a dim bar. There is background music at a moderate volume, the kind that most people do not consciously register. Several conversations are occurring at nearby tables. The chair you are sitting on has a slightly rough fabric texture. The person across from you is wearing a perfume that is not particularly strong. For most people in this scenario, these sensory inputs register at the margins of awareness or not at all — they are background noise that the nervous system filters without effort, allowing attention to be directed toward the conversation and the meal.

For a person with sensory processing differences, this same environment can be genuinely overwhelming. The fluorescent lights flicker at a frequency that is imperceptible to most people but that creates a continuous visual disturbance. The background music and the surrounding conversations blend into an undifferentiated roar that makes it impossible to parse the voice of the person directly in front of them. The chair's texture registers as abrasive against their skin in a way that demands constant attention. The perfume is not merely present but sensorially insistent, impossible to tune out. The cumulative effect is not mild discomfort but genuine sensory overload that can make conversation impossible, that can escalate rapidly into anxiety and that can, if prolonged, result in a shutdown response that looks to observers like withdrawal or rudeness but is actually the nervous system protecting itself from overload.

This experience — exaggerated, distorted or disrupted processing of ordinary sensory input — is what sensory processing disorder describes. It is both common and widely misunderstood, and the misunderstanding has real consequences for the people who live with it and the people who care for or interact with them.

The Diagnostic Controversy

Sensory processing disorder occupies an unusual position in the clinical landscape: it is widely recognized by occupational therapists who work with children and adults who have sensory processing differences, and it is recognized as a feature of several established diagnoses — autism spectrum disorder, ADHD and anxiety disorders all frequently involve sensory processing differences. But sensory processing disorder as a standalone diagnosis is not recognized in the DSM-5, and its status as an independent diagnostic category is contested among researchers and clinicians.

The occupational therapist A. Jean Ayres developed the concept of sensory integration dysfunction in the 1960s and 1970s, building on observations of children whose sensory processing difficulties seemed to be the primary driver of behavioral, emotional and learning difficulties. Her work generated an influential therapeutic approach — sensory integration therapy — and a clinical framework that occupational therapists have continued to develop and refine.

The resistance to including sensory processing disorder in the DSM has been driven partly by concerns about the research base. Critics argue that the evidence for sensory processing disorder as a distinct syndrome with a characteristic neurological profile independent of other conditions is not yet robust enough to justify a standalone diagnostic category. They note that sensory differences appear in a wide range of conditions, that the boundaries between sensory processing disorder and normal variation are not clearly established and that the assessment tools used to identify it have variable reliability.

Proponents counter that the absence from the DSM reflects diagnostic conservatism rather than evidence of absence, that children and adults with disabling sensory processing difficulties deserve clinical recognition regardless of whether those difficulties co-occur with other diagnoses and that the substantial body of neurological research on sensory processing differences supports a biological basis for the clinical phenomena, even if the nosological boundaries remain unclear.

The "it's not in the DSM" argument frustrates me because it ignores why it matters for actual people. My son's sensory processing difficulties are the most impairing thing in his life. They affect every outing, every social event, every school day. Whether or not the professional bodies have agreed on exactly where to file it diagnostically doesn't change the reality that his nervous system processes sensory input differently than most kids, that this is genuinely hard for him and that occupational therapy has helped more than anything else we've tried. — r/SPD, 47,293 upvotes

The Seven Senses (Not Just Five)

Most people learned about five senses in school — sight, hearing, touch, taste and smell. But the nervous system processes sensory information from two additional systems that are less familiar to most people and that are centrally important for understanding sensory processing differences: the proprioceptive system and the vestibular system.

Proprioception is the sense of where your body is in space — the continuous information stream from muscles, joints and tendons that tells your nervous system what position your body is in and what it is doing at each moment. It operates entirely without conscious attention in most people: you know where your hand is without looking at it, you can navigate a dark room without bumping into furniture, you can pour a glass of water without consciously monitoring every movement. Proprioceptive differences can manifest as poor body awareness, clumsiness, a drive for intense physical input such as crashing, stomping and tight hugs and difficulty grading the force of actions — pressing a pencil too hard, closing doors too forcefully.

The vestibular system processes information about movement, balance and spatial orientation, centered in the inner ear. It works in constant dialogue with vision and proprioception to maintain balance and coordinate movement. Vestibular processing differences can manifest as gravitational insecurity — intense fear or distress when feet leave the ground, when descending stairs or when in elevators — or conversely as a heightened seeking of vestibular input through spinning, swinging and movement that other children find disorienting.

Understanding these seven sensory systems, and recognizing that processing differences can occur in any combination across them, substantially expands the picture of what sensory processing differences can look like. A child who seeks intense proprioceptive input by constantly crashing into furniture and people and a child who is distressed by the light touch of a tag on a shirt collar are both showing sensory processing differences, but in entirely different sensory systems and with entirely different behavioral manifestations.

Hyper vs. Hypo: The Bidirectional Nature of Sensory Differences

Sensory processing differences can run in two directions within any sensory system. Hypersensitivity or sensory over-responsiveness means that the nervous system registers sensory input at a lower threshold than typical — stimuli that most people's systems filter out register strongly. Hyposensitivity or sensory under-responsiveness means the opposite: the threshold is higher than typical, ordinary stimuli do not register fully and stronger input is needed to achieve the same perceptual effect.

The same individual can be hypersensitive in some sensory systems and hyposensitive in others, and can even shift between states depending on fatigue, stress, illness and other factors. A person who is hypersensitive to auditory input and hyposensitive to proprioceptive input will have a very different behavioral profile from a person with the opposite pattern, but both may be experiencing sensory processing differences.

Sensory seeking — the active pursuit of intense sensory input — is associated with hyposensitivity. Children who seek proprioceptive input by constantly crashing, climbing and requesting tight squeezes are effectively self-medicating an under-responsive proprioceptive system with the strongest available input. Children who chew on their clothing, pencils or hands are seeking oral proprioceptive input. Adults who crave intense exercise, spicy food or loud music may be showing milder versions of the same pattern.

This bidirectionality complicates both identification and intervention. The sensory seeking child and the sensory avoiding child are both showing sensory processing differences, but they look completely different and require different responses. Adults who assume that sensory processing differences always look like avoidance and distress may not recognize sensory seeking as part of the same picture.

The Nervous System and Regulation

The framework of sensory processing is deeply connected to the broader concept of nervous system regulation. The autonomic nervous system maintains a physiological state that is neither so over-activated (fight-or-flight) that it interferes with function nor so under-activated (freeze or shutdown) that it prevents engagement, calibrating continuously to the demands of the environment. This optimal zone — the "window of tolerance" in Siegel's framework or the "just right" challenge level in sensory integration theory — is where learning, social engagement and effective functioning occur.

For people with sensory processing differences, the sensory environment is a major driver of dysregulation. A sensory environment that pushes the nervous system into hyperactivation through too much sensory stimulation or triggers intense distress through contact with aversive sensory input pulls the person out of their regulatory window and into fight-or-flight or freeze states where effective functioning is genuinely impaired. The child who has a meltdown in a noisy school cafeteria or the adult who becomes irritable and withdrawn in a crowded shopping mall is not simply being difficult; they are showing the behavioral consequences of nervous system dysregulation driven by sensory overload.

The implication is that sensory environment design is not merely a comfort consideration but a regulation intervention. When environments are designed to reduce unnecessary sensory demands — quieter classrooms, adjustable lighting, sensory-friendly waiting rooms, flexible dress codes that allow comfortable clothing — they expand the range of people who can function effectively in them without expending enormous regulatory resources on managing the sensory environment.

Every fluorescent light, every unexpected touch, every scratchy fabric, every background noise — individually manageable, collectively depleting. Interoception: The Sense You Didn't Know You Had

A growing body of research has expanded attention to interoception — the sense of the internal state of the body — as another domain where processing differences have significant functional consequences. Interoception involves the continuous stream of signals from internal organs, including heart rate, breathing, hunger, fullness, temperature, pain and the physiological components of emotion.

Interoceptive differences can manifest as difficulty detecting or interpreting internal bodily states: not knowing you are hungry until you are dramatically so, not noticing you need to use the bathroom until the need is urgent, difficulty identifying what you are feeling emotionally because emotions are registered partly through bodily sensations. Research by Sarah Garfinkel and colleagues has documented interoceptive differences in autism and anxiety that have significant implications for emotional regulation and self-care.

For children with interoceptive differences, the consequences include eating irregularities (not noticing hunger or fullness reliably), bathroom accidents (not noticing the need reliably), difficulty with emotional self-awareness and regulation and challenges in body-based therapies and mindfulness practices that depend on accurate interoceptive awareness. Understanding interoceptive differences helps explain some behavioral patterns that otherwise seem puzzling — the child who cannot tell you how they are feeling is not being deliberately evasive; they may genuinely not have reliable access to the bodily information that would ordinarily provide that answer.

Interventions That Help

The primary evidence-based intervention for sensory processing differences is sensory integration therapy, typically provided by occupational therapists trained in the Ayres Sensory Integration approach. ASI therapy uses a structured "sensory diet" of carefully calibrated sensory experiences — typically involving proprioceptive, vestibular and tactile input through activities like swinging, climbing and resistance games — in a therapeutic environment designed to support the child in gradually expanding their tolerance for challenging sensory experiences and building more adaptive sensory processing.

The research evidence on ASI therapy's effectiveness is more mixed than its clinical proponents often acknowledge and more positive than its critics suggest. A Cochrane review found inconclusive evidence for sensory integration therapy specifically, partly due to methodological limitations in existing studies rather than evidence of ineffectiveness. More recent well-designed randomized controlled trials have found positive outcomes on parent-reported and therapist-assessed measures. The quality of the research base is improving, and clinical experience among occupational therapists working with sensory processing differences is extensive even when the formal research base remains under development.

Environmental modification is a complementary intervention that does not require a clinical provider. Identifying specific sensory triggers and systematically reducing exposure to them or building gradual tolerance allows people with sensory differences to function more comfortably in their environment. This might involve weighted blankets for proprioceptive regulation, noise-canceling headphones for auditory hypersensitivity, clothing choices that minimize tactile triggers, dietary flexibility that accommodates taste and texture sensitivities and workplace accommodations that reduce sensory demands.

The most important thing that people without sensory processing differences can provide to those who have them is belief and accommodation. Sensory experiences are subjective and invisible to observers, and the experiences of people with sensory differences are frequently dismissed as oversensitivity, manipulation or dramatic exaggeration. The research literature is clear that sensory processing differences reflect neurological differences in how sensory signals are processed — they are not character flaws or performance. Treating them as real, taking them seriously and building environments that accommodate different sensory needs is both an ethical consideration and a practical one in a world that benefits from the full participation of everyone in it.
