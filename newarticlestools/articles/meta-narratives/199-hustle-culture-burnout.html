Hustle Culture and the Burnout It Was Always Going to Create

12 min read • Meta-Narratives
▲ 118,492 found this validating

Gary Vaynerchuk, the social media entrepreneur who became perhaps the most recognized face of hustle culture, has posted variations of the same message thousands of times across his platforms: while you are sleeping, someone else is working. While you are resting, someone else is grinding. While you are watching television, someone else is building the future that will render your career obsolete. The message is designed to produce a specific psychological state, a low-level anxiety about inadequate effort that can be relieved only by working more. It is effective as a content strategy because anxiety is engaging, and because the partial truth embedded in it, that consistent effort does compound over time, provides enough cover for the motivational manipulation to feel like wisdom rather than exploitation.

Hustle culture as a coherent cultural formation emerged most visibly in the 2010s, though its roots run deeper through Silicon Valley mythology, hip-hop entrepreneurialism, immigrant work ethic narratives and the Protestant work ethic that Max Weber traced to the theological anxiety of Calvinist predestination. What distinguished the 2010s version was the combination of social media amplification, the smartphone-enabled collapse of boundaries between work and non-work time, the precarity of the gig economy that required many workers to cobble together multiple income streams, and a specific mythology of tech entrepreneurship in which sleeping at the office was a badge of honor and work-life balance was for people who lacked ambition. Mark Zuckerberg famously ran a company by the motto "move fast and break things." Elon Musk has repeatedly suggested that working 80-100 hour weeks is simply what success requires, tweeting in 2018 that "nobody ever changed the world on 40 hours a week."

"I did the hustle culture thing from about 22 to 28. I'm 34 now and the honest assessment is: yes, I built things. I also missed my dad's health decline, lost my closest friendships, developed an anxiety disorder, and had two relationships end because I was basically absent even when I was physically present. The ROI was negative and I can't get those years back." r/Entrepreneur • 47,293 upvotes

The World Health Organization added burnout to the International Classification of Diseases in 2019, formally recognizing it as an occupational phenomenon characterized by feelings of energy depletion or exhaustion, increased mental distance from one's job, and reduced professional efficacy. The classification was the culmination of decades of research beginning with the psychologist Herbert Freudenberger, who coined the term "burnout" in a 1974 paper describing what he observed in volunteer workers at a free clinic who had become exhausted, cynical and ineffective through sustained overcommitment. Christina Maslach at UC Berkeley developed the Maslach Burnout Inventory in 1981, the most widely used measurement tool, and has spent four decades documenting burnout as primarily an organizational and structural phenomenon rather than an individual failure of resilience, a distinction that most of the popular discourse on burnout systematically inverts.

The Structural vs. Individual Frame

Maslach's research consistently identifies six domains of work environment that predict burnout: workload, control, reward, community, fairness and values alignment. When work is chronically demanding, when workers have insufficient autonomy, when effort is inadequately rewarded, when workplace community is damaged or absent, when processes feel unfair and when workers' values conflict with organizational demands, burnout follows with considerable predictability. The individual's resilience, mindset and self-care practices modulate the experience, but they do not override the structural conditions. This is why burnout rates are not randomly distributed across populations but cluster predictably in jobs with high demand and low control, in professions that experienced the most severe understaffing during COVID, and among workers who have the least power to renegotiate their conditions.

The popular response to the burnout epidemic has nonetheless been almost uniformly framed in individual terms. Arianna Huffington founded Thrive Global after collapsing from exhaustion in 2007, building a wellness empire premised on the idea that individuals need to change their relationship to sleep, stress and recovery. The "self-care" industry that exploded in the 2010s offered baths, candles, meditation apps and wellness retreats as responses to systemic overwork. The framing was gently mocked by critics who noted that the prescription for structural conditions producing burnout was being sold back to the burned-out people as luxury goods at a premium, a dynamic that the writer Anne Helen Petersen called "treating the symptom rather than the disease" in her widely read 2019 BuzzFeed essay on millennial burnout.

Who Hustle Culture Actually Serves

The most important political economy question about hustle culture is who benefits from its widespread adoption as a value system. When workers internalize the belief that their value is equivalent to their output and that limitations on their output reflect personal failures of commitment, several things follow that are advantageous for employers. Workers self-monitor and self-discipline in ways that reduce the need for external supervision. Workers who experience exhaustion or disengagement attribute this to personal inadequacy rather than to work conditions, reducing the likelihood of collective action or demands for structural change. Workers compete with each other on the basis of demonstrated commitment, driving up the baseline of acceptable effort without corresponding increases in compensation. And workers who burn out can be replaced with fresh workers who have not yet learned the limits of their own endurance, creating a de facto human capital extraction model in which individual workers bear the full cost of the depreciation of their capacity.

Jonathan Malesic's "The End of Burnout" (2022) traces the ideological history of work as a source of identity and meaning in American culture, arguing that burnout is the inevitable product of the gap between the enormous meaning we are expected to derive from work and the reality of most actual work environments. We ask work to provide identity, community, purpose, status, financial security and creative fulfillment simultaneously. Most work environments provide one or two of these, adequately or not, and the gap between what we expected and what we receive is experienced as personal failure rather than as a sign that work was always going to be an insufficient source of complete human meaning.

"The thing hustle culture doesn't tell you is that Elon Musk and Gary Vee are not working 80 hours because it's the only path to success. They're doing it because they have a specific kind of psychology that finds meaning almost exclusively in work. Recommending that to everyone is like a marathon runner recommending everyone run marathons for their health. It works for some people. It injures most people." r/antiwork • 52,847 upvotes

The COVID Reckoning and Great Resignation

COVID-19 produced what may be the most significant mass reassessment of work values in decades. The pandemic forced millions of workers to experience what full work-life integration actually felt like when the separation between work and home collapsed entirely, and the experience was, for many, clarifying in the wrong direction. The combination of remote work, homeschooling, caregiving and job insecurity produced a period of sustained overwork that many found unsustainable and revelatory. Research by Microsoft's WorkLab found that weekly meeting time had increased by 148 percent since the start of the pandemic, collaboration was happening at all hours, and workers reported feeling unable to disconnect. The pandemic also gave many workers the unusual experience of witnessing death at scale, which has a documented effect of clarifying what one actually values rather than what one has been told to value.

The "Great Resignation" of 2021-2022, in which 47 million Americans voluntarily left their jobs according to Bureau of Labor Statistics data, was widely interpreted as a rejection of hustle culture's premises. Research by the Pew Research Center found that the leading reasons were low pay, lack of advancement opportunities and feeling disrespected at work rather than specifically an anti-work ideology. But the scale of voluntary departures, particularly from service and healthcare sectors where burnout was most severe, suggested that the pandemic had depleted a psychological reserve of tolerance for poor working conditions that hustle culture had been drawing on for years. When Anthony Klotz, the organizational psychologist who coined "Great Resignation," described it as a pent-up desire for change that the pandemic had both created the conditions for and catalyzed, he was describing something recognizable to anyone who had watched someone finally leave a relationship they had known was wrong for years.

Quiet Quitting as Recalibration

The "quiet quitting" phenomenon of 2022, in which workers described simply doing the work their job description required and declining to perform the unpaid emotional and psychological labor that hustle culture had normalized as baseline expectation, generated enormous media coverage and considerable management anxiety. The term was somewhat misleading, since most "quiet quitters" were not quitting at all. They were simply declining to go above and beyond what they were compensated to do, a position that sounds unremarkable when stated clearly but was treated as a form of workplace deviance by commentators who had internalized hustle culture's premise that the appropriate relationship to employment involves genuine overextension as a demonstration of commitment.

The management anxiety about quiet quitting was itself revealing. It suggested that many business models had been built on extracting above-contract labor from workers whose commitment, identification with the company's mission or financial desperation could be relied upon to produce more work than they were formally paid for. When that surplus extraction declined, the business model showed stress. The appropriate response, raising compensation and improving conditions to earn the discretionary effort that workers had been previously providing for free, was less common than the response of treating the recalibration to contractual expectations as a character failure of workers who had become insufficiently dedicated.

The Generational Shift

Survey data consistently shows that younger workers, particularly those entering the workforce after 2015, report lower willingness than their predecessors to sacrifice personal relationships, health and time for career advancement. A 2023 Deloitte survey of Gen Z and millennial workers found that most would refuse a job that compromised their work-life balance even at higher compensation, and that mental health and meaning ranked consistently above salary as job satisfaction factors. Older commentators have reliably interpreted this as evidence of insufficient ambition or softness. A different interpretation, supported by the actual conditions these workers have experienced, is that they are accurately calibrating based on evidence their predecessors did not have.

Millennials, who came of age economically in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, watched a generation that had followed all the hustle culture prescriptions, worked hard, delayed gratification, built careers, experience catastrophic wealth destruction through factors entirely outside their control. They entered an economy in which the returns to credential inflation (more education required for the same jobs their parents held) and housing cost explosion meant that the hustle culture bargain, work hard enough and success will follow, was simply not returning what it had promised. Skepticism about overwork among people who watched its promised payoffs fail to materialize is not irrationality. It is Bayesian updating.

The honest reckoning with hustle culture does not conclude that effort is unnecessary or that aspiration is harmful. It concludes that effort imposed without limits, without recovery and without proportionality to genuine reward is not ambition. It is extraction. And the cultural formation that made that extraction feel like a personal value to be proud of rather than a condition to be negotiated against was always more useful to the people profiting from it than to the people performing it.
