The Limits of Therapy Culture

12 min read • Meta-Narratives
▲ 88,293 found this thought-provoking

Sometime in the past decade, therapy-speak escaped the consulting room and colonized everyday language. "I need to set a boundary." "That's triggering for me." "They're being really toxic." "I'm working through some childhood trauma." "You need to do the work." "That's not my emotional labor to give." These phrases, which would have been recognizable primarily to mental health professionals and their clients twenty years ago, now appear routinely in casual conversation, on social media, in workplace HR communications and in the scripts of mainstream television dramas. The spread of psychological vocabulary into ordinary life is often celebrated as evidence of growing mental health awareness and the destigmatization of emotional difficulty. It is those things, partly. It is also something more complicated, and the complications are worth examining honestly rather than defensively.

The sociologist Eva Illouz documented the "therapeutic turn" in American culture across several books, arguing in "Cold Intimacies" (2007) and "Saving the Modern Soul" (2008) that the psychological vocabulary and frameworks developed in clinical settings have been absorbed into everyday self-understanding in ways that have transformed how people relate to themselves and each other. Illouz's analysis is neither purely celebratory nor purely critical. She acknowledges that the therapeutic framework offered many people, particularly women, a language for naming experiences of harm and unfair treatment that had previously lacked legitimate expression. The ability to say "this is emotional abuse" rather than "I'm just being difficult" represented a genuine expansion of moral vocabulary. But she also argues that the therapeutic lens systematically interprets human experience through an individualistic framework focused on psychological states rather than social structures, and that this has had costs that are less frequently discussed.

"I've been in therapy for years and I think it's genuinely helped me. But I also notice that 'I need to work on myself' can function as a way of never actually being ready to show up for other people, and 'protecting my mental health' can be a way of avoiding anything difficult. The same framework that helped me understand genuine harm can be used to avoid all discomfort." r/therapy • 24,847 upvotes

The psychologist Jonathan Haidt and the legal scholar Greg Lukianoff raised related concerns in "The Coddling of the American Mind" (2018), arguing that several therapeutic concepts had been absorbed into campus culture in ways that reinforced rather than challenged cognitive distortions. Their central example was the treatment of emotional discomfort as evidence of harm: if encountering a difficult idea makes you feel unsafe, therapeutic culture may encourage interpreting that feeling as a signal that the idea is harmful and should be avoided, when cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) actually prescribes the opposite approach, treating emotional discomfort in response to non-dangerous stimuli as a target for habitual exposure rather than avoidance. The "safety" language of campus culture, they argued, borrowed therapeutic credibility while inverting therapeutic logic.

The Trauma Framework's Expansion

The concept of trauma has undergone a significant expansion in popular usage over the past twenty years. In its clinical origins, post-traumatic stress disorder referred to psychological responses to events outside the range of normal human experience: combat, sexual assault, severe accidents and natural disasters that threatened life. The diagnostic criteria required that the stressor involve actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence. Over time, and particularly with the development of concepts like "complex trauma" (from repeated interpersonal harm over time) and "developmental trauma" (from adverse childhood experiences), the clinical category expanded to include a wider range of experiences. Bessel van der Kolk's influential "The Body Keeps the Score" (2014) argued that developmental and relational trauma produces neurological changes comparable to those from acute traumatic events, a claim that has shaped both clinical practice and popular understanding.

In popular usage, however, "trauma" has expanded further still. It is now routinely applied to experiences of embarrassment, rejection, interpersonal conflict, disappointment and difficulty that are upsetting but well within the normal range of human experience. This expansion has both benefits and costs. The benefit is that more people feel their suffering is taken seriously and have access to a framework for understanding their emotional responses as having comprehensible causes rather than being evidence of personal weakness. The cost is that the word "trauma" loses its ability to distinguish between experiences that require professional intervention and those that require ordinary human support and resilience, and that people who have experienced the kind of trauma that produces PTSD may find their suffering minimized when the same language is applied to much less severe experiences.

The researcher Lucy Foulkes, in "Losing Our Minds" (2021), argues that expanding the definition of mental disorder and psychological harm to include ordinary human difficulty may actually worsen outcomes for some people, because labeling a difficult but normal experience as a symptom can reinforce the idea that one is damaged and incapable in ways that become self-fulfilling. The research on "emotional perfectionism," the belief that one should feel good and that negative emotions indicate something wrong, suggests that the therapeutic culture's focus on identifying and naming negative emotional states can sometimes intensify rather than relieve them when not accompanied by effective techniques for working through them.

Boundaries and Their Discontents

The concept of healthy personal boundaries is genuinely valuable and was for a long time insufficiently represented in mainstream discourse. The ability to identify and communicate one's limits, to say no to requests that exceed what one is willing or able to give, and to disengage from relationships and situations that are harmful are important capacities that many people, particularly those raised in environments that did not model or support them, need to develop. The problem is not the concept but the way it has been operationalized in popular therapy culture, where "setting a boundary" has in some communities become an all-purpose justification for avoiding any relational demand that produces discomfort, and where the invocation of boundary-setting can function as conversation-ending rhetoric that precludes the kind of negotiation and repair that relationships require.

The philosopher Kieran Setiya has noted that there is something philosophically peculiar about the therapeutic injunction to "protect your energy" and "prioritize your wellbeing," not because self-care is unimportant but because it can be taken to imply that one's emotional comfort is the primary criterion for evaluating relational obligations. Relationships, and especially close relationships, inherently involve being affected by others' suffering, being called upon when it is inconvenient, tolerating others' difficult moods and navigating conflicts that do not resolve cleanly. A framework that consistently interprets these demands as violations of one's boundaries, rather than as the ordinary friction of genuine connection, produces people who are less able to sustain the kinds of long-term, committed relationships that research consistently identifies as the primary source of human wellbeing.

When Therapy-Speak Replaces Politics

One of the most significant critiques of therapy culture comes from the left rather than the right, arguing that the individualization of social problems that therapeutic language produces has depoliticizing effects. When racial injustice is discussed primarily in terms of the psychological harm it causes to individuals rather than the structural arrangements that produce and reproduce it, the implied solutions are therapeutic rather than political. When workplace exploitation is framed as producing burnout that individuals should treat with self-care rather than as a structural condition requiring collective action, the therapeutic framing serves the interests of the people benefiting from that exploitation. When economic anxiety is medicalized as a mental health issue to be managed by an app, the political conditions producing that anxiety go unaddressed.

The critic Amber A'Lee Frost has argued that "therapy brain," the tendency to interpret political and social problems through a psychological lens, produces a specific kind of progressive paralysis in which political engagement is perpetually deferred pending the completion of personal healing work. The injunction to "do the work" can mean internal psychological examination or political organizing, and in popular therapy culture the internal meaning consistently displaces the external one. This is not a necessary feature of therapeutic thinking, since social justice traditions have always insisted on integrating personal and political transformation. But the commodified therapy culture that has become dominant tends to sell personal transformation as a substitute for political engagement rather than as preparation for it.

The Access Problem and Its Ironies

Therapy culture has spread the language of mental health widely while the actual practice of therapy remains inaccessible to most of the population that could benefit from it. A 2022 survey by the American Psychological Association found that 42 percent of Americans who sought therapy were unable to find a therapist who was taking new patients, could see them promptly and accepted their insurance. The average cost of a therapy session in the United States is $100-$200, and most insurance plans have limited behavioral health coverage despite federal mental health parity laws. In rural areas, the shortage of mental health providers is severe: the Health Resources and Services Administration estimated in 2022 that over 150 million Americans live in designated Mental Health Professional Shortage Areas.

The irony is that therapy-speak has become most prevalent in exactly the demographic that has the most access to actual therapy, the educated, upper-middle-class, predominantly white population that can afford private practitioners, takes extended leave to "focus on mental health" and has the social capital to make therapeutic self-disclosure a form of sophistication rather than a source of vulnerability. For the demographic that most lacks access to mental health treatment, including low-income communities, rural communities, communities of color and immigrant communities where mental health stigma remains strong, the therapy-speak cultural shift has provided less benefit and more risk of having ordinary hardship pathologized and then not treated.

What Therapy Culture Gets Right

The critique of therapy culture's excesses should not obscure what the broader cultural shift toward psychological awareness has genuinely accomplished. Suicide is discussed more openly than at any previous point in American cultural history, and evidence suggests this reduces rather than increases risk. The destigmatization of depression and anxiety has encouraged treatment-seeking that has helped millions of people who would previously have suffered without support. The mainstream acceptance that children's adverse experiences have lasting psychological effects has improved child welfare policy, parenting culture and educational practice. The recognition that relational dynamics in workplaces have meaningful effects on employee wellbeing has improved some organizational cultures. These are genuine achievements that should not be sacrificed to critique.

The goal of a more psychologically informed culture is worth defending. The question is whether the specific form that psychological awareness has taken in popular therapy culture, individualistic, consumer-oriented, vocabulary-heavy and structurally uncritical, is the best available form, or whether a version that maintained the genuine insights while being more honest about the limits of therapeutic framing, more attentive to structural conditions and more accessible across class and demographic lines would serve people better. The answer seems clearly yes, but getting from here to there requires exactly the kind of uncomfortable self-examination that therapy culture, at its best, is supposed to encourage.
