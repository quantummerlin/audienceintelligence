The Wellness Industrial Complex

13 min read • Meta-Narratives
▲ 92,847 found this eye-opening

The Global Wellness Institute estimated the global wellness economy at $4.5 trillion in 2021 and projected growth to $7 trillion by 2025. This figure encompasses gyms and fitness studios, healthy eating and nutrition, wellness tourism, complementary and alternative medicine, personal care and beauty, preventive and personalized health, traditional and complementary medicine, workplace wellness, wellness real estate and the mental wellness sector. It is one of the largest and fastest-growing segments of the global economy, built on the premise that health is not merely the absence of disease but a positive state of optimal physical, mental and spiritual functioning that requires constant active cultivation, typically through the purchase of products and services. The industry's genius, and arguably its central problem, is that it has defined health as a destination that can be perpetually approached but never reached, ensuring a permanent market for the next intervention, supplement, practice or retreat that will finally close the gap between where you are and where you could be.

The contemporary wellness industry has two distinct but overlapping wings. The first is rooted in genuine public health: exercise, nutrition, stress management, adequate sleep and preventive medical care all have solid evidence bases and genuinely improve health outcomes. The research supporting regular physical activity, Mediterranean-style dietary patterns, mindfulness-based stress reduction and adequate sleep is extensive and reproducible. This wing of wellness overlaps substantially with mainstream medicine and public health, even if it often reaches people through commercial channels that extract profit from advice that could be delivered cheaply. The second wing is something quite different: a sprawling alternative health economy built on unproven or disproven interventions, celebrity endorsement, spiritual marketing and the exploitation of genuine anxiety about health in a system where conventional medicine is often inadequate, expensive and impersonal.

"I spent three years deep in wellness culture. Supplements, infrared saunas, functional medicine practitioners, elimination diets, a $300 jade roller. I got healthier in some ways because I was paying attention to my body for the first time. But looking back, the thing that actually helped was sleeping more and reducing stress. The $8,000 I spent in supplements was mostly expensive pee." r/wellness • 31,847 upvotes

Gwyneth Paltrow's Goop brand has become the most discussed emblem of the wellness industry's second wing, offering products and recommendations that range from the mundane to the medically dubious to the actively dangerous. The $66 "This Smells Like My Vagina" candle is merely absurd. The promotion of vaginal jade eggs, which Goop claimed improved hormonal balance and were eventually the subject of a $145,000 settlement with California health regulators, was potentially harmful. The promotion of coffee enemas for liver detoxification runs contrary to medical evidence and carries genuine risks. What makes Goop interesting analytically is that it exists on a continuum with mainstream wellness culture rather than at a radical extreme from it. The epistemological framework that makes jade eggs seem plausible, a combination of anti-establishment skepticism about conventional medicine, appeal to ancient wisdom, and testimonial evidence presented as more authentic than clinical trials, is continuous with the framework operating throughout the wellness industry at varying degrees of extremity.

The Detox Myth and the Supplement Industrial Complex

Few wellness concepts have been more commercially successful and less scientifically supportable than detoxification. The human body has dedicated detoxification systems, primarily the liver and kidneys, that process and eliminate waste products continuously. The concept of "toxins," as used in wellness marketing, is almost entirely undefined: no specific toxins are named, no mechanism of accumulation is proposed and no measurement of toxin levels is offered before or after the intervention. Edzard Ernst, the physician and researcher who has studied alternative medicine for decades, has noted that whenever he asks detox practitioners which toxins their protocols remove and in what quantities, they cannot answer. The answer, in most cases, is that the concept does not correspond to any real physiological process that the marketed intervention affects.

The dietary supplement industry in the United States operates under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994, which does not require supplements to be proven safe or effective before sale. The FDA can take action after a supplement is on the market if it proves harmful, but the burden of proof is reversed relative to pharmaceutical regulation. This regulatory structure, the result of intense industry lobbying, has produced a $50 billion US market in which most products have limited evidence of efficacy for the claims made about them. A 2015 investigation of herbal supplements by the New York Attorney General found that four out of five products tested at major retailers did not contain the herbs listed on their labels. A 2017 JAMA analysis of supplements marketed for weight loss and muscle building found that 776 had FDA-identified adulteration with prescription drugs, controlled substances or unapproved pharmaceuticals between 2007 and 2016.

Wellness and Class

The wellness industry is one of the most explicitly class-stratified consumer markets in the modern economy, a fact that its spiritual and democratic rhetoric systematically obscures. The aspiration of optimal health is presented as universally available through the right choices, while the material costs of those choices range from the substantial to the prohibitive. A Peloton subscription costs roughly $44 per month after a $1,495 bike purchase. Functional medicine consultations typically cost $300-600 per hour and are rarely covered by insurance. Organic produce costs an average of 47 percent more than conventionally grown equivalents according to Consumer Reports research. Wellness retreats at recognized destinations cost thousands of dollars per day. The "clean eating" philosophy, which prescribes minimally processed whole foods, is most straightforwardly implemented by people with the time, kitchen facilities and grocery access to prepare meals from scratch, which correlates strongly with socioeconomic position.

The sociologist Jen Gunnels has described this as "wellness as class performance," arguing that the adoption of particular wellness practices functions as a form of cultural capital that signals membership in an educated, health-conscious class, independent of the actual health benefits the practices produce. The glass of green juice in the Instagram post, the yoga mat visible in the background of the Zoom call, the mention of a meditation practice: these signal a relationship to the body and to health that carries class meaning beyond the physical outcomes. This is not purely cynical. People genuinely pursue health. But the class signaling and health pursuit are bundled inseparably, which means that class-accessible version of wellness, the subsidized gym membership or the walking program, carries less social cachet than the expensive version regardless of equivalent health benefits.

"I worked as a wellness coach for four years and the thing I eventually couldn't square was that my clients were overwhelmingly upper-middle-class white women optimizing already-good health, while people dying of preventable conditions from lack of access to basic healthcare were completely outside the wellness ecosystem. The industry talks about democratizing wellness but it's fundamentally a luxury product." r/nutrition • 19,293 upvotes

The Medicalization of Normal Experience

One of the wellness industry's most significant effects on public health discourse is the medicalization of experiences previously considered normal aspects of human life. Aging, for instance, has been progressively reframed in wellness culture from an inevitable biological process to a condition that can be significantly slowed through the right interventions, generating a longevity industry that produced $25 billion in revenue in 2021. The research basis for most popular longevity interventions, including NAD+ precursors, rapamycin use in healthy adults, most peptide therapies and numerous "biohacking" protocols promoted by figures like Bryan Johnson, is either preliminary, contested or extrapolated from animal models in ways that may not translate to humans. The risk of harm from pursuing aggressive longevity interventions without medical supervision is real. But the marketing precedes the evidence because the commercial opportunity is available now and the evidence base will take decades to develop.

Ordinary psychological states have undergone similar medicalization. Sadness associated with identifiable life events was not historically considered a medical problem requiring intervention, but the expansion of depression diagnosis and treatment to include subclinical states, combined with pharmaceutical marketing directly to consumers after 1997's FDA ruling permitting direct-to-consumer advertising, has created a cultural framework in which a wide range of negative emotional states are considered medical problems. Wellness culture simultaneously criticizes pharmaceutical treatment of psychological states and offers its own alternative interventions, from adaptogens and nootropics to ayahuasca retreats and somatic therapy, that position the same range of normal human suffering as a condition requiring professional treatment and product purchase.

When Wellness Becomes Harmful

The wellness industry's most serious harm is not its pseudoscience, irritating as that is. It is the category of cases where wellness culture delays or prevents engagement with effective conventional treatment. The orthorexia nervosa phenomenon, an obsessive preoccupation with eating correctly that was formally described in 1997 by Steven Bratman, is partly a product of wellness culture's moral framework around food, in which "clean" eating is a form of virtue and any deviation is a contamination requiring correction. The anti-vaccine movement draws substantially from wellness culture's general framework of distrust toward pharmaceutical medicine and preference for "natural" immunity, a convergence that became acutely visible during the COVID-19 pandemic. The promotion of "alternative" cancer treatments by wellness practitioners has repeatedly contributed to patients forgoing conventional treatment during windows when it would have been most effective.

The psychologist Steven Bratman, who coined orthorexia, has written extensively about how the same values promoted by wellness culture, attention to ingredients, preference for whole foods, commitment to purity, translate into pathology when they become rigid, all-consuming and disconnected from evidence. The line between health-conscious behavior and health-damaging obsession is a matter of degree rather than kind, and wellness culture's tendency to frame health behaviors in moral terms (clean vs. toxic, pure vs. contaminated, natural vs. artificial) creates the conditions for those behaviors to escalate beyond any point of diminishing returns into active harm.

The Evidence-Based Middle

The honest account of wellness neither dismisses the entire industry as fraud nor accepts its most extreme claims. Physical activity is one of the most well-established health interventions available, with evidence of benefit across cardiovascular health, mental health, cognitive function, metabolic health and longevity. Dietary patterns emphasizing whole foods, vegetables, legumes and minimal processed food have consistent evidence of benefit. Adequate sleep has such pervasive effects on health, cognitive function and mental health that its consistent underselling by both hustle culture and the wellness industry is remarkable. Stress reduction through whatever mechanism works for a given person, whether mindfulness meditation, exercise, social connection or simply adequate vacation time, has measurable health benefits. These interventions are mostly cheap, accessible and unglamorous, which is probably why they are not the primary products of a $4.5 trillion industry.

The wellness industry's genuine contribution has been to expand the mainstream cultural understanding of health beyond the purely biomedical. The recognition that psychological states, relationships, meaning and environmental factors matter for physical health, that the body and mind are not separate systems, and that prevention is more valuable than treatment has moved from alternative medicine fringe to mainstream medicine consensus over the past thirty years, driven partly by genuine research and partly by cultural pressure from wellness consumers. The challenge is to hold onto these insights while discarding the pseudoscience, the class barriers, the perpetual inadequacy marketing and the exploitation of genuine healthcare system failures that have made the wellness industry, at its worst, a very profitable way of making healthy people feel sick.
