Can We Actually Change the Narrative?

13 min read • Meta-Narratives
▲ 79,493 found this hopeful

"Change the narrative" has become one of the most overused phrases in contemporary public discourse. Communications consultants charge handsomely to change their clients' narratives. Political campaigns promise to change the national narrative. Advocates for social justice movements argue that changing the narrative about their communities is a precondition for policy change. The phrase is everywhere, deployed with the confidence of people who believe they understand what narratives are, how they work and what changing them requires. That confidence is usually misplaced, not because narratives cannot change, but because the people most confident about their ability to change them are often the least clear about the mechanisms through which narrative change actually occurs, and most liable to mistake the declaration of a new narrative for its actual establishment.

What is a cultural narrative in the first place? The term is used loosely to mean anything from the specific stories told about particular events to the broad frameworks of assumption that shape what seems natural, inevitable or morally obvious in a given culture. The cognitive linguist George Lakoff distinguishes between surface frames, specific arguments and messages, and deep frames, the underlying metaphors and assumptions that determine which surface frames seem natural and which seem foreign. When conservatives framed inheritance tax as a "death tax," they were not just choosing a new label. They were activating a deep frame in which taxation is a form of punishment and death is already a sufficient burden. The liberal "estate tax" framing activates a different deep frame, in which inherited wealth concentration is a social problem and redistribution is a form of fairness. No single message changes a deep frame. Frames are changed through repeated reinforcement over time, through the stories that make a frame emotionally vivid and through the gradual accumulation of what feels common-sensical to a population.

"The thing that strikes me about actual narrative change is how slow it looks from inside and how fast it looks from outside. Same-sex marriage went from majority opposition to majority support in like 10 years in the US. The people who worked on that shift for decades thought they were losing the whole time. Then suddenly they'd won." r/changemyview • 18,847 upvotes

The marriage equality movement is probably the most extensively studied recent example of rapid narrative change in the United States. In 1996, the Defense of Marriage Act passed with overwhelming congressional support and was signed by a Democratic president. In 1996, Gallup polling showed only 27 percent of Americans supported same-sex marriage. By 2015, when the Supreme Court's Obergefell v. Hodges decision established it as a constitutional right, support had reached 60 percent. By 2023 it stood at 71 percent. This is a dramatic shift in public opinion over roughly two decades, in a country where fundamental value shifts typically take much longer. The people who produced this shift can explain what they did, and the explanation is more complicated than "we changed the narrative."

The Mechanisms of Narrative Change

The political strategist Anat Shenker-Osorio, whose research on progressive messaging draws on cognitive linguistics and experimental communication research, describes narrative change as operating through several simultaneous mechanisms that reinforce each other rather than through any single intervention. The first is the repeated articulation of a clear values-based frame that connects the specific issue to broader values the target audience already holds. The marriage equality movement was initially framed around rights and legal equality, which resonated with a rights-based American political tradition but felt abstract and legalistic. The reframe toward love, commitment and family as universal values that gay couples expressed in forms identical to straight couples was more emotionally accessible and harder to oppose without seeming to oppose love itself.

The second mechanism, closely related to Kwame Anthony Appiah's analysis of historical moral revolutions, was personal relationship. Research conducted for the marriage equality movement found that knowing someone who is gay was the single strongest predictor of supporting same-sex marriage, stronger than any demographic or attitudinal variable. This finding drove a strategic shift toward encouraging gay people to come out to their families, friends and communities, not just for personal authenticity reasons but as a deliberate political strategy. The "It Gets Better" project, launched by Dan Savage in 2010, was one manifestation of this strategy. The Human Rights Campaign's emphasis on personal stories was another. When a grandparent has a gay grandchild they love, the abstract narrative about gay people as threatening to marriage and family becomes personally impossible to maintain. Personal relationship is, empirically, one of the most reliable narrative-change mechanisms available.

When Elites Lead and When They Follow

A persistent debate in the study of public opinion and narrative change is whether elite opinion leads mass opinion or follows it. The elitist theory holds that most people do not have coherent, considered views on most political questions, and that their expressed opinions are largely cues taken from political and media elites they trust or identify with. The populist theory holds that elite opinion is itself largely responsive to underlying shifts in public values that powerful interests then ratify or contest. The empirical reality is more complicated than either theory suggests, with the causal direction varying by issue, era and the specific configuration of institutional power.

The tobacco industry's decades-long campaign to manufacture doubt about the causal link between smoking and cancer, documented in Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway's "Merchants of Doubt" (2010), is a case study in elite narrative management successfully delaying mass opinion change. The industry did not persuade most scientists. It created sufficient public confusion about scientific consensus that political action was delayed by decades, during which tens of millions of people developed smoking-related diseases. The fossil fuel industry's substantially similar campaign on climate change, documented in the same book and subsequently confirmed by internal industry documents, has been even more consequential. These cases suggest that well-resourced interests can successfully maintain damaging narratives well past the point at which evidence should have shifted them, by exploiting the complexity of scientific communication and the structural limitations of journalism.

"What the tobacco and climate cases show is that the question isn't really 'can narratives change' but 'who has the power to prevent narratives from changing when it's in their financial interest to do so.' The answer to that question is darker and more structural than most narrative-change discourse acknowledges." r/collapse • 24,293 upvotes

The counterpoint to this structural pessimism is the history of movements that changed narratives against entrenched elite resistance. The labor movement changed the narrative about the relationship between employers and workers over the late nineteenth and early twentieth century, shifting what seemed natural about working hours, safety conditions and collective bargaining from a framework in which employer authority was nearly absolute to one in which worker rights were recognized as legitimate. The civil rights movement changed the narrative about race and citizenship over roughly two decades of sustained organizing, litigation, civil disobedience and communication strategy. The environmental movement, beginning with Rachel Carson's "Silent Spring" (1962), changed the narrative about industrial pollution from an inevitable cost of progress to a preventable harm requiring regulation. These narrative changes were produced against fierce opposition from well-resourced interests and required sustained effort over years and decades. They were also real, durable and consequential for millions of people.

The Role of Crisis in Accelerating Change

Narrative change often accelerates during crises, when existing frameworks visibly fail to explain or address what is happening. The Great Depression discredited the narrative of laissez-faire capitalism sufficiently to create the political conditions for the New Deal's expansion of government economic intervention. The Vietnam War discredited the Cold War consensus narrative about American military intervention sufficiently to produce a period of significant foreign policy restraint. The AIDS crisis, which was initially managed by a government narrative of inaction and affected communities' irrelevance, generated a form of activist organizing, ACT UP's direct action campaigns beginning in 1987, that changed the FDA's drug approval process in ways that still benefit all patients. COVID-19 discredited several dominant cultural narratives simultaneously, including the narrative that individual resilience and market mechanisms could manage public health threats without collective action, though the direction of subsequent narrative change was contested and the new narratives that emerged were more fragmented than unified.

The narrative scholar Marshall Ganz, who studied organizing with Cesar Chavez and later developed a framework for public narrative called "story of self, story of us, story of now," argues that crisis creates narrative openings that skilled organizers can fill. The "story of now" element is specifically about the urgency created by a moment in which a decision must be made and a choice about the future is available. Without a felt sense of now, of this moment being consequential and contested, the story of self and the story of us remain motivationally inert. Crisis provides the now. The question is whether the available narrative infrastructure, the stories, the frames, the people with the relationships to tell them, is in place to fill the opening that crisis creates.

The Limits of Narrative-First Strategy

The critique of "narrative change" as a political strategy comes from several directions simultaneously. From the structural left, the argument is that narratives reflect underlying material conditions and power relations rather than independently shaping them, and that focusing on narrative without addressing the material conditions produces a politics of symbolism that leaves structural arrangements intact. From the pragmatic center, the argument is that narrative strategies often overestimate the persuadability of people who are not already sympathetically disposed and underestimate the importance of institutions, laws and organizational capacity in producing durable change. From the communication research tradition, the argument is that narrative change is much slower and harder than it looks when described retrospectively, and that the confidence with which advocates announce they are "changing the narrative" is typically premature.

The advertising researcher Les Binet and Peter Field's research on brand communication, while not directly about political narrative, is instructive here. Their analysis of the Institute of Practitioners in Advertising's effectiveness database found that brand building, which works through the emotional salience of repeated long-term exposure rather than rational argument, requires consistent investment over years to produce durable effects. Single campaigns rarely shift brand perception significantly. The aggregate effect of consistent communication over time, combined with genuine product or organizational quality that the communication reflects rather than fabricates, produces the durable associations that make a brand trusted. The analogy for narrative change in political or social contexts is that the "campaigns" and "messaging strategies" that advocates deploy are most effective when they are layered on top of decades of consistent organizing, relationship-building and demonstrated commitment that the narrative reflects rather than invents.

What Actually Works

The research on successful narrative change, synthesized from social movement history, communication science and public opinion research, points toward a fairly consistent set of factors. Effective narrative change is personal: it works through relationships rather than through broadcast communication alone. It is long-term: it requires sustained investment over years, not campaigns measured in news cycles. It connects the specific to the universal: the most effective frames link concrete material conditions to widely shared values rather than appealing to identity or abstract principles alone. It requires credible messengers: communication from trusted community members, religious leaders, doctors or family members is more narrative-changing than communication from political advocates or media figures, because people update on evidence from sources whose testimony they trust and discount evidence from sources they believe have something to gain from persuading them.

And narrative change works when it is paired with organizational capacity. The story of the marriage equality movement is not only a story of changed messaging. It is a story of sustained legal strategy through organizations like Lambda Legal and the ACLU, electoral organizing in key states, legislative lobbying, philanthropic investment and the construction of a movement infrastructure that could deploy resources strategically over decades. The narrative shift was real and necessary. It was not sufficient on its own. The question for anyone asking "can we change the narrative?" is less about the narrative than about the organizational infrastructure and long-term commitment through which narrative change is produced. That question is harder and less glamorous, but it is the right one.
