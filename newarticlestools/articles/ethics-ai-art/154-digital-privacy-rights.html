Digital Privacy as a Human Right: The Case for Taking It Seriously

13 min read  ·  Privacy  ·  Digital Rights
68,947 upvotes
Advertisement

The most common response to privacy concerns on the internet is a shrug: "I have nothing to hide." It is such a widespread response that privacy scholars have developed an entire genre of rebuttals to it. The philosopher Daniel Solove has a short book titled "Nothing to Hide: The False Tradeoff Between Privacy and Security." The legal scholar Neil Richards calls the nothing-to-hide argument "one of the most pervasive and destructive arguments in modern discussions of privacy." Edward Snowden, in a 2015 interview, offered what may be the most memorable counter: "Saying you don't care about privacy because you have nothing to hide is like saying you don't care about free speech because you have nothing to say."

The persistence of the nothing-to-hide argument reflects something genuinely important about how most people in stable liberal democracies experience privacy: as a personal convenience rather than a structural necessity. If you are not doing anything that would attract state or corporate attention, if you belong to no targeted minority group, if your political views are mainstream and your behavior is conventional, the erosion of privacy may feel largely theoretical. You go about your life undisturbed and conclude that the people who talk urgently about privacy rights are either unusually paranoid or have something to hide.

This experience is real but misleading. Privacy's value is most visible in its absence, and its absence tends to become visible at the worst possible moments. Understanding privacy as a serious political and ethical concept requires taking seriously not just your own current situation but the structural role privacy plays in enabling the exercise of every other right, and not just in your current political context but in the full range of political contexts that history suggests are possible.

What Privacy Actually Is

Privacy is routinely conflated with secrecy, which is part of why the nothing-to-hide argument seems so compelling. Secrecy means hiding things that, if known, would lead to negative consequences — guilt, embarrassment, punishment. If privacy were primarily about secrecy, then people who are not doing anything wrong would have little reason to value it.

But privacy scholars argue that privacy is more fundamentally about control and context. Alan Westin's influential 1967 definition describes privacy as "the claim of individuals, groups, or institutions to determine for themselves when, how, and to what extent information about them is communicated to others." Helen Nissenbaum's contextual integrity framework, developed in her 2010 book "Privacy in Context," argues that privacy norms are fundamentally about appropriate information flows within specific social contexts rather than about secrets. Medical information shared with a doctor flows appropriately to other treating physicians but not to your employer. Personal struggles shared in a support group flow appropriately among members but not to the internet at large. Intimate conversations flow appropriately between the people having them but not to a platform's advertising partners.

Privacy violations, on this account, occur not when secret wrongdoing is exposed but when information flows in ways that violate the contextual norms under which it was shared. A person who discusses their medical history in a doctor's office has not "hidden" anything. They have shared information in a context where sharing was appropriate. When that information is subsequently sold to an insurance company or an employer, the privacy violation is not about secrecy but about the inappropriate migration of information across contextual boundaries.

My medical history, my financial situation, my location patterns, my private communications — none of this is secret wrongdoing, but all of it is information I want to control who has access to. Privacy as the Enabler of Other Rights

The human rights case for privacy rests not just on the intrinsic value of personal autonomy but on privacy's structural role in enabling the exercise of other rights. Free speech requires privacy because speech is chilled when speakers know they are being monitored. Judicial opinions finding broad surveillance powers unconstitutional regularly note that the knowledge of surveillance deters the expression of unpopular or nonconformist views even when no specific punishment for expression exists. The deterrence operates through self-censorship.

Research has documented this chilling effect empirically. A 2016 study by Elizabeth Stoycheff found that after the Snowden revelations about NSA surveillance, search traffic for topics the government might find suspicious declined among users who were aware of the surveillance. People were changing their search behavior not because their searches were illegal but because they feared how their information might be used. The privacy violation was already occurring — government collection of search data was already happening — but awareness of it produced behavioral changes consistent with the self-censorship that surveillance is theoretically supposed to cause only when it is deployed punitively.

Freedom of association similarly depends on privacy. When membership in unpopular or targeted organizations can be known, that knowledge deters membership. The NAACP's 1950s battles with Southern state governments to avoid disclosing its membership lists — a fight that reached the Supreme Court in NAACP v. Alabama in 1958 — illustrate the relationship between associational privacy and the ability to organize around disfavored causes. The government sought the membership lists not to punish members directly but knowing that exposure of membership would cause employers, landlords and neighbors to punish members through private action.

Political privacy — the secret ballot — is so fundamental to democratic governance that it is nearly universally protected even in democracies with weak civil liberties protections in other domains. The logic is the same: if voting choices are known to those who have power over voters, the exercise of voting choices is systematically compromised by fear of retaliation. Privacy here is not about hiding wrongdoing; it is about enabling a structural precondition for meaningful democratic participation.

The Targeting Problem

One of the most important practical arguments for privacy as a structural right rather than merely a personal preference is what might be called the targeting problem. Privacy is not equally valuable to all people in all circumstances. It is most valuable to people who are in some way vulnerable to targeting — members of minority religious or political groups, people whose sexual orientation or gender identity is not accepted in their community, undocumented immigrants, journalists with sensitive sources, activists in authoritarian states, abuse survivors hiding from their abusers, people with stigmatized health conditions or mental health histories.

The nothing-to-hide argument implicitly assumes a mainstream majority perspective. If you belong to a group that has reason to fear targeting — by government, by employers, by private actors with the ability and motivation to cause you harm — then privacy is not an abstract concern but a concrete safety requirement. The erosion of privacy in the digital environment does not affect all people equally. It concentrates risk on those who are already most vulnerable.

This dynamic has become visible in several concrete ways. Data broker databases containing sensitive personal information have been used by stalkers and domestic abusers to locate victims who had relocated to escape them. Government surveillance programs built with a stated purpose of counter-terrorism have been used to monitor civil rights activists, journalists and political dissidents. Location data sold by data brokers has been used to identify and target people who visited abortion clinics in states where abortion access is contested. Employer use of social media monitoring to screen job applicants systematically disadvantages people from certain cultural backgrounds or religious traditions where social media expression is more personal and less managed.

The Aggregation Problem

A particular challenge in understanding digital privacy is the aggregation problem — the fact that individually innocuous pieces of information become highly sensitive when combined. Knowing someone's name is harmless. Knowing their employer is harmless. Knowing their neighborhood is harmless. Knowing their physical description is harmless. Knowing their daily commute route is harmless. Knowing the gym they attend is harmless. But knowing all of these things together creates a profile detailed enough to facilitate stalking, targeted harassment or worse.

Data brokers aggregate exactly this kind of information from dozens of sources — public records, consumer transactions, social media, location data from mobile apps, electoral rolls and many others — and compile it into individual profiles that are available for purchase. The people whose profiles are compiled have not made any single disclosure that they would recognize as privacy-invasive. Each individual piece of information was disclosed in a specific context for a specific purpose. The aggregation into a comprehensive profile is itself the privacy violation, and it occurs without the subject's knowledge or consent.

Nissenbaum's contextual integrity framework captures this dynamic precisely: the relevant question is not whether each piece of information was technically shared in some context but whether the aggregated profile respects the norms of any context in which the individual pieces were originally shared. A person who disclosed their location to a mapping app to get driving directions did not, by any reasonable interpretation, consent to that location data being combined with their purchase history, their medical searches and their communications metadata to build a profile sold to employers and insurers.

The International Human Rights Framework

Privacy is formally recognized as a human right under international law. Article 12 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights states that "no one shall be subjected to arbitrary interference with his privacy, family, home or correspondence." Article 17 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which has legal force for ratifying states, provides nearly identical protection. The UN Human Rights Committee has interpreted this provision broadly to cover digital communications and electronic data.

In 2013, following the Snowden revelations, the United Nations General Assembly adopted a resolution affirming that privacy rights apply online as well as offline and that mass surveillance programs may violate the right to privacy regardless of whether they target citizens or non-citizens. In 2015, the Human Rights Council appointed a Special Rapporteur on the Right to Privacy, who has issued reports on digital surveillance, data protection and the relationship between privacy and freedom of expression.

These international frameworks have limited practical enforceability, particularly against the most powerful actors — the governments and corporations that operate the world's largest surveillance infrastructure. But they provide normative benchmarks that have influenced domestic law in many countries and that provide a basis for advocacy and litigation that purely domestic frameworks may not afford.

The European Court of Human Rights has issued significant rulings applying the right to privacy under Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights to digital surveillance, finding that the UK's mass surveillance program violated the Convention in 2021 and imposing requirements on how member states may conduct targeted and bulk interception of communications. These rulings have practical effects within the Council of Europe's jurisdiction and indirect effects on the behavior of intelligence agencies in allied countries.

GDPR: Promise and Limits

The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation remains the most ambitious regulatory framework for digital privacy rights enacted by any major jurisdiction. Its core principles — data minimization, purpose limitation, consent requirements, rights of access and deletion, data portability and breach notification — represent a serious attempt to operationalize privacy as a right rather than a preference.

The regulation has produced meaningful effects. Google and Facebook face ongoing enforcement proceedings that have resulted in billions of euros in fines. The Data Protection Authorities of member states have developed enforcement capacity that did not exist previously. Hundreds of enforcement actions have been brought against organizations across multiple sectors for GDPR violations. The law has influenced privacy regulation in dozens of other countries — Brazil, India, South Korea and many others have enacted data protection frameworks modeled partly on GDPR.

But GDPR has also revealed the limits of consent-based privacy regulation. The framework requires that data collection be based on a legal basis — most commonly consent or legitimate interest — and that consent be freely given, specific and informed. In practice, the consent mechanism has been largely captured by dark pattern design: cookie consent banners structured to make accepting all cookies easy and declining difficult, consent flows that present rejecting data collection as a lengthy multi-step process while accepting is a single click, "legitimate interest" claims that are used to bypass consent requirements for purposes far removed from what users would recognize as legitimate.

The deeper problem is that individual consent is not an adequate framework for collective privacy problems. When social media platforms collect behavioral data on virtually the entire population and use it to build prediction models that affect credit, employment and insurance outcomes for everyone, individual opt-out choices cannot address the systemic effect. A person who opts out of all behavioral tracking is still affected by systems trained on data from people who did not opt out. Privacy at scale is a collective good that requires collective solutions — regulatory requirements, structural restrictions on data collection and use — rather than individual consumer choices.

I'm a data protection lawyer and I can tell you the consent framework in GDPR was always going to be gamed. The entire concept of "informed consent" to complex data processing is aspirational when you have tens of thousands of data brokers, hundreds of ad tech intermediaries and processing purposes that are deliberately obscured. You cannot meaningfully consent to something you cannot understand. The consent model is a political compromise that gave industry a way to comply formally while changing almost nothing substantively. — r/law, 37,483 upvotes

The United States Exceptionalism Problem

The United States presents a distinctive case in the global privacy landscape: the world's most economically powerful digital economy, the home of most of the world's largest data-collecting corporations, and one of the few developed democracies with no comprehensive federal privacy law. The American approach to privacy is sectoral rather than general: federal law specifically protects health information (HIPAA), children's online data (COPPA), financial information (Gramm-Leach-Bliley) and a handful of other specific categories. The vast majority of commercial behavioral data collection operates with no federal legal restriction beyond the FTC's authority over deceptive trade practices.

This exceptionalism is not accidental. It reflects the political influence of the technology and advertising industries, which have successfully lobbied against comprehensive federal privacy legislation for more than two decades. When federal legislation has come close to passing, industry-preferred provisions — federal preemption of stronger state laws, no private right of action for individuals, voluntary compliance over mandatory requirements — have been advocated as the price of industry support and have produced versions of the legislation that privacy advocates argue would be worse than the current patchwork.

California has filled part of the gap with the California Consumer Privacy Act and its successor, the California Privacy Rights Act, which provide California residents with rights to know what data is collected about them, to delete it and to opt out of its sale. Several other states have enacted similar laws. This state-level patchwork creates compliance complexity for companies but does not provide consistent national protection and lacks the strong enforcement mechanisms that GDPR provides through its data protection authority structure.

Surveillance, Power and the Permanent Record Problem

One of the most significant differences between pre-digital and digital surveillance is what might be called the permanent record problem. Before digital data collection, information about individual behavior was transient unless specifically and deliberately recorded. A conversation not overheard left no trace. A trip not documented in writing was unknown to anyone not present. A purchase made in cash was unrecorded. The default state of information about people was ephemerality.

The default state has reversed. In a world of smartphones, connected services, digital payment systems and ubiquitous cameras, the default is now permanent recording. Every digital communication leaves a trace in server logs. Every location accessed through a smartphone leaves a record with the carrier. Every search query is logged. Every purchase made through a card or digital payment system is recorded. Accessing information online creates a record of having sought that information.

This reversal has profound implications for the relationship between individuals and power. The powerful have always maintained records about those subject to their power; the innovation of comprehensive digital surveillance is that the record is now continuous rather than episodic, comprehensive rather than selective and permanently searchable rather than difficult to retrieve. A government with access to ten years of an individual's communication records, location history, purchase history and search history knows things about that person that no one in history has been able to know about any individual short of comprehensive physical surveillance.

The implications differ depending on the government's character. In a stable liberal democracy with strong rule of law, this information is subject to legal constraints — warrant requirements, use limitations, independent oversight — that limit its use to legitimate law enforcement purposes. But the permanent record exists independently of the political context. A democracy that survives can look back through surveillance records accumulated during its democratic period if it becomes less democratic. A government that changes character does not need to begin surveillance from scratch; it inherits the surveillance infrastructure accumulated by its predecessors.

This argument does not require that any specific government intend to become authoritarian. It requires only that political circumstances can change, that surveillance infrastructure persists across political transitions and that comprehensive behavioral records accumulated under one set of political conditions can be weaponized under different political conditions. Historical examples are not difficult to find: the Stasi's surveillance records were used to maintain social control long after the initial political rationale for their collection; census data collected in multiple countries was used to facilitate persecution of targeted minorities during subsequent periods of political violence.

Reclaiming Privacy as a Collective Project

Privacy advocacy is often framed as an individual project — using privacy tools, changing personal settings, opting out of data collection. These individual actions have real but limited value. The structural problem with this framing is that privacy in the modern data environment is a collective good whose erosion affects everyone regardless of individual choices, and whose protection requires collective solutions.

Those collective solutions operate at multiple levels. Legal reform — comprehensive federal privacy legislation in the United States, stronger enforcement of existing frameworks in the EU and elsewhere, international data governance agreements that constrain the most powerful actors — is the most important level because it changes the default rules rather than requiring individual opt-out choices. Technical standards — privacy-preserving computation techniques, data minimization standards built into platform infrastructure, default end-to-end encryption — can make privacy the easy path rather than the difficult one. Economic restructuring — reducing the dominance of surveillance-based advertising as the primary funding model for digital services, supporting alternative funding models like subscriptions and public funding — addresses the underlying incentive structure that drives data collection.

None of these changes is imminent. The political economy of digital privacy favors the status quo: the entities that benefit most from comprehensive data collection are large and politically influential, while the people who bear the costs are numerous but diffuse and often unaware of the mechanisms affecting them. But the recognition of privacy as a fundamental human right rather than a personal preference or a consumer protection issue changes the normative stakes in ways that matter for the longer-term political trajectory.

Privacy is not primarily about hiding wrongdoing. It is about the structural conditions that make it possible for individuals to develop their own views, associate with others on their own terms, hold power accountable and live without the chilling effects of continuous monitoring. These conditions are prerequisites for the kind of human flourishing and democratic self-governance that most people, across political differences, claim to value. The question is whether we will notice their erosion before the erosion becomes irreversible.
