Deepfakes and the Synthetic Media Crisis

12 min read  ·  Synthetic Media  ·  Technology Ethics
79,482 upvotes
Advertisement

The word "deepfake" entered common usage in late 2017, when a Reddit user using that handle began posting synthetic pornographic videos featuring celebrities' faces swapped onto adult performers' bodies. The technology enabling those videos — generative adversarial networks trained to map faces onto source footage — had been published in academic papers. The Reddit user had made it accessible to non-experts. Within months, the technique had spread widely enough that Motherboard published an investigative piece documenting the growing ecosystem of non-consensual synthetic pornography.

That original use case — the non-consensual sexualization of real people through synthetic media — remains the dominant real-world harm from deepfakes despite the years of coverage focused on political disinformation. Estimates from 2023 research suggest that roughly 96 percent of deepfake videos circulating online are non-consensual pornography, and that 99 percent of the targets are women. The gap between this reality and the public discourse, which has focused heavily on the threat of deepfake political propaganda, tells something important about whose harms get prioritized and whose get minimized.

Understanding deepfakes requires separating the technology from its uses, the actual current harms from the hypothetical future harms and the cases where technical countermeasures are feasible from those where they are not. None of these separations are clean, but making them honestly is necessary for thinking clearly about what synthetic media technology means and what should be done about it.

How the Technology Works

The term "deepfake" originally referred specifically to face-swap videos created using deep learning — hence the name. But the category has expanded to include a range of synthetic media technologies. Voice cloning systems can generate a convincing reproduction of a specific person's voice from as little as a few seconds of audio. Text-to-speech models can be fine-tuned on a person's voice samples to produce speech in their voice saying anything. Video generation systems can produce realistic video of people saying or doing things that never happened. Audio and video generation can be combined to produce entirely synthetic audiovisual content featuring real individuals.

The underlying mechanism for most sophisticated deepfakes involves generative adversarial networks or, more recently, diffusion models. A GAN consists of two neural networks in competition: a generator that produces synthetic content and a discriminator that tries to distinguish the synthetic content from real content. Through iterative competition, both networks improve — the generator gets better at producing convincing fakes and the discriminator gets better at detecting them, driving the generator to improve further. The result is a system that can produce synthetic media that defeats human detection.

Diffusion models, which underlie newer systems like Stable Diffusion and DALL-E, work differently: they learn to reverse a process of gradually adding noise to images, allowing them to generate new images by starting from random noise and systematically removing it according to learned patterns. These models have produced dramatic improvements in image quality and are increasingly being applied to video generation. The computational resources required to run these models have dropped rapidly enough that sophisticated deepfake creation no longer requires specialized hardware or expertise.

I do forensic media analysis professionally and I want to correct a common misconception: detecting deepfakes is not getting easier as the technology improves, it's getting harder. Every time we develop better detectors, people improve the generation side. We're in an arms race we're probably not winning. The realistic near-term goal is not "reliably detect deepfakes" but "make producing convincing ones require enough effort that casual abuse becomes harder." The Harm Hierarchy: Who Is Actually Being Hurt

Researcher Danielle Citron, who has written extensively on what she calls "deepfake sexual abuse," argues that the dominant framing of deepfakes as primarily a political disinformation problem reflects a systematic devaluation of harms experienced primarily by women. The harms from non-consensual synthetic pornography are immediate, severe and well-documented. Targets describe loss of employment, damaged relationships, psychological trauma, harassment and in some cases stalking and physical violence that follows online targeting.

The mechanics of harm are specific and worth understanding. When a deepfake pornographic video featuring a real person is distributed online, removing it is extraordinarily difficult. Major platforms have policies against non-consensual intimate imagery, but enforcement is inconsistent and labor-intensive. New platforms and hosting services operate in jurisdictions with weaker enforcement. The video propagates through peer-to-peer sharing and dark web repositories beyond the reach of any single platform's moderation. Victims face the ongoing burden of monitoring and requesting removal across dozens of sites indefinitely.

The psychological effects are comparable to those documented in studies of non-consensual pornography involving real images. Victims report hypervigilance, intrusive thoughts, depression and a pervasive sense of being violated and exposed in public. Many report changing their behavior — leaving social media, changing jobs, moving — to try to limit the reach of the content. Some report that partners, family members and employers encounter the content, compounding the damage. The harm is not abstract or theoretical; it is concrete, ongoing and life-altering.

Celebrity deepfakes receive attention because of the subjects' public profiles, but the technology is increasingly being used to target private individuals. Ex-partners, workplace rivals, people with online public profiles that provide training images and young women who appear in any publicly accessible photos are all potential targets. As the technical barrier to creating convincing deepfakes continues to drop, the population of potential perpetrators expands.

Political Disinformation: The Actual Evidence

The disinformation framing has dominated policy discussions partly because it appeals to concerns about democratic governance that carry more institutional weight than individual harm to non-famous women, and partly because early academic and journalistic coverage was heavily focused on hypothetical future harms from political deepfakes rather than on documenting current harms.

What does the actual evidence show? Several high-profile cases of deepfake political disinformation have occurred. Audio deepfakes of political figures making false statements have circulated in countries including Slovakia, Bangladesh and Nigeria around election periods. A deepfake video of Ukrainian President Zelensky purportedly calling on Ukrainian soldiers to surrender circulated early in the Russian invasion of Ukraine. In each case, the content was identified as fake relatively quickly and its actual impact on public opinion appeared limited.

This relatively modest documented impact on political opinion does not mean the risk is negligible. Research on political deepfakes suggests they are most effective in lower-attention contexts — local elections, partisan media ecosystems where fact-checking is limited, situations where the target audience already holds beliefs consistent with the fake content. A convincing deepfake of a political figure making a compromising statement, released hours before an election, might influence outcomes in a close race before effective debunking could spread.

The more pervasive effect may be what researchers call the "liar's dividend" — the possibility that the mere existence of sophisticated deepfake technology allows bad actors to deny authentic evidence by claiming it is fabricated. A politician caught on video saying something damaging can now more plausibly claim the video is a deepfake, introducing doubt where none is warranted. This effect is difficult to measure directly but potentially significant for accountability journalism and legal proceedings that rely on audiovisual evidence.

The liar's dividend thing keeps me up at night more than actual deepfakes. We already live in a world where powerful people can say "fake news" about real reporting and substantial numbers of people will believe them. Deepfake technology gives that denial more technical plausibility. The damage to epistemic trust isn't primarily from people being fooled by fakes — it's from everyone being unsure whether anything visual can be trusted. — r/journalism, 41,847 upvotes

Voice Cloning and Audio Deepfakes

Audio deepfakes have received less attention than video deepfakes but are in some ways more immediately dangerous. Voice cloning technology has advanced to the point that a convincing reproduction of a person's voice can be generated from as little as 30 seconds of audio. This creates obvious risks for fraud and manipulation that go beyond political disinformation.

The FBI issued a warning in 2023 about "vishing" attacks — voice phishing — using AI-generated voice clones. In documented cases, scammers have called elderly individuals using cloned voices of their family members, claiming to be in emergency situations and requesting wire transfers. "Grandparent scams" using this technique have resulted in significant financial losses. A 2023 case involved a cloned voice of an executive being used to authorize a $243,000 wire transfer. The technology lowers the skill barrier for social engineering attacks significantly, because generating a convincing voice clone no longer requires acting ability or even prolonged interaction with the target.

Audio deepfakes are also increasingly used in non-consensual intimate contexts. Voice clones of real individuals are used to generate explicit audio content without consent, causing harm directly analogous to deepfake video pornography. Detection is arguably harder with audio than with video because the quality artifacts that help identify video deepfakes are more subtle in audio form and because people are generally less trained to evaluate the authenticity of audio recordings than of video.

Legal Responses: A Patchwork

Legal responses to deepfakes have developed unevenly and remain inadequate in most jurisdictions. In the United States, a federal law specifically addressing deepfakes does not exist as of 2024. Several states have enacted laws: California's AB 602 requires disclosure when deepfakes are used in political advertising; Virginia, Georgia and California have enacted laws against non-consensual deepfake pornography; Texas has criminalized deepfakes used to influence elections. The patchwork is inadequate because it leaves most states unprotected, varies in scope and penalty, and does not address cross-jurisdictional distribution.

Federal legislative proposals have been introduced repeatedly without passing. The DEEPFAKES Accountability Act would require synthetic media to carry digital watermarks and disclosures. The Malicious Deep Fake Prohibition Act would criminalize the use of deepfakes to commit crimes. Neither has advanced to a vote. The legislative paralysis reflects both the general difficulty of passing technology regulation and specific lobbying pressure from tech companies that develop AI generation tools and do not want their products subject to broad mandatory disclosure requirements.

The European Union's approach is somewhat more comprehensive. The AI Act requires that AI systems generating synthetic content clearly label it as AI-generated when it depicts real people. The Digital Services Act requires that large platforms develop processes for reporting and removing non-consensual synthetic intimate images. These requirements apply to platforms operating in the EU regardless of where they are headquartered, creating some extraterritorial pressure on American companies.

Civil litigation has produced some results for high-profile targets. A class action lawsuit against several AI companies for using individuals' likeness and voice data without consent is proceeding through courts. The right of publicity — the legal right to control commercial use of one's name, image and likeness — provides a potential avenue for claims that do not fit neatly into copyright or criminal frameworks, though it has traditionally applied to commercial uses rather than to malicious non-commercial harassment.

Platform Policies and Their Limits

Major platforms have adopted policies against non-consensual deepfake pornography and, to varying degrees, against deepfake political content. Meta, TikTok, YouTube and Twitter/X all have policies requiring disclosure of AI-generated content or prohibiting non-consensual synthetic intimate images. Enforcement is inconsistent and often reactive rather than proactive — content is typically removed after it has been flagged and reported rather than before it gains significant distribution.

The technical challenge is real. At scale, proactive detection of deepfakes would require running every uploaded video through detection systems with low false-positive rates, and detection accuracy is insufficient for that kind of automated enforcement. Detection systems trained on current deepfakes are frequently defeated by slightly modified generation techniques, creating an ongoing cat-and-mouse dynamic that platform detection is generally losing.

Content authenticity initiatives offer a potential longer-term technical approach. The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA), backed by Adobe, Microsoft, Google and others, is developing a technical standard for attaching cryptographic provenance metadata to media files — essentially a chain of custody record that documents where a piece of content came from and what modifications it has undergone. If widely adopted, this standard would make it much easier to verify whether media content has been modified from its original form.

The limitation of provenance-based approaches is that they require that content be captured with provenance-aware devices or applications from the outset. A deepfake creator simply does not use provenance-aware tools. The system works well for establishing that content is authentic when it comes with provenance metadata, but the absence of provenance metadata does not prove that content is inauthentic, only that its provenance is unknown. It reduces uncertainty rather than eliminates it.

The Verification Problem and Epistemic Consequences

One of the deeper consequences of widespread synthetic media is what researchers call "epistemic damage" — harm to the shared epistemic infrastructure that makes public reasoning and democratic deliberation possible. This goes beyond any specific fake content fooling any specific audience. It concerns the general corrosion of trust in visual evidence that occurs when everyone knows that convincing fakes are possible.

Historically, photographs and videos carried a presumption of authenticity because making convincing fakes was technically difficult. Courts admitted them as evidence with relatively modest authentication requirements. Journalism relied on them as primary documentation. The assumption that "seeing is believing" was not unreasonable when manipulation required significant expertise and usually left detectable artifacts.

That presumption is no longer epistemically justified. A photograph or video of someone doing or saying something can no longer be assumed authentic without provenance verification. Courts are already grappling with how to handle digital evidence authentication. Journalists have had to develop new verification protocols. The cognitive and institutional burden of media authentication has increased substantially, and these costs are distributed unevenly — people with more media literacy bear them better than those without.

The political consequences run in multiple directions simultaneously. On one hand, deepfake disinformation can spread false content that damages reputations or spreads false beliefs. On the other hand, the possibility of claiming that authentic content is faked provides new tools for denying accountability. Both effects damage epistemic trust, but they damage it in ways that favor different actors. Sophisticated actors with access to communication channels to spread denial claims benefit from the liar's dividend. Less sophisticated actors who lack those channels are more vulnerable to deepfake smear campaigns they cannot rebut effectively.

What Effective Responses Would Require

Addressing the harms from synthetic media effectively would require action on multiple fronts simultaneously, none of which is politically easy.

On the legal front, a comprehensive federal law against non-consensual synthetic intimate imagery — with criminal penalties for creation and distribution, civil remedies for victims and platform liability for failure to implement reasonable detection and removal systems — would provide a meaningful baseline that current state-level patchwork laws do not. Proposals to extend the right of publicity to cover synthetic representations of identifiable individuals, regardless of commercial intent, would address gaps in existing intellectual property law.

On the technical front, investment in provenance infrastructure and detection research is valuable even given its limitations. The C2PA standard deserves broader adoption by camera manufacturers, social media platforms and editing software developers. Detection research should be funded as a public good rather than left entirely to the platforms that have structural incentives to minimize the apparent scope of deepfake harm.

On the platform front, treating non-consensual synthetic intimate imagery with the same urgency and resources as child sexual abuse material — with proactive detection, immediate removal and law enforcement cooperation — would align platform responses with the actual severity of harm. The current disparity in platform urgency between these two categories of non-consensual intimate content is difficult to justify on any principled basis.

None of these responses fully solves the problem. Synthetic media technology will continue to improve. Bad actors will find ways around legal and technical constraints. The epistemic damage from widespread awareness that convincing fakes are possible is not reversible by removing any specific piece of fake content. What effective responses can do is raise costs for abusers, reduce impunity, provide remedies for victims and limit the most severe individual harms. That is a meaningful goal even if it falls well short of eliminating the underlying risks.

The conversation about deepfakes needs to stay grounded in the actual distribution of harms rather than being captured by the most dramatic hypothetical scenarios. The women whose lives have been damaged by non-consensual synthetic pornography are not hypothetical. The political deepfakes that influence elections are still largely hypothetical, at least at scale. Getting the priorities right matters for allocating resources, designing legal frameworks and building public awareness of what synthetic media technology actually does in the real world.
