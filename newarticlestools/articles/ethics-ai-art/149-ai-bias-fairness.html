AI Bias and the Fairness Problem

In 2016, ProPublica published an investigation into a risk assessment tool called COMPAS (Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions) that was being used by courts across the United States to inform decisions about bail and sentencing. The tool assessed the probability that a defendant would reoffend, and courts were using its outputs to help decide who went to prison and who went home. ProPublica's analysis found that COMPAS was almost twice as likely to falsely flag Black defendants as future criminals, and almost twice as likely to falsely flag white defendants as low risk. The manufacturer of the tool disputed the analysis and the methodological debate that followed was genuinely complex. But the core finding -- that an algorithmic system supposedly delivering objective, data-driven assessments was producing outputs with significant racial disparities -- captured something important about the promise and the reality of algorithmic decision-making.

The promise is seductive: replace the inconsistent, prejudiced, emotionally variable judgments of human decision-makers with objective data-driven assessments that treat everyone by the same rules. The reality is that algorithms learn from historical data, and historical data encodes historical patterns of discrimination, exclusion and differential treatment. A hiring algorithm trained on historical hiring data will learn that the historically preferred candidate looked a certain way. A lending algorithm trained on historical loan data will learn that people in certain zip codes have historically defaulted at higher rates -- zip codes that are not racially neutral because housing segregation was not racially neutral. The algorithm does not introduce the bias. It launders the bias already present in the data, presenting it as objective mathematical output that is harder to contest than a human judgment.

The Three Sources of Algorithmic Bias

The sources of bias in AI systems are multiple and operate at different stages of the development and deployment process. Understanding them separately is necessary for understanding why algorithmic fairness is harder than simply "use better data."

The first source is training data bias. Machine learning systems learn patterns from data, and if the data represents a biased world, the system learns biased patterns. This is unavoidable if the data is historical, because history records discriminatory practices as facts rather than distortions. Historical arrest data reflects policing patterns -- who was policed, which neighborhoods were targeted, which behaviors were prosecuted -- that were not racially neutral. Training a predictive policing algorithm on historical arrest data means training it on the outputs of discriminatory policing, which will produce a system that recommends policing the same neighborhoods for the same reasons, producing more arrests in those neighborhoods, confirming the pattern. The feedback loop converts historical discrimination into algorithmic recommendation and then into future reality.

The second source is feature selection bias. The variables that go into an algorithm -- what data is collected, what is measured, what is used as a proxy for what -- reflect the values and assumptions of the people who designed the system. Word embeddings trained on large text corpora reliably associate "woman" with "homemaker" and "man" with "professional," not because the researchers intended this but because the text corpus reflects a history of gendered associations. Facial recognition systems trained predominantly on white faces perform significantly worse on dark-skinned faces, not because skin color was intentionally included as a feature but because the demographic composition of the training data made lighter skin the implicit baseline. Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru's "Gender Shades" study found error rates for gender classification that were up to thirty-four percentage points higher for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men in commercial facial recognition systems from IBM, Microsoft and Face++.

"The thing that genuinely surprised me about the Buolamwini research was not that bias existed in facial recognition -- I expected that. It was the magnitude. Ninety-nine percent accuracy on light-skinned men and sixty-five percent accuracy on dark-skinned women are not rounding errors in the same system. That's a fundamentally different product for different groups of people, and the groups who get the worse product are already the most vulnerable to its harms." r/MachineLearning · 16,293 upvotes

The third source is what Solon Barocas and Moritz Hardt call "feedback loop bias" or "disparate impact amplification": the way algorithmic deployment in the real world creates feedback that reinforces the patterns the algorithm detected. Predictive policing systems send more officers to certain neighborhoods, which produces more arrests in those neighborhoods, which generates more training data showing those neighborhoods as high-risk, which sends more officers there. Credit scoring systems that deny loans to people in certain zip codes prevent those people from building credit history, which keeps their credit scores low, which prevents them from accessing loans. The algorithm's output becomes an input to the future data that will be used to retrain the algorithm, creating self-fulfilling prophecies that are mathematically robust and almost invisible to challenge from within the system.

The Mathematical Impossibility of Fairness

One of the most important and most uncomfortable findings in the formal study of algorithmic fairness is that several mathematically reasonable definitions of fairness are mutually incompatible. In 2016, researchers demonstrated the "impossibility theorem" of algorithmic fairness: that calibration (the requirement that the algorithm's predicted probability of an outcome is accurate for both groups), equal false positive rates (requiring the same rate of false risk labels across groups) and equal false negative rates cannot all be satisfied simultaneously when the base rates of the outcome differ between groups.

In the COMPAS case, the base rates of recidivism differed between Black and white defendants in the dataset. If you calibrate the algorithm (make its predictions accurate), and if the base rates differ, you cannot simultaneously achieve equal false positive and false negative rates across groups. Any choice satisfies some fairness criteria at the expense of others. Northpointe (the COMPAS manufacturer) argued the algorithm was calibrated -- that its risk scores meant the same thing across racial groups. ProPublica argued that equal false positive rates were the appropriate fairness criterion. They were both right about their own criterion. The controversy was never resolvable within the mathematical framework, because the available fairness criteria were genuinely in conflict.

This impossibility result has been confirmed and extended by subsequent work in the algorithmic fairness literature. It means that any algorithmic decision system deployed in a domain with differential base rates across groups will inevitably satisfy some fairness criteria while violating others. The choice of which fairness criteria to prioritize is not a technical decision. It is a value decision -- a question about which kinds of errors are more costly, whose interests to prioritize, what equality means in a particular context. Presenting algorithmic outputs as objective and technically neutral obscures the value choices embedded in the system design.

High-Stakes Domains and Their Specific Harms

Algorithmic decision systems are deployed across an enormous range of high-stakes domains where their biases have life-altering consequences. Understanding the specific mechanisms and documented harms in several of these domains makes the abstract argument concrete.

In criminal justice, the COMPAS case was not isolated. Risk assessment tools are used in jurisdictions across the United States and internationally for bail, sentencing and parole decisions. The research consistently finds that these tools produce racially disparate outputs and that their claimed predictive accuracy advantage over human judgment is often small or nonexistent. A 2018 study by Julia Dressel and Hany Farid at Dartmouth found that COMPAS predicted reoffending about as accurately as a survey of people with no criminal justice training -- that is, not particularly well. The "objectivity" of algorithmic assessment in this domain appears to be largely a legitimacy claim rather than a demonstrated accuracy claim.

In hiring, algorithm-assisted screening tools have been found to encode gender biases (Amazon scrapped an AI recruiting tool in 2018 after discovering it downgraded resumes from women's colleges), racial biases (callback rates for racially stereotyped names are lower in automated screening as well as human screening), and age biases. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has noted that facially neutral algorithmic screening criteria can produce discriminatory outcomes in violation of employment law even without discriminatory intent, but enforcement in this area has lagged significantly behind deployment.

In healthcare, algorithmic tools that allocate resources, predict disease risk or recommend treatments have been found to encode biases in several documented cases. Obermeyer and colleagues published a landmark 2019 Science paper demonstrating that a widely used healthcare algorithm systematically underestimated the severity of illness in Black patients compared to white patients with the same measured health needs, because it used healthcare cost as a proxy for health need -- and Black patients historically incurred lower healthcare costs because they faced greater access barriers, which the algorithm encoded as lower need rather than as differential access.

"The Obermeyer finding should be required reading for anyone who thinks 'more data and better algorithms' automatically means 'more fair.' The algorithm wasn't using race as a variable. It was using healthcare costs, which in a racially unequal healthcare system encodes race indirectly. Fixing algorithmic bias means fixing what the algorithm is measuring, not just who built it." r/healthcare · 13,847 upvotes

The Accountability Gap

One of the most significant problems with algorithmic decision systems is the difficulty of establishing accountability for their outcomes. When a human decision-maker makes a discriminatory judgment, there is at least in principle a route to accountability: the decision-maker can be identified, their reasoning can be examined, and if their judgment was discriminatory they can be held responsible. When an algorithm produces a discriminatory output, accountability is diffuse. The algorithm's developers may be a different organization from the one deploying it. The training data may have been compiled by yet another party. The specific decision about which features to include and how to weight them may have been made by engineers who didn't see themselves as making value judgments at all. And the mathematical complexity of the system may make it genuinely difficult to explain why it produced a specific output in a specific case.

The European Union's GDPR established a limited "right to explanation" for algorithmic decisions, but the implementation has been contested and the actual explanations provided under it are often so generic as to be useless. The EU AI Act, moving toward implementation, attempts to create more substantive accountability requirements for "high-risk" AI systems including those used in criminal justice, employment, credit and healthcare. The regulatory framework is more advanced in the EU than in the United States, where sector-specific agencies are attempting to apply existing law to algorithmic contexts that the law was not written to address.

What Technical Solutions Can and Cannot Do

A substantial research field of "fairness-aware machine learning" has developed to address algorithmic bias technically, producing tools and methods for detecting, measuring and mitigating bias in algorithmic systems. These tools are genuine contributions. They can identify disparities that would otherwise be invisible, provide metrics that can be monitored over time, and in some cases reduce specific measured disparities through technical interventions. But the limitations of technical solutions to fundamentally social and political problems are real and important.

Technical fairness interventions cannot resolve the impossibility theorem: they can optimize for specific fairness criteria but they cannot satisfy all reasonable fairness criteria simultaneously in settings with differential base rates. Technical interventions that remove racially correlated variables from the model do not necessarily remove racial disparities in outputs if other variables remain that are correlated with race through historical patterns. And technical interventions cannot address the prior question of whether algorithmic decision systems should be used in a given domain at all -- whether the accuracy gains, if any, are worth the accountability costs, whether the use of such systems forecloses appeals to human judgment that defendants or applicants would otherwise have access to, and whether the domains in which these systems are deployed are appropriate venues for automated decision-making given their life-altering stakes.

The deeper question is about the relationship between technical systems and social justice. Algorithmic systems are built within existing social arrangements and learn from their data. Deploying more powerful algorithmic systems in contexts defined by existing inequalities does not neutralize those inequalities. In many documented cases it amplifies them while providing a patina of mathematical objectivity that makes them harder to challenge. The path to fairer outcomes runs through addressing the underlying inequalities in the social arrangements from which the data is drawn, not only through optimizing the mathematical properties of the systems that learn from them.
