How Recommendation Algorithms Shape What You Think Is Real

13 min read  ·  Algorithms  ·  Media Theory
76,384 upvotes
Advertisement

In 2019, a researcher at Google named Guillaume Chaslot published findings from a project he had worked on during his time at YouTube before being fired. Chaslot had analyzed YouTube's recommendation algorithm — the system that selects which video plays next after you finish watching one — and concluded that it systematically amplified conspiratorial, sensational and politically extreme content. The more outrageous a video, the more watch time it tended to generate, and the algorithm optimized for watch time, which meant that the recommendation pathway from ordinary content toward extreme content was short and well-traveled.

YouTube disputed the findings. The company's internal research, it argued, showed that the algorithm was not systematically radicalizing users. Independent researchers produced contradictory findings. Some found evidence consistent with Chaslot's account; others found little evidence of a broad radicalization pipeline. The debate remained unresolved in part because YouTube and other platforms declined to give researchers access to the internal data that would be required to settle it definitively.

What was not in dispute — and what the debate often obscured — is a more fundamental question about what recommendation algorithms do regardless of whether they specifically amplify political extremism. These systems make decisions about what information you encounter, in what order, with what implicit weighting toward particular framings and framers, at a scale that touches essentially every person who uses the internet. That is a power of extraordinary scope. The question is not just whether it is being exercised well but whether we have adequate frameworks for thinking about what "well" means when an algorithm is effectively curating the informational universe for two billion people.

The Mechanics: What Algorithms Actually Optimize For

Recommendation algorithms optimize for measurable proxies of engagement because engagement is what generates advertising revenue. The specific metrics vary by platform: YouTube optimizes heavily for watch time and session length; Facebook and Instagram optimize for likes, shares and comments; TikTok optimizes for completion rate (whether you watch a video to the end) and loop plays; Spotify optimizes for streams and playlist additions; Twitter/X optimizes for time on platform and interaction rate.

Each of these metrics is a proxy for something harder to measure directly — whether content is genuinely valuable to the user, whether it is accurate, whether it makes the user more or less well-informed or more or less emotionally stable. The problem with proxies is that optimizing for them creates incentives to produce content that scores well on the measurable metric regardless of whether it scores well on the underlying thing the metric is supposed to represent.

This dynamic is an instance of Goodhart's Law, formulated by the economist Charles Goodhart in 1975: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Content that generates angry reactions generates high engagement metrics even if — or precisely because — it makes people feel worse. Content that confirms existing beliefs generates more shares than content that challenges them even when the confirming content is false. Content that triggers moral outrage generates more comments than content that is nuanced and accurate. In each case, the metric reward and the content quality are inversely related.

I spent six months studying YouTube recommendation pathways as part of my dissertation research. The radicalization claim is probably overstated for most users. But the more subtle thing I found was a systematic bias toward content that generates strong negative emotions — anxiety, outrage, disgust. Not because the algorithm "wants" you to feel bad, but because those emotions reliably produce the engagement metrics the algorithm is optimizing for. It's not malicious. It's an optimization function with the wrong objective. — r/AcademicPhilosophy, 48,293 upvotes

Filter Bubbles: Myth, Reality and the Nuanced Truth

The "filter bubble" concept, introduced by Eli Pariser in his 2011 book of the same name, holds that personalization algorithms create self-reinforcing information environments where users are systematically exposed to content that confirms their existing views and shielded from challenging perspectives. The concept became enormously influential, generating years of academic research and regulatory attention.

The research findings have been more complicated than the concept's initial reception suggested. Studies of Facebook's news feed algorithm found that it does reduce ideological diversity in the content users see compared to a reverse-chronological feed, but by a smaller amount than the concept implied and by less than users' own choices would produce without any algorithmic intervention at all. People's social networks are ideologically segregated, and even an ideologically neutral algorithm would produce bubble-like effects simply by serving content that the ideologically segregated social graph generates.

Studies of search filter bubbles have found modest personalization effects that do not amount to the dramatic echo chambers the concept implies. Research on TikTok's "For You Page" — which is particularly aggressive in serving personalized content — found that new users are quickly channeled into persistent content neighborhoods that are difficult to exit but that these neighborhoods reflect user behavior signals rather than ideological sorting per se.

So filter bubbles are real but smaller and more complex than the concept as popularly understood. The more precise concern is not that algorithms create hermetically sealed ideological chambers but that they systematically underserve content that generates moderate engagement while overserving content at the emotional extremes of the engagement distribution. A nuanced, accurate article about a complex political question generates less engagement than a misleading but emotionally compelling version of the same topic. A video that calms down anxiety about a health question generates less watch time than a video that amplifies the anxiety. The effect is not ideological censorship but emotional amplification, and the consequences are significant even without the full filter bubble effect.

TikTok's For You Page and the Interest Graph Revolution

TikTok represents a significant evolution in recommendation algorithm design that deserves separate analysis because its approach is structurally different from the social graph-based systems that dominate other platforms. Facebook and Twitter primarily surface content from people you have chosen to follow — a social graph recommendation. TikTok's For You Page uses what is called an interest graph, surfacing content based on inferred interests regardless of social connections.

The practical implication is that TikTok's algorithm is far more aggressive in making initial inferences about user preferences and acting on them. A new user who watches three videos about a particular topic will immediately see their feed populated with more content on that topic, even if they follow no relevant accounts. The system learns extraordinarily quickly from minimal behavioral signals and serves content that the user did not request and does not know is being served based on inferences about their preferences.

This approach produces dramatically higher engagement than social graph recommendation, which is why TikTok grew so rapidly and why every other major platform is now attempting to replicate its recommendation approach. It also produces a distinctive phenomenological experience: users frequently describe the For You Page as eerily accurate, as if the algorithm "knows them" at an almost uncomfortable level. This accuracy comes from the combination of sensitive inference from behavioral signals and the platform's willingness to act aggressively on those inferences without the user's explicit direction.

Research has documented several concerning patterns in TikTok's recommendation behavior. Studies using fresh accounts found that expressing minimal interest in weight loss content quickly produces a feed heavily populated with content promoting extreme dieting and exercise behaviors. Similar pathways exist from mildly anti-social content toward more extreme versions. The platform's moderation team has documented internal guidelines attempting to limit these amplification patterns, but enforcement has been inconsistent and the underlying optimization objective — maximizing engagement time — creates structural pressure toward content that triggers compulsive viewing.

The Rabbit Hole Problem

One specific pattern in recommendation systems that has drawn sustained research attention is what is colloquially called the "rabbit hole" — a recommendation sequence that starts from ordinary content and progressively surfaces more extreme or niche content on the same topic. The pattern exists because of how recommendation systems build user preference models.

If you watch a video about, say, historical military conflicts, and you watch it to completion, the algorithm infers interest in the topic and recommends more content. The next video might be slightly more dramatic or emotionally intense because such content generates higher completion rates. If you watch that one too, the algorithm refines its model further in the direction of intense content on the topic. Over several iterations, a user who started with a moderate interest in history might be recommended conspiracy documentaries, extremist political analysis or other content far removed from where they started.

The mechanism does not require that the algorithm be "trying" to radicalize anyone. It is a straightforward consequence of optimizing for watch time using a preference model that updates on each interaction. Content that generates longer watch times — which tends to be more emotionally engaging, more sensational or more addictive — gets recommended more. The user's preference profile drifts toward whatever generates the longest watch times, which is not necessarily what the user would choose if they were selecting content deliberately rather than following algorithm recommendations.

Research by Max Fisher, documented in his book "The Chaos Machine," found this pattern replicated across multiple platforms and multiple topic domains. The rabbit hole is not unique to political content; it appears in fitness content (ordinary exercise videos to disordered eating content), finance content (investing basics to get-rich-quick schemes to financial conspiracy theories) and relationship content (dating advice to pickup artist communities to incel forums). The common thread is that emotional intensity and niche identity are correlated with watch time, and the algorithm rewards both.

Cultural Homogenization and Its Opposite

A different concern about recommendation algorithms operates at the level of culture rather than individual experience. Pre-algorithm media culture was characterized by a combination of mass shared experience (everyone watching the same TV shows, reading the same newspapers, engaging with a shared cultural mainstream) and a much smaller niche market for content that fell outside the mainstream. The internet's early promise was to empower niche interests by enabling audiences too small to support physical distribution to find each other globally.

Recommendation algorithms have produced a more complex outcome. On the one hand, they have enabled extraordinary niche content ecosystems that would never have been viable in a broadcast media environment. The global audience for any specific interest, however narrow, can be assembled and served in ways that pre-internet media could not accommodate. This is genuinely valuable for people whose interests, identities and experiences fell outside the mainstream cultural offering.

On the other hand, recommendation algorithms also create powerful homogenizing pressures when they are optimized at scale. When the same optimization objective — maximize watch time or engagement — governs billions of content recommendations simultaneously, the algorithm effectively selects for content characteristics that are universally engaging across diverse audiences: novelty, emotional intensity, social validation signals, identity reinforcement and mild outrage. These characteristics tend to dominate across cultures, languages and content categories, producing a kind of lowest-common-denominator emotional engagement that is globally consistent even as the specific content varies.

The result is a paradox: simultaneously more niche content and more homogeneous emotional tenor across that content. The specific topics and communities multiply while the emotional register — anxious, excited, outraged, validated — converges toward what the optimization function rewards.

Spotify, Music and the Algorithmic Canon

The effects of recommendation algorithms on music culture provide a case study that is somewhat insulated from the political controversies that dominate discussions of social media algorithms, making the structural dynamics easier to see clearly. Spotify's recommendation systems — particularly its Discover Weekly playlist and the radio features that generate automatic playlists based on a seed track or artist — now significantly shape what music gets heard by whom.

Musicologist Glenn McDonald, who worked at Spotify for years, has documented how the platform's recommendation infrastructure interacts with the economics of music creation. Songs that generate high completion rates — that people listen to all the way through rather than skipping — score better in Spotify's algorithms than songs that are skipped more often. This creates a structural preference for shorter songs with immediate hooks over longer songs that develop more slowly. Average song length on Spotify has dropped significantly over the decade of the platform's dominance.

The algorithm also tends to amplify already-popular content and to cluster listeners in genre spaces that are easy to navigate algorithmically but that may not reflect the actual structure of musical affinity. Critics have argued that the recommendation infrastructure is shaping musical production toward characteristics that score well algorithmically — short, immediately hooky, emotionally simple — rather than characteristics that would be valued if listeners were choosing more deliberately.

Independent artists have had deeply mixed experiences with algorithmic recommendation. On one hand, Spotify's algorithm occasionally surfaces smaller artists to audiences that would never have found them through traditional gatekeepers. On the other hand, the economic model that funds the algorithm — streaming revenue — distributes that revenue so heavily toward already-popular artists that the long tail of independent music earns tiny fractions of a cent per stream. The algorithm provides discovery exposure while the economic model concentrates revenue at the top.

The Accountability Gap

One of the most important structural features of recommendation algorithms is that they operate without meaningful external accountability. They make consequential decisions about what information billions of people encounter, but those decisions are made by private companies with no obligation to disclose their logic, no legal framework requiring that they optimize for public benefit rather than engagement and no effective mechanism for outside review of their effects.

This accountability gap is not unique to recommendation algorithms — it characterizes much of the tech industry's operation — but it is particularly pronounced here because the effects of recommendation systems are diffuse and gradual rather than acute and visible. When a bank's algorithm discriminates against loan applicants, there are identifiable victims with cognizable legal claims. When a recommendation algorithm spends years steering millions of users toward progressively more extreme content, the harm is real but distributed across millions of users over time in ways that are extremely difficult to attribute causally to the algorithm.

Proposals for algorithmic accountability have taken several forms. Mandatory algorithmic audits — requiring platforms to submit their recommendation systems to independent review — would provide external visibility into systems that currently operate entirely in the dark. Algorithmic transparency requirements — requiring platforms to explain the logic of their recommendations to users and to regulators — would make the systems more legible without necessarily changing them. User control requirements — giving users the ability to meaningfully modify recommendation algorithm parameters rather than accepting the default engagement-optimized settings — would shift some power back to individuals.

None of these proposals has been adopted at scale, though the European Union's Digital Services Act includes some transparency and audit requirements for large platforms' recommendation systems. The practical challenge is that recommendation algorithms are extraordinarily complex systems where the causal chain from design choice to user experience is not well understood even by the engineers who build them. Auditing them is technically difficult, and the results of audits are likely to be contested.

People frame this as a free speech issue — who decides what content gets amplified — but that framing assumes the alternative is no curation. There is no neutral feed. Someone is always making choices about what content gets surfaced. The real question is whether those choices should be made in the dark by private companies optimizing for engagement revenue, or whether there should be transparency, accountability and some requirement that public interest be part of the objective function. — r/technology, 52,847 upvotes

Designing for Something Better

The most constructive framing of the recommendation algorithm problem may be not "how do we regulate what platforms are doing" but "what would recommendation systems look like if they were designed to benefit users rather than to maximize engagement revenue?"

Researchers have proposed several alternative optimization objectives. "Time well spent" frameworks, associated with Tristan Harris and the Center for Humane Technology, would orient systems toward users' reflective preferences — what they would want to have watched if they thought about it — rather than their momentary behavioral signals. Epistemic quality metrics, measuring whether content is accurate and high-quality, could be incorporated alongside engagement metrics. Diversity metrics, measuring whether recommendations expose users to a range of perspectives rather than reinforcing a single perspective, could counteract the filter bubble dynamics that pure engagement optimization produces.

Some platforms have made limited moves in these directions. YouTube has added friction to recommendations of certain categories of borderline content, reducing but not eliminating the rabbit hole effect for those specific categories. Facebook has experimented with showing users more content from close friends and family and less from news publishers, though the stated rationale was reducing misinformation rather than improving wellbeing. Twitter/X briefly experimented with labeling content as having high or low credibility before abandoning the project.

These interventions are modest and partial, but they demonstrate that the engagement optimization objective is not technologically inevitable. Platforms can incorporate other objectives into their recommendation systems; they simply have not had sufficient competitive pressure or regulatory compulsion to do so systematically. The recommendation algorithm problem is ultimately a governance problem dressed up as a technology problem. The technology can be designed differently; the question is whether the political and economic conditions exist to require it.
