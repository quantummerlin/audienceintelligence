AI Creativity and Authorship: Who Owns What the Machine Makes

13 min read  ·  Creativity  ·  Intellectual Property
74,918 upvotes
Advertisement

In September 2022, a Colorado art competition made international headlines when the winning entry in the digital arts category turned out to have been created using Midjourney, an AI image generation system. The winner, Jason Allen, had spent weeks crafting detailed prompts and selecting from among hundreds of generated variations, ultimately submitting a piece called "Théâtre D'Opéra Spatial" — a lush, cinematic image of a futuristic opera house rendered in the baroque style. The backlash was immediate. Other artists accused Allen of cheating. Some demanded that competition rules be amended to exclude AI-generated work. The debate spilled across social media and into mainstream news coverage, raising questions that have only become more urgent since.

Who created "Théâtre D'Opéra Spatial"? Allen made choices about aesthetics, composition, subject matter and mood. He iteratively refined prompts, evaluated outputs and selected the final image. Midjourney's system generated the pixels, drawing on patterns learned from billions of human-created images. The human painters, photographers and illustrators whose work trained the model contributed visual knowledge without their knowledge or compensation. The answer to the authorship question depends entirely on what you think authorship is — a question that copyright law, philosophy of creativity and the art world have been circling for centuries without reaching consensus, and that AI has now made suddenly and practically urgent.

What Copyright Law Actually Says

American copyright law has a fairly clear position, though the implications are uncomfortable for everyone involved. Copyright protection in the United States requires human authorship. This requirement goes back to a series of nineteenth-century Supreme Court cases and has been affirmed and elaborated in subsequent decades. The Copyright Office issued guidance in 2023 stating explicitly that works produced entirely by AI systems without human creative control are not copyrightable. If a generative AI system produces an image in response to a prompt, and the prompt is the only human input, the resulting work enters the public domain immediately.

The harder question is what counts as sufficient human creative control to establish copyright. The Copyright Office's 2023 guidance on AI-assisted works draws a distinction between cases where AI is used as a tool under human creative direction — analogous to using Photoshop or a camera — and cases where the AI system is the primary creative agent and the human's role is limited to providing a prompt. In the former case, copyright protection is available for the human-authored elements. In the latter case, it is not.

This distinction is conceptually defensible but practically difficult to apply. When a photographer selects a camera, adjusts settings, composes a frame and presses a shutter, the camera performs a complex technical operation that the photographer does not control at the level of individual photons. The photographer's creative choices are expressed through and realized by the camera's mechanism. When a writer uses Midjourney, they make choices about subject, style, mood and composition, and the AI performs a complex technical operation that the writer does not control at the level of individual pixels. The structural similarity is obvious, and the line between "tool" and "co-creator" is genuinely difficult to locate.

I'm a professional illustrator and I've been thinking hard about this. The "AI is just a tool" argument sounds compelling until you realize the tool is making essentially all the visual decisions. When I use Photoshop, I'm controlling every brush stroke. When I use Midjourney, I'm controlling the concept and the AI is controlling the execution — and in visual art, execution is where most of the creative work lives. That's not like using a camera. It's more like commissioning an artist. — r/ArtificialIntelligence, 43,829 upvotes

The Training Data Problem

The copyright question around AI-generated outputs is complicated, but the copyright question around AI training data may be even more consequential. Every major generative AI system was trained on enormous datasets of human-created content. Stability AI's training data included approximately 5.8 billion image-text pairs scraped from the internet. OpenAI's language models were trained on hundreds of billions of words of text from books, websites, academic papers and other human-authored content. Midjourney trained on a similarly vast corpus of visual art.

The legal question is whether training a machine learning model on copyrighted content constitutes infringement. The tech companies argue that it does not, under a "fair use" theory. Fair use allows copyrighted material to be used without permission for purposes including criticism, commentary, scholarship, research and certain transformative uses. The companies argue that training a model is transformative because the model does not store or reproduce the training data; it extracts statistical patterns that are then used to generate new content. The training process is, on this view, more like reading a library than photocopying it.

The artists and authors who have brought lawsuits against Stability AI, Midjourney, OpenAI and other companies argue the opposite. Their central claim is that the models do memorize training data in a functional sense — they can reproduce near-identical copies of training images when prompted in certain ways, and they can reproduce passages from books that appeared in training data. More importantly, they argue that whether or not the technical process involves direct copying, the economic effect is equivalent to copying: the AI system produces outputs that directly compete with the creators whose work it was trained on, without compensating those creators.

As of 2024, these cases are working their way through courts with no definitive resolution. The outcomes will significantly shape the economics of the creative industries for decades. If training on copyrighted data without permission is found to be fair use, the current business model of AI companies is validated and artists have essentially no recourse. If it is found to be infringement, the entire generative AI industry faces either massive retroactive liability or the need to secure licenses for training data — which would be both expensive and logistically complex.

What Is Creativity, Actually?

Beneath the legal arguments sits a deeper philosophical question that the law's resolution will not settle: does AI actually create? The answer depends on what you think creativity is, and reasonable people disagree sharply.

One view, associated with cognitive scientists like Margaret Boden, distinguishes between exploratory creativity, combinatorial creativity and transformational creativity. Exploratory creativity involves systematically exploring the possibility space within an existing conceptual framework. Combinatorial creativity involves recombining familiar elements in novel ways. Transformational creativity involves genuinely breaking from existing frameworks and establishing new conceptual territory. Boden argues that current AI systems can achieve the first two kinds of creativity but not the third. They can produce novel combinations within learned patterns — which is impressive and commercially valuable — but they cannot fundamentally reframe the conceptual space itself, which is what the greatest human creative works do.

Others push back on this hierarchy. They note that most human creative work is also exploratory or combinatorial rather than truly transformational, and that the distinction between "working within a framework" and "transforming the framework" is less clear than it appears. Every human artist learns from prior artists, internalizes conventions, develops a vocabulary of moves and recombines them in novel ways. The process of learning from millions of examples to produce novel combinations is not obviously less creative just because it happens in silicon rather than carbon.

The philosopher Nick Zangwill has argued that creativity requires intentionality — the work must be produced by an agent who intends to make something and who has aesthetic responses to their own productions. A language model generates text by predicting probable next tokens; it does not intend to write a poem in any psychologically robust sense, and it has no aesthetic response to the poem it produces. On this view, AI systems are not creative agents but very sophisticated text-continuation machines, and calling their outputs "creative" confuses the product with the process.

The intentionality argument is interesting but I'm not sure it holds up. I've talked to plenty of professional writers who describe their best work as emerging from something like automatic writing — following the logic of the story without exactly "intending" each sentence. Intentionality in creative work is murky for humans too. The cleaner distinction to me is whether the system has genuine preferences and something at stake. A poet cares whether the poem is good. A language model has no stake in the output. — r/philosophy, 67,293 upvotes

The Artist's Perspective

For working artists, the philosophical debates about creativity are somewhat abstract compared to the immediate economic question: what does AI do to the market for human-made art? The answer varies considerably by medium and sector, but in several areas the effects are already dramatic.

Stock illustration and commercial illustration have been hit hardest. Platforms like Shutterstock and Adobe Stock have seen sharp declines in submissions from human illustrators as AI-generated images now dominate certain categories of commercial visual content. The work that previously supported thousands of illustrators' livelihoods — creating generic business images, conceptual illustrations for articles, stock imagery for websites — can now be done in seconds with a text prompt and costs a small fraction of what a commissioned illustration costs.

The music industry is navigating similar disruption. AI systems can now generate commercially adequate background music in any genre in seconds. Platforms that previously paid human composers for production music have rapidly shifted toward AI-generated alternatives. The effect on composers who relied on licensing income from this category of work has been severe. In the writing industry, content farms that previously employed low-wage human writers to produce SEO-optimized articles have shifted to AI generation, and the market for routine online content writing has collapsed.

Whether this constitutes a genuine threat to creative culture or merely the latest in a long series of technological disruptions that ultimately expanded creative opportunity depends on what you think the creative industries are for and who they serve. Previous technological disruptions — photography, recorded music, desktop publishing — did ultimately generate new creative opportunities even as they eliminated specific jobs. But those disruptions typically took decades to play out, giving affected industries time to adapt. The pace of current AI disruption is significantly faster, and the range of creative tasks that AI can perform adequately is expanding rapidly.

The Collaboration Question

One framing that some artists and researchers find more productive than the "AI versus human" opposition is the question of collaboration. If AI is understood not as an autonomous creator or a passive tool but as a collaborator with distinctive capabilities and limitations, different questions come into focus. How do human-AI creative collaborations work? What does each party contribute? How does the collaboration change the human partner's creative process?

Research on this question is still early but some interesting patterns are emerging. Studies of professional writers using AI writing assistants have found that the tools substantially change how writers think about their own work — sometimes productively, sometimes not. Writers report that seeing plausible AI-generated alternatives to their own sentences can help them recognize what they want to say by contrast. They also report that the availability of always-ready text generation can undermine the productive difficulty that some writers depend on for their best work, replacing wrestling with language with browsing through AI suggestions.

Visual artists working with generative AI describe something similar. The tools are most useful for artists who have developed strong aesthetic sensibilities and can use them as a rapid prototyping environment for ideas that they would then realize through other means. They are less useful for artists still developing their visual vocabulary, because the feedback loop that normally helps artists develop — making something, evaluating it critically, understanding why it succeeded or failed, trying again — is disrupted when the making is largely automated.

The choreographer Wayne McGregor, who has worked extensively with AI tools in developing dance pieces, has described the experience as genuinely collaborative in the sense that the AI-generated suggestions opened conceptual territory he would not have explored on his own. The collaboration changed his work, he argues, in ways that are more than purely instrumental — not just helping him execute pre-formed ideas more efficiently but actually changing what ideas he had. This is a stronger claim about AI creativity than the "tool" framing allows for, even if it falls short of treating the AI system as an autonomous creative agent.

Attribution, Credit and the Ghost in the Machine

Questions about authorship have direct implications for how AI-assisted work should be disclosed, attributed and credited. Current practices are wildly inconsistent. Some artists openly disclose their use of AI tools; others do not. Publishers, galleries and other institutions have varying and often unclear policies. Academic journals have adopted disclosure requirements; most other publishing contexts have not.

The disclosure question is not merely academic. If a user commissions a graphic designer and receives an AI-generated image with no disclosure, they have paid for human creative labor but received something different. If a reader buys a book described as a novel but it was generated by an AI with minimal human editing, the purchase is arguably under false pretenses. If a patient receives a cancer screening interpretation that was produced by an AI system but presented as a physician's assessment, the implications for accountability are significant.

One proposed framework distinguishes between AI as medium, AI as co-creator and AI as author. When a writer uses an AI system to generate text that they substantially revise, the AI is functioning as a medium — like a typewriter or a dictation service. When a writer uses AI to generate significant portions of a text and incorporates them with light editing, the AI is functioning as a co-creator and co-authorship attribution is appropriate. When the AI system generates the primary content and the human's role is limited to prompt engineering and selection, the human's credit might be better described as "curator" or "producer" than "author."

The music industry is moving toward requiring AI disclosure on streaming platforms which is genuinely important for audiences. But I keep thinking about the counterfactual: if AI-generated music is indistinguishable from human-generated music, does the distinction actually matter to the listener? I think it does — not because of quality but because of context. A song that emerged from a human's specific grief means something different than the same sequence of notes generated statistically. Whether that difference justifies different market value is a separate question. — r/musicproduction, 38,472 upvotes

The Copyright Cliff and Its Consequences

One concrete and near-term consequence of the current legal situation is that AI-generated works without sufficient human authorship are not copyrightable, which means they immediately enter the public domain. This has some interesting implications that are not always recognized in the debate.

If a company generates AI content for commercial purposes and cannot copyright it, competitors can freely reproduce and use that content without permission or payment. This reduces the incentive to invest in AI-generated content, which might seem like a good outcome for human artists competing with AI-generated alternatives. But it also creates pressure toward the kind of minimal human intervention needed to establish copyright — adding a human element to AI-generated content not because it improves the work but because it secures intellectual property rights.

There is also a curious asymmetry in the current situation. AI companies can use human-created works to train their models, producing systems that compete with the creators whose work was used. But the AI-generated outputs that result from that process cannot be copyrighted by the companies as AI-generated works. The humans whose work trained the models get no compensation and the AI companies get no copyright protection. The main beneficiaries of the current arrangements are end users who get access to powerful AI generation tools, and companies that sell access to those tools as services rather than as copyrightable content.

International Divergence

Different countries have taken substantially different approaches to the question of AI authorship, and the divergence creates both opportunities and complications for the global creative industries. China's National Copyright Administration issued guidance in 2023 that recognized copyright in AI-generated works when there is substantial human creative input — a standard similar to the US approach but perhaps applied differently in practice. The UK previously had a provision in its copyright law allowing computer-generated works to be copyrighted by the person who made the arrangements for the work to be generated, though the Copyright Office recommended removing this provision in 2022.

The European Union's AI Act, which took effect in 2024, includes transparency requirements for AI-generated content — systems that generate synthetic media must disclose that it is AI-generated, and certain high-risk AI applications face more stringent requirements. The Act does not directly address copyright in AI-generated works, leaving that question to be worked out through litigation and possibly future legislation.

The international divergence means that AI companies can structure their operations to take advantage of the most favorable legal environments, while creators face different levels of protection depending on where they are located and where their work is consumed. This kind of regulatory arbitrage is familiar from other technology domains — the tax optimization strategies of multinational tech companies provide a clear precedent — and is equally problematic when applied to intellectual property.

What the Debate Obscures

The public debate about AI creativity has a tendency to focus on edge cases — the competition-winning AI image, the AI-generated novel — rather than on the more pervasive and economically significant effects that operate below the threshold of public attention. The commercial illustrator who can no longer find work because AI image generation has undercut the market for stock art is not a newsworthy story in the way that an award-winning AI painting is. The content writers whose livelihoods have been eliminated by AI text generation are not producing newsworthy controversy in the way that a high-profile copyright lawsuit does.

This focus on the dramatic cases obscures something important: the question of what we want the creative industries to be for. Creative work serves multiple social functions. It provides livelihoods for creators. It produces cultural artifacts that people value aesthetically and emotionally. It transmits cultural memory across generations. It provides a mode of meaning-making and self-expression for both creators and audiences. It models and explores ethical and emotional possibilities in ways that other forms of discourse cannot.

Some of these functions can be served by AI-generated content. Others arguably cannot, or can only be served imperfectly. A piece of music generated algorithmically may be technically indistinguishable from human-composed music and may serve the hedonic function of music perfectly well. But it does not transmit the composer's specific emotional experience, does not represent a human consciousness grappling with the challenges of musical form and does not create the kind of human connection between creator and audience that is part of what many listeners value in music they love.

Whether that difference should translate into different market value, different legal protections or different disclosure requirements is a genuinely contested question. What seems clear is that answering it requires being explicit about what we think creative work is for — not just what legal frameworks say about intellectual property and not just what economic efficiency suggests about resource allocation, but what vision of human flourishing and cultural life we are trying to protect and promote. Those are philosophical and political questions, not technical ones, and they will not be resolved by better algorithms or clearer copyright guidance alone.
