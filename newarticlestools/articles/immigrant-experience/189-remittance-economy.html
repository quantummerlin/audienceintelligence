The Remittance Economy: Money, Obligation and Love Across Borders

12 min read  |  Immigrant Experience  |  Global Economics
74,293 upvotes

Every month, in thousands of small transactions that rarely make financial news, a significant portion of the global economy moves. A construction worker in Dubai sends $300 to his mother in Kerala. A nurse in London transfers £400 to her parents in Lagos. A restaurant worker in New York wires $200 to her children in El Salvador. A software engineer in San Francisco sends $800 to support her siblings' education in the Philippines. Individually these transactions are invisible. In aggregate they constitute one of the largest financial flows on the planet.

The World Bank estimated global remittances to low- and middle-income countries at approximately $647 billion in 2022, with total global remittance flows including flows to high-income countries exceeding $800 billion. To put this in context, official development assistance, the foreign aid that governments provide and that generates considerable diplomatic attention and political debate, totaled approximately $204 billion in the same year. Remittances are not merely comparable to foreign aid; they are several times larger, they flow more directly to families rather than through government or NGO intermediaries and they are far more stable across economic cycles, since individual immigrants continue to support their families even when official development flows contract during global downturns.

These numbers describe a macroeconomic phenomenon of genuine significance. But they do not describe the experience of the people doing the sending, who are navigating not just a financial transaction but a complex web of obligation, love, guilt, expectation and the specific challenge of maintaining economic and emotional responsibility for people who live in a different country, in a different economic reality and often in the context of family dynamics that migration has permanently disrupted.

The Scale of Dependency

Remittances are not marginal income supplements for the families that receive them. In many of the countries that are largest recipients of remittance flows, they constitute a significant share of national GDP and a primary income source for a substantial portion of the population. The World Bank's data for 2022 shows remittances as a percentage of GDP exceeding 30% in Tonga, Lebanon and Samoa, exceeding 20% in several additional countries including Honduras, El Salvador and Nepal and representing double-digit GDP shares in more than twenty countries globally. In El Salvador, remittances accounted for approximately 24% of GDP in 2022, making them the country's largest source of foreign exchange by a considerable margin.

At the household level the dependency can be even more complete. Research by Manuel Orozco at the Inter-American Dialogue and others has found that in many remittance-receiving communities, the households with a family member working abroad are systematically wealthier, better-nourished, better-educated and more likely to have access to healthcare than comparable households without a migrant member. The remittance is not supplementary to these households' economic life; it is its foundation.

This dependency has well-documented macroeconomic effects that are debated in development economics. On the positive side, remittances provide foreign exchange, support consumption that stimulates local economic activity and fund investments in education and health that improve human capital. On the potentially negative side, sustained high remittance flows can generate a form of Dutch disease in which the local currency appreciates, domestic production becomes less competitive internationally and local labor markets are distorted by wage levels influenced by the comparison to foreign earning potential. Research on whether remittances accelerate or retard long-term development in receiving countries has not produced a consensus conclusion; the effects appear to depend heavily on the specific economic context and on how the remittances are used at both the household and national level.

The Sender's Experience

The macroeconomic literature on remittances focuses on receiving countries and receiving households. The experience of the senders receives much less systematic attention, despite the fact that the sustainability and the psychological cost of the remittance relationship is determined primarily by what happens on the sending end.

Research by Peggy Levitt, whose foundational work on transnational migration introduced the concept of "social remittances" alongside financial ones, has documented the extraordinary financial sacrifice that many remittance senders make relative to their own income and living standards. Studies of Mexican, Filipino and Central American immigrant workers in the United States have found that many send 20-30% of their income to family abroad, a proportion that would be considered extraordinary sacrifice in any other context and that leaves senders living well below the income levels they could maintain if they retained their full earnings.

The motivation for this sacrifice is complex and does not reduce cleanly to either purely altruistic family obligation or purely rational economic calculation. Research on migrant remittance behavior by economists including Oded Stark and David Bloom, who developed the "new economics of labor migration" framework in the 1980s, emphasizes that migration and remittances are often collective household strategies rather than individual decisions, with the migrant member understood as the household's investment in a diversified income portfolio. From this perspective, remittances are not gifts or charitable transfers but fulfillments of an implicit economic contract that the entire household entered when it supported or facilitated the migration.

The emotional texture of the remittance relationship is shaped by geographic distance in ways that money cannot fully bridge. The immigrant who sends money home is maintaining a financial presence in the family's life while being physically absent from it. The money can pay for a parent's medical care, a sibling's school fees or repairs to the family home, but it cannot be present at a graduation, a wedding, a deathbed or any of the other moments when physical presence is what family means. Many immigrants describe the remittance as a substitute for presence that is consciously inadequate, a way of discharging obligation toward people they love while being unable to give them what they actually need.

The Expectation Architecture

One of the most psychologically complex dimensions of the remittance relationship is the way that consistent sending establishes expectations that then become obligations. The family that depends on monthly remittances plans around them, makes commitments that assume their continuity and can be genuinely destabilized when they are interrupted. The immigrant who loses a job, has a medical emergency, faces unexpected expenses or simply needs to rebuild their own financial cushion faces a terrible calculation: protecting their own financial stability may require reducing or stopping the remittances that their family has come to depend on, and the human cost of this on the receiving end is real and immediate.

Research by Orozco and others has found that this dynamic creates a specific financial trap for many immigrant workers: the obligation to continue remitting even during personal financial difficulty leads many to take on debt, work additional hours under unfavorable conditions or forgo investments in their own financial security, including retirement savings and emergency funds, in order to maintain the remittance flow. The immigrant who has been sending money home for fifteen years may reach middle age with their family in the home country having benefited substantially from this investment while they themselves have limited personal savings and a compromised ability to handle financial emergencies.

The expectation architecture is also shaped by status dynamics within the family and community back home. In many remittance-sending cultures, the family member who has "made it" abroad carries a status that comes with specific social obligations. The migrant who returns home without gifts, who is seen to be living modestly despite their foreign income or who reduces their financial support is not simply making a personal financial decision; they are violating a social contract that has implications for the family's standing in the community. The pressure to maintain the performance of success abroad, partly through continued remittances, can be intense and is not always visible to observers who see only the financial transaction.

The Financial Infrastructure Problem

The mechanics of sending money internationally have historically been exploitative in ways that have consumed a significant portion of the value that remittances would otherwise deliver. Traditional money transfer services including Western Union and MoneyGram have charged fees in the range of 5-10% of the transferred amount for many corridors, particularly for transfers to countries in Africa, the Caribbean and parts of Latin America. On global remittance flows of $800 billion, even a 5% average fee represents $40 billion annually extracted from some of the world's poorest families through the process of moving money that was already earned.

The United Nations and World Bank have set a target of reducing the global average cost of remittances to 3% of the transferred amount as part of the Sustainable Development Goals. Progress toward this target has been uneven. The global average cost of sending $200 had declined to approximately 6.2% by 2022, according to World Bank data, down from over 9% a decade earlier, but still well above the 3% target. The most expensive corridors, often those serving smaller or less commercially attractive markets, can still involve fees of 10% or more. The emergence of digital money transfer services including Wise, Remitly and others has introduced meaningful competition on some corridors and driven down costs in those markets, but the benefits have been unevenly distributed and have not reached the highest-fee corridors where the need for cost reduction is greatest.

Banking access represents another dimension of the financial infrastructure problem. Many remittance senders and receivers operate outside the formal banking system, either because they lack the documentation required for a bank account, because banking services are inaccessible in their communities or because the distrust of formal financial institutions that characterizes many migrant communities reflects rational historical experience. The move toward digital transfer services has reduced some of these barriers by enabling money to be sent and received through mobile phone accounts that require less documentation than bank accounts, but full financial inclusion for the global remittance ecosystem remains a distant goal.

Social Remittances and Their Less-Discussed Effects

Peggy Levitt's concept of social remittances refers to the ideas, behaviors, identities and social capital that flow back to sending communities alongside financial transfers. The migrant who returns home, even temporarily, carries the cultural software of the host society back with them: different ideas about gender roles, democratic participation, individual rights, religious practice, environmental behavior and countless other domains where exposure to a different cultural context has shifted their frameworks.

These social remittances flow in both directions and have effects that are more ambiguous than financial remittances. In some contexts, the values transmitted by returned migrants have contributed to democratic mobilization, expanded rights for women and reduced tolerance for corruption, effects that development scholars have largely viewed as positive. In other contexts, the cultural changes brought back by migrants have disrupted social cohesion, created generational and gender conflicts and introduced consumption patterns and aspirational frameworks that local economies cannot satisfy, contributing to further migration as people seek the lifestyles they have seen returned migrants embody.

The remittance economy, in both its financial and social dimensions, is ultimately a consequence of the profound global inequality in the distribution of economic opportunity. If people could earn sufficient incomes to support their families without migrating, most would not migrate. The remittance flow is a workaround for the absence of economic development, a way of distributing some of the gains from higher-productivity economies to the people left behind by the global distribution of capital and opportunity. It is a remarkably effective workaround in many respects, and the individual sacrifices on which it rests are extraordinary acts of love and commitment. But it is not a substitute for the more fundamental changes in global economic structure that would make it unnecessary.
