How Modern Propaganda Works

The word "propaganda" conjures a specific historical image: Soviet-era posters of heroic workers, Nazi films depicting racial enemies, wartime pamphlets urging enlistment. These are genuine examples of propaganda, but they represent a model that has been substantially superseded. Contemporary influence operations share the goal of manipulating public belief and behavior, but they operate through mechanisms so different from their twentieth-century predecessors that applying the same term sometimes obscures more than it reveals. Understanding how modern propaganda works requires understanding both the continuities with older methods and the distinctive features that make digital-era influence operations so effective and so difficult to counter.

The classical model of propaganda was fundamentally broadcast: a centralized source produced a message and disseminated it to a mass audience through state or quasi-state media channels. The audience's role was passive reception. Sophisticated propaganda analysts like Jacques Ellul recognized that the most effective propaganda was not primarily about persuading people to believe false things but about channeling authentic emotions and genuine grievances in desired directions. But even Ellul's more sophisticated model assumed a relatively clear distinction between the propagandist and the propagandized.

Contemporary influence operations have dissolved that distinction in ways that make them substantially harder to detect and resist.

The Russian Internet Research Agency Model

The Senate Intelligence Committee's detailed investigation of Russian interference in the 2016 US election, published in 2019, provides one of the most thoroughly documented case studies of contemporary influence operations. The Internet Research Agency, a St. Petersburg-based "troll farm" funded by Yevgeny Prigozhin, employed hundreds of staff working in shifts to manage thousands of fake social media accounts targeting American audiences.

What was distinctive about the IRA's operation was not that it was trying to get Americans to believe Russian state propaganda. It was trying to amplify genuine American divisions, authentic American grievances and real American voices in ways that served Russian strategic interests. IRA accounts did not primarily spread false content; they primarily spread real content, selected and amplified because it was divisive. They created Facebook groups and Twitter accounts that presented themselves as authentic American voices, built genuine followings among real American users, organized real events attended by real people and then used those platforms to intensify conflicts around race, immigration, gun rights and political identity that already existed.

The sophistication of the operation was in its authenticity. IRA staff were instructed to study American culture carefully, to write in convincingly American registers for different regional and demographic audiences, to post content that felt genuine because it was rooted in genuine American concerns. The accounts performed authenticity so well that real activists collaborated with them, real events they organized were attended by real people and real Americans shared their content because it resonated with real feelings.

"What disturbs me most about the IRA revelations isn't that Russians were lying to Americans. It's that the most effective thing they could do was tell Americans things that were essentially true, just curated and amplified for maximum division. They didn't have to manufacture the anger. They just had to find it and turn up the volume. That's much harder to defend against." — r/geopolitics, 43,829 upvotes

Firehosing and Epistemic Exhaustion

Political scientist Thomas Rid describes a propaganda technique that he calls the "firehose of falsehood," though it might more precisely be called the firehose of confusion. Rather than telling a consistent false story that can be debunked, it floods the information space with such a volume of contradictory, implausible and outrageous claims that audiences cannot effectively evaluate any of them. The goal is not to persuade people of any specific false claim but to exhaust their capacity to distinguish true from false, to produce a state of epistemic exhaustion in which "nothing is certain" and "everyone lies" feel like reasonable conclusions.

This technique exploits a basic asymmetry in information environments: producing confusion is much cheaper than producing clarity. A single false claim can be generated in seconds and requires extensive reporting effort to definitively debunk. A hundred simultaneous false claims make comprehensive debunking practically impossible, ensuring that most will circulate indefinitely regardless of their falsity. The firehose technique is particularly effective against audiences trained by journalistic norms to present "both sides," because it can manufacture apparent controversy around settled questions by simply generating content on the "other side."

The strategy of "flooding the zone with shit," as former Trump adviser Steve Bannon put it with unusual candor, operates through exactly this mechanism domestically. The purpose is not to establish any particular narrative but to prevent any narrative from being established, to create a permanent state of confusion in which objective facts cannot achieve the consensus status they need to function as shared political reality. A democracy requires some minimum of shared factual common ground to function, and epistemic flooding is specifically designed to destroy that common ground.

Astroturfing and the Construction of Fake Consensus

Astroturfing, the creation of apparently grassroots movements that are actually organized and funded by central actors, is not new. Public relations firms have been creating fake grassroots campaigns for corporate and political clients since at least the 1980s. What digital platforms have done is reduce the cost of astroturfing dramatically while increasing its potential reach and credibility.

A coordinated network of fake accounts can give any claim the appearance of widespread organic support by liking, sharing and commenting on it simultaneously, pushing it into the algorithmic feed of people who haven't actively sought it out. The signal that something is popular or trending is itself information that shapes belief, and manufactured trending can produce genuine trending as real users engage with content they perceive as organically popular. The fake consensus becomes real through a self-fulfilling process: fake accounts create apparent popularity, which drives algorithmic distribution to real users, which generates genuine engagement, which generates more algorithmic distribution, which creates genuine virality that is no longer dependent on the original fake network.

Researchers studying Twitter activity around contested political events have consistently found coordinated inauthentic behavior networks operating, typically identified by patterns of near-simultaneous activity, account creation date clustering, amplification of fringe content from small accounts, and linguistic patterns inconsistent with claimed user demographics. These networks are not primarily foreign; many are domestic, operated by political operatives, commercial astroturfers and ideological advocates who have developed sophisticated methods for manufacturing apparent consensus.

Microtargeting and the Personalization of Manipulation

Cambridge Analytica's 2018 scandal brought public attention to a practice that had been developing for years: the use of detailed psychological and behavioral data to deliver highly personalized political messaging to individual voters. The specific claims made by Cambridge Analytica about the effectiveness of their methods were substantially exaggerated, and the role of their specific techniques in the 2016 election results has been disputed by independent researchers. But the underlying practice of using psychographic targeting to personalize political messaging is real and continues to develop.

The psychographic model they used, developed by Cambridge University researcher Aleksandr Kogan, classified individuals on the OCEAN personality dimensions (Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism) based on their Facebook activity, and then tested different message framings for the same political content targeted at different personality profiles. A gun rights message framed around freedom and individual autonomy would be delivered to high-openness, high-extraversion profiles; the same underlying message framed around protection and stability would be delivered to high-conscientiousness, high-neuroticism profiles.

The significance of this approach is not primarily its claimed effectiveness, which as noted has been questioned. It is the model it represents: propaganda optimized not for a mass audience but for individual psychological profiles, delivered at a scale that individual broadcast cannot achieve and with a personalization that mass broadcast cannot match. As the data available for psychographic profiling improves, as the language models capable of generating personalized content improve, and as the costs of personalized content generation decline toward zero, this model will become progressively more powerful.

Deepfakes and the Coming Epistemic Crisis

The technology for creating convincing video forgeries of public figures saying things they never said has developed rapidly since 2017, when "deepfake" methods first became widely available. The current state of deepfake detection and generation is roughly equivalent to an arms race in which offense is temporarily ahead of defense: the most sophisticated generation methods can produce video that current detection tools cannot reliably identify. This will not be true permanently, and the technical cat-and-mouse game will continue. But the window during which convincing deepfakes are difficult to distinguish from real footage represents a significant vulnerability.

The strategic value of deepfakes in information operations may be less about their direct effectiveness than about their indirect effects. A public that knows that convincing video forgeries exist will be less certain about the authenticity of genuine footage they encounter. The liar's dividend, as law professors Robert Chesney and Danielle Keats Citron have called it, means that the mere possibility of deepfakes gives bad actors a plausible deniability defense for genuine footage of their actions. A genuine recording of a politician saying something compromising can be dismissed as a deepfake, and in an environment where the public cannot reliably distinguish genuine from forged video, this dismissal can be effective.

Inoculation at Scale and Democratic Resilience

The prebunking research discussed elsewhere, which has shown that exposure to weakened versions of influence operation techniques can provide cognitive resistance against those techniques, represents one of the more promising avenues for building democratic resilience against propaganda. Studies in multiple countries have found that brief interventions explaining how astroturfing, emotional manipulation and false consensus manufacturing work produce measurable improvements in people's ability to identify these techniques when they subsequently encounter them.

Several European democracies have incorporated media literacy and information manipulation education into school curricula, with Finland again representing the most ambitious implementation. Research on Finnish media literacy education suggests that it produces citizens who are better at identifying manipulation attempts and more confident in their ability to navigate information environments, though measuring the effects on political behavior is methodologically difficult.

What no individual-level intervention can address is the structural advantage that influence operations enjoy in algorithmically amplified information environments: content that exploits human psychological tendencies, including threat response, tribal identity and outrage, travels faster and farther than content that doesn't, regardless of its accuracy or the intentions of those who produced it. Changing those structural incentives through platform regulation, algorithmic transparency requirements and changes in advertising economics remains the most consequential intervention available, and also the one most effectively resisted by the commercial interests that benefit from the current system.
