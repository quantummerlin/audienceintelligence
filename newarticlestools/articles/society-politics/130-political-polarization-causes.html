Why We Are So Polarized

The Pew Research Center has tracked partisan polarization for decades, and their data tells a story that is difficult to look at directly. In 1994, 64 percent of Republicans held more conservative views than the median Democrat, and 70 percent of Democrats held more liberal views than the median Republican. By 2022, those numbers had risen to 97 and 94 percent respectively. The ideological overlap that had characterized American politics for most of the twentieth century had essentially disappeared. More striking, perhaps, is the affective dimension: in 1994, 21 percent of Republicans and 17 percent of Democrats viewed the opposing party "very unfavorably." By 2022, those numbers had risen to 62 and 54 percent. Americans have not just moved further apart in their views. They have come to genuinely dislike, distrust and in some cases fear each other at rates without precedent in modern survey data.

The standard explanations offered for this shift are familiar enough to have become clichés: social media filter bubbles, Fox News, gerrymandering, the decline of moderates, economic inequality, demographic change and the general coarsening of political discourse under various recent political figures. Each of these explanations contains some truth and none is sufficient. Understanding polarization at the level it deserves requires a more layered account that takes seriously both the structural and the psychological dimensions of what has happened.

The Distinction That Changes Everything: Issue vs. Affective Polarization

Political scientists distinguish between two types of polarization that are frequently conflated but behave quite differently. Issue polarization refers to increasing divergence in the policy positions that partisans hold: whether Republicans and Democrats hold increasingly different views on taxes, healthcare, immigration, climate and other concrete policy questions. Affective polarization refers to increasing negative feelings toward the opposing party: whether partisans have grown to dislike, distrust and dehumanize the other side regardless of specific policy content.

The empirical evidence on these two types points in somewhat different directions. Issue polarization is real but more limited than popular perception suggests. On many concrete policy questions, the gap between ordinary Republican and Democratic voters is not as large as their party elites, their media environments and their self-reported partisan identities would suggest. Majorities of both parties support background checks for gun purchases, some form of immigration reform, spending on infrastructure and protecting Social Security. The "culture war" issues on which the parties seem most divided are often ones where elite polarization substantially exceeds mass polarization.

Affective polarization, by contrast, has increased dramatically and is not well explained by issue divergence. People feel more strongly negative about the other party than their specific policy disagreements would warrant. They overestimate how extreme the other side is: research by More in Common found that both Democrats and Republicans dramatically overstated how many in the opposing party held extreme views. The average Democrat thought 44 percent of Republicans earned over $250,000 per year (the actual number is about 2 percent). The average Republican thought 38 percent of Democrats identified as LGBTQ (the actual number is about 6 percent). The imagined other side is a cartoon, and the cartoon is scarier and more extreme than the actual people.

The Sorting of American Politics

One of the most important structural shifts underlying contemporary polarization is what political scientists call partisan sorting: the process by which people who hold liberal views have increasingly concentrated in the Democratic Party and people who hold conservative views have increasingly concentrated in the Republican Party. This sounds like a tautology, but it represents a significant change from the mid-twentieth century, when both parties contained substantial internal ideological diversity. Conservative Democrats, particularly Southern Democrats, and liberal Republicans were common features of the political landscape as recently as the 1980s. They have largely disappeared.

The sorting process is partly a story about the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and subsequent realignment of Southern whites from the Democratic to the Republican Party, a process that accelerated over several decades and transformed both parties' ideological profiles. It is partly a story about the nationalization of politics: as national political identity has become more salient and local political identity less so, people have sorted themselves into ideologically coherent national parties even where local conditions might once have produced more mixed affiliations.

Bill Bishop documented the geographic dimension of this sorting in "The Big Sort" (2008), showing that Americans had been steadily moving into communities with people who vote like them, whether through active choice or through the cumulative effect of individual decisions about where to live. In 1976, 27 percent of Americans lived in "landslide counties" that gave one party a margin of over 20 percentage points. By 2004, that number had risen to 48 percent. By 2020, it was higher still. The geographic self-segregation by political identity that has resulted from these trends means that many Americans rarely encounter people who vote differently in the course of ordinary life, which makes the partisan out-group an abstract category defined primarily by media representations rather than actual human beings.

Elite Polarization and the Incentive Structure of Politics

Congressional polarization has increased even faster than mass polarization, and the mechanisms are somewhat different. The DW-NOMINATE scores that political scientists use to track congressional ideology show that Congress has become substantially more polarized over the past forty years, with the parties' median positions moving steadily apart and ideological overlap between them declining to near zero. Today's most conservative Democrat in Congress is more liberal than today's most liberal Republican, a configuration that was common in the 1970s and 1980s but has now essentially disappeared.

The incentives facing elected officials contribute systematically to this pattern. Primary elections, in which a relatively small and ideologically extreme electorate selects party nominees, reward politicians for ideological purity rather than bipartisan pragmatism. Campaign financing that depends on small donors who are ideologically motivated, and on large donors who support ideologically consistent candidates, creates similar pressures. The media ecosystem that covers politics rewards conflict and ideological clarity over nuance and compromise. And the decline of the seniority system and the rise of leadership power in Congress has reduced the incentives for individual legislators to build bipartisan coalitions, since leadership now controls committee assignments and floor schedules in ways that make cooperation with the opposing party difficult and politically costly.

The asymmetric polarization argument, associated with political scientists Thomas Mann and Norman Ornstein, holds that Republican polarization has been substantially more pronounced than Democratic polarization over the past several decades, with the GOP moving further from the mainstream than Democrats have. This argument remains contested, but the evidence that the Republican Party's ideological center of gravity shifted further and faster than the Democratic Party's during the 1990s, 2000s and 2010s is reasonably strong. Understanding why one party polarized faster than the other is important for understanding the overall dynamics of the current moment.

Social Identity and the Psychology of Partisan Conflict

Social identity theory, developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner in the 1970s and 1980s, provides an important psychological framework for understanding affective polarization. The theory holds that people derive significant portions of their self-esteem from their group memberships, and that this motivates them to perceive their own group favorably and competing groups unfavorably, even in the absence of concrete competition over resources or policy. In-group favoritism and out-group derogation arise from the basic structure of social identity, not from substantive conflict.

Liliana Mason's work, particularly her book "Uncivil Agreement" (2018), applies this framework to American partisan politics with illuminating results. She shows that partisan identity has become more "mega-identitarian" over time: it now correlates more strongly than it once did with racial identity, religious identity, geographic identity and cultural identity. A person who identified as a Democrat in 1975 might have been urban or rural, religious or secular, of any race, in any region. A person who identifies as a Democrat in 2025 is statistically more likely to be urban, non-religious, nonwhite and located in a coastal state. The partisan identity has absorbed other social identities, creating a mega-identity that feels more essential to self-concept than any of its components alone would be.

When partisan identity becomes a mega-identity of this kind, challenges to partisan positions feel like challenges to the self rather than merely disagreements about policy. Updating one's position on a political question in response to new evidence feels threatening to group membership, because changing positions could be read as disloyalty. Compromise with the opposing party feels threatening to identity, because it requires treating the out-group as worthy partners rather than as an existential threat. The policy becomes secondary to the identity, which is why disagreements that seem to be about facts and policies are often actually about identity and belonging.

Economic Anxiety and Distributional Politics

The economic dimension of polarization is contested but important. Research by Piketty and colleagues on political economy has documented a striking pattern: in the mid-twentieth century, left parties in Western democracies were predominantly the parties of lower-income voters, while right parties were predominantly the parties of higher-income voters. This class alignment has substantially inverted in recent decades, with educated upper-middle-class voters shifting strongly toward left parties and working-class voters shifting significantly toward right ones. The left parties have become the parties of educational elites and the right parties the parties of what political scientists call the "left-behind" working class.

The causes of this realignment are contested, but several factors seem important. Globalization and deindustrialization have devastated working-class communities in ways that the left parties, increasingly oriented toward the cultural concerns of their professional-class bases, were poorly positioned to address. The perceived cultural condescension of educated progressives toward working-class values, religious practice and place-based identity has driven resentment that maps onto political mobilization. And the populist right has been more effective than the reformist left at offering what political scientist Francis Fukuyama calls "recognition politics": acknowledgment of specific communities' dignity, grievances and importance in ways that speak directly to the resentments generated by economic and cultural marginalization.

What Reduces Polarization and What Doesn't

Research on what actually reduces affective polarization, as distinct from issue polarization, produces some counterintuitive findings. Contact with out-party members can reduce affective polarization but tends to work best when it is sustained and involves genuine interdependence and equal status. Brief or superficial encounters with members of the opposing party can increase rather than decrease negative feelings, partly because such encounters are more likely to activate in-group vs. out-group identity salience than to produce genuine human connection.

Accuracy nudges, prompting people to think about whether information they're sharing is accurate before they share it, produce modest but consistent reductions in political misinformation sharing. Exposure to the actual views of out-party members, rather than to media representations of those views, tends to reduce the dramatic overestimation of out-party extremism that More in Common documented. Structural reforms that change the incentives of elected officials, including ranked-choice voting, open primaries and redistricting reform, show evidence of modest effects on elite polarization in contexts where they have been implemented.

What has consistently failed to reduce polarization is direct confrontation of partisan misinformation, appeals to shared identity and values in political communication, or increased exposure to cross-cutting political content through social media (which, as noted above, can increase rather than reduce polarization when the content encountered is primarily the most extreme and inflammatory material the other side produces). The mechanisms of polarization are deeply embedded in identity, incentive structures and media dynamics that are not easily addressed by individual-level interventions. Addressing them structurally, through changes in how elections are conducted, how political media is incentivized and how civic institutions operate, is probably necessary to produce meaningful change.
