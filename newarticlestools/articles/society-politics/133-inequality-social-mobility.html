The Myth of the Meritocracy

69,384 upvotes · by u/born_on_third_base · 13 min read
▲ 69,384 people found this relatable

The American Dream is one of the most durable myths in modern political culture. It holds that regardless of where you start, hard work and talent will carry you to success, that the circumstances of your birth are not your destiny and that the United States offers a degree of social mobility unmatched anywhere in the developed world. Americans believe in this story more strongly than citizens of almost any other wealthy country. They also believe it more strongly than the evidence warrants, and the gap between the belief and the reality has grown substantially over the past half century.

The empirical research on social mobility, developed extensively by economists Raj Chetty, Nathaniel Hendren and colleagues, paints a picture that is considerably bleaker than the myth. A child born in the bottom fifth of the income distribution in the United States has roughly an 8 percent chance of reaching the top fifth. A child born in the top fifth has roughly a 33 percent chance of staying there. The degree to which where you end up is determined by where you started is substantially higher in the United States than in most comparable wealthy democracies, meaning that the country that most strongly believes in social mobility actually has less of it than the countries whose citizens are more skeptical.

The Great Gatsby Curve

Economist Miles Corak popularized the concept of the Great Gatsby Curve: the empirical relationship between income inequality and intergenerational social mobility across countries. The curve shows, consistently and across different datasets and methodologies, that countries with higher income inequality have lower intergenerational social mobility. The United States, with among the highest income inequality in the developed world, has among the lowest social mobility. The Nordic countries, with lower inequality, have substantially higher mobility.

The causal mechanisms connecting inequality to immobility are multiple and mutually reinforcing. High inequality concentrates resources in ways that allow wealthy families to invest heavily in their children's human capital through private education, enrichment activities, tutoring, college admissions consulting and legacy preferences at elite universities, while low-income families cannot. It concentrates residential sorting in ways that produce vast differences in school quality, neighborhood safety and peer environments between affluent and poor communities. And it concentrates political influence in ways that make policies benefiting wealthy families more likely to be enacted and maintained than policies that would improve opportunities for low-income children.

The "opportunity hoarding" concept, developed by Richard Reeves of the Brookings Institution, describes the specific mechanisms through which upper-middle-class families, not just the very wealthy but the top fifth of the income distribution, systematically use their advantages to preserve their children's position while advocating for meritocratic ideals. Legacy college admissions, exclusionary zoning that limits access to high-quality school districts, unpaid internships that only children with family support can afford, and professional licensing requirements that serve as barriers to entry more than quality assurance mechanisms are all tools through which advantages are transmitted under cover of merit.

I watched my classmates go into college application season with college counselors who had placed hundreds of students at Ivies, parents who donated buildings, family connections to the people reading their applications. I had none of that. I got into a good school but not the same schools they did, and they were not smarter or more hardworking than me. They just had more infrastructure. I don't resent them for it. Chetty's Geography of Opportunity

Raj Chetty's research on "Opportunity Atlas" data, which tracks the economic outcomes of millions of Americans born between 1978 and 1983 by childhood zip code and family income, produced findings that surprised even researchers in the field. Social mobility, it turned out, varies enormously by geography in ways that are not well predicted by state or regional economic conditions. Some counties in otherwise poor states have high mobility; some counties in otherwise prosperous states have low mobility. The variation is local, granular and strongly connected to specific features of community environment.

The factors most strongly associated with high mobility in specific communities include: low residential segregation by income and race, lower income inequality within the community, stronger civic participation and social capital (measured by participation in community organizations), lower rates of concentrated poverty and better school quality. The features least strongly associated include: crime rates (which have a weaker independent association with mobility than commonly assumed) and the specific economic base of the community.

The research also documented a striking finding about the role of social networks. Poor children who grew up in communities with more cross-class connections, specifically where they were more likely to have social ties to children from wealthier families, showed substantially better economic outcomes as adults than poor children from otherwise comparable communities with more economically segregated social networks. The mechanism appears to be information and network access: knowing people across economic lines provides information about educational and career opportunities, and provides connections that can translate into job referrals, recommendations and informal mentoring.

Wealth vs. Income and the Inheritance Economy

The standard discussion of inequality focuses on income, but wealth, the stock of assets rather than the annual flow of earnings, is at least as important for understanding social mobility and may be more important. Income determines current living standards; wealth determines long-term security, the ability to weather setbacks, access to capital for starting businesses and the inheritance that can be passed on to the next generation.

Wealth inequality in the United States is substantially more extreme than income inequality. According to Federal Reserve data, the top 1 percent of households own about 32 percent of all wealth, the top 10 percent own about 70 percent and the bottom 50 percent own about 2 percent. The racial wealth gap is even more striking: the median white family holds roughly eight times the wealth of the median Black family and five times the wealth of the median Hispanic family, differences that reflect not current income differentials but the accumulated effects of historical wealth-stripping policies, discriminatory lending and residential segregation over generations.

Economists estimate that inherited wealth accounts for somewhere between 40 and 80 percent of total wealth accumulation, with the range depending on methodology and how broadly "inheritance" is defined to include inter-vivos transfers, gifts, reduced rent and other forms of parental financial support beyond formal bequests. Even using conservative estimates, the degree to which wealth position is transmitted across generations substantially undermines the meritocratic story of wealth as the reward for individual effort and talent.

The Psychology of Meritocracy Belief

If the empirical case against strong meritocracy beliefs is robust, why do those beliefs persist, and why do they persist most strongly in the country where social mobility is most limited? Research in social psychology provides several complementary answers. System justification theory, developed by John Jost and colleagues, proposes that people are motivated to believe that the social system they live in is fair and legitimate, because believing it is illegitimate generates uncomfortable cognitive dissonance and existential anxiety. The motivation to justify the system is particularly strong among people who benefit from it, since they have the most at stake in its legitimacy, but it operates across the socioeconomic spectrum.

The "just world hypothesis," described by Melvin Lerner, proposes that people are motivated to believe that people get what they deserve and deserve what they get, because the alternative, that outcomes are substantially arbitrary and unjust, is psychologically threatening. This hypothesis predicts that people will tend to find post-hoc justifications for existing inequalities: attributing success to individual virtue and failure to individual failing, even when the evidence for these attributions is weak.

The psychological costs of meritocracy belief are substantial and underappreciated. For people who don't succeed by conventional measures despite effort and ability, the meritocratic framework places full responsibility for that failure on themselves. The student who works hard and doesn't get into an elite university, the worker who tries hard and loses their job to automation, the entrepreneur who builds something genuinely good and still fails: meritocracy tells all of them that the outcome reflects their own inadequacy. The result is not just disappointment but shame, which has documented consequences for mental health and which makes the standard political response to these experiences, mutual solidarity and systemic advocacy, psychologically difficult to access.

I kept trying to figure out what I'd done wrong. What Genuine Opportunity Would Require

The research on social mobility is not a counsel of despair. Several policy interventions have been shown to substantially improve intergenerational mobility. Early childhood education, particularly for low-income children, shows among the strongest returns of any public investment, with long-term effects on educational attainment, earnings and health that researchers like James Heckman have documented extensively. The Perry Preschool Program and Abecedarian Project, both rigorous randomized controlled trials of early childhood interventions, found returns of seven to twelve dollars for every dollar invested when the effects on reduced crime, increased employment and reduced public assistance dependence were fully accounted for.

Residential integration, reducing the extreme segregation by income and race that characterizes American metropolitan areas, has strong associations with improved mobility outcomes for low-income children. Research on the Moving to Opportunity experiment, which randomly assigned housing vouchers for low-income families to use in higher-opportunity neighborhoods, found that children who moved before age thirteen showed substantially higher adult earnings, higher college attendance rates and lower rates of single parenthood. The environment children grow up in matters enormously, and policies that give more children access to higher-quality environments produce lasting improvements in their outcomes.

What the research consistently shows is that genuine opportunity requires substantial investment in the conditions of childhood: quality early education, economically and racially integrated neighborhoods, well-funded schools, stable housing, access to healthcare and the social networks that provide information and connections. These are not things that individual effort can substitute for. A child cannot work harder to get a better school district or more civic infrastructure. The conditions of opportunity are collective conditions, and making them more equal requires collective action. The meritocratic story that obscures this basic fact is not just empirically inaccurate. It is politically convenient for those who benefit from inequality and politically harmful for those who need things to change.
