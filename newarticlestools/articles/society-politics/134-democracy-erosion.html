How Democracies Die Slowly

76,204 upvotes · by u/constitutionwatch · 13 min read
▲ 76,204 people found this relatable
Advertisement

The fantasy version of democratic collapse is clean and cinematic. Tanks roll into the capital square. The radio goes silent. Someone announces that the constitution has been suspended. Everyone understands immediately that something catastrophic has happened. There is a clear before and after. Resistance organizes, or it doesn't, but the rupture itself is unmistakable.

The real version is nothing like that. Political scientists Steven Levitsky and Lucan Way spent years studying democratic collapses around the world and reached a conclusion that should unsettle anyone who assumed they would recognize the moment if it came: most democracies in the twenty-first century don't end with a bang. They erode. The institutions that protect democratic governance are hollowed out gradually, each individual step looking defensible or at least ambiguous, the cumulative effect only visible in retrospect. By the time the erosion becomes undeniable, the mechanisms for reversing it have often already been compromised.

This is not an abstract concern for distant countries. Freedom House has documented declining political rights and civil liberties globally for seventeen consecutive years as of their 2023 report. The V-Dem Institute at the University of Gothenburg, which maintains the most comprehensive dataset on democratic quality, found that by 2022, roughly seventy-two percent of the world's population lived in autocracies, a share larger than at any point since the 1980s. Some of these were longstanding autocracies. But a significant number represented countries that had been classified as democracies within living memory and had since slid backward through a process their own populations largely failed to recognize while it was happening.

Understanding how that process works is not an academic exercise. It is practical knowledge about the thing most people in democratic societies say they value most.

The Classic Playbook

In their landmark 2018 book "How Democracies Die," Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt identified a recurring pattern across cases as varied as Hungary under Viktor Orban, Turkey under Erdogan, Venezuela under Chavez and Maduro, and Peru under Alberto Fujimori. What these cases share is not the methods of twentieth-century fascism. None of them began with suspended constitutions or abolished parliaments. All of them began with elected leaders who then used the powers of office to gradually rewrite the rules in their own favor.

The playbook typically unfolds in three phases. First, the incoming leader tests the guardrails. They take some action that pushes against established norms, watch what happens, and retreat or advance based on the response. If courts push back and the pushback is politically costly, the leader backs down. If courts are slow or the political cost is low, the probe becomes a precedent. Second, the leader begins capturing the referee institutions: courts, electoral commissions, prosecutorial offices, anti-corruption bodies, state media. This is usually done legally, through appointments and procedural changes, which makes it hard to characterize as an attack on democracy rather than a routine use of legitimate authority. Third, once the referees have been captured, the rules themselves can be changed with little effective resistance, and the formal trappings of democracy (elections, parliaments, constitutions) continue to exist while their substance has been drained.

"The thing that gets me is how normal everything looked while it was happening. Orban's Hungary didn't feel like a coup. It felt like a series of legislative decisions, each one individually defensible, that in combination created a system where the opposition genuinely cannot win even in a free vote because the rules have been rigged at every level. That's the horror. Democracy died in a suit and tie." r/PoliticalScience · 14,293 upvotes

Levitsky and Ziblatt's key insight is that the most important safeguards of democratic governance are not written in any constitution. They are norms: mutual toleration (accepting the legitimacy of political opponents) and institutional forbearance (restraining yourself from using every legal power available to you). The written rules of democratic systems are almost always exploitable by a sufficiently motivated actor who is willing to use them as weapons. What prevents that exploitation is a shared commitment among political elites to the informal norms that make the system function. When those norms erode, the written rules provide much thinner protection than most people assume.

The Hungary Case Study

Hungary is the most studied case of democratic backsliding in a European Union member state, which makes it particularly instructive because it happened within a context of international oversight that was supposed to prevent exactly this outcome. Viktor Orban and his Fidesz party won a landslide parliamentary victory in 2010 after a period of economic crisis and a genuinely incompetent left-wing government. He had a two-thirds parliamentary supermajority, which under the Hungarian constitution gave him the ability to change the constitution itself. He used it.

Within two years, Orban had implemented a new constitution (called the Fundamental Law) that restructured the Constitutional Court, changed electoral rules in ways that favored the majority party, lowered the mandatory retirement age for judges to force out hundreds of experienced jurists and replace them with loyalists, and created new regulatory bodies staffed with Fidesz allies to oversee media and elections. Each individual change had a legal basis. Each was portrayed as reform rather than power consolidation. The EU complained but took no consequential action. By the time EU institutions recognized the pattern clearly, the institutional landscape had been so thoroughly reshaped that reversing it through normal political means had become essentially impossible.

The electoral system changes are perhaps the most technically sophisticated part of the Fidesz model. A 2011 electoral reform redrew parliamentary district boundaries, reduced the number of parliamentary seats (which disproportionately hurt smaller parties), and changed the rules for forming candidate lists in ways that advantaged the incumbent. A subsequent law governing campaign finance and media access further tilted the playing field. By the 2022 election, independent analysts calculated that Fidesz could win a parliamentary majority with roughly forty-five percent of the vote, while the opposition would need something close to sixty percent. Orban won that election with fifty-four percent of the vote, which translated into a two-thirds parliamentary supermajority. The system had been engineered to make opposition victory nearly impossible short of a catastrophic Fidesz collapse.

The Norm Erosion Problem

The United States has its own version of this story, though it doesn't map cleanly onto the Orban model. American democratic backsliding scholars tend to point to a longer timeline of norm erosion that predates any single political actor. Newt Gingrich's transformation of congressional politics in the 1990s toward maximally adversarial tactics (treating opponents as enemies rather than rivals, weaponizing procedural rules, shutting down the government as a bargaining chip) represents one significant moment of norm erosion. The McConnell decision to hold the Merrick Garland Supreme Court seat open for nearly a year in 2016, citing a "principle" that was invented for that specific case and abandoned the moment circumstances changed in 2020, represents another. The refusal of a substantial portion of Congress to certify the 2020 election results represents a third, qualitatively different kind of norm violation because it directly concerned the peaceful transfer of power itself.

Political scientists have been careful about where the United States sits on the democratic backsliding spectrum. V-Dem's 2021 report classified the United States as having experienced "significant autocratization" between 2015 and 2020, placing it among the countries showing the most democratic decline during that period globally. But they also noted that American institutions had shown more resilience than predicted, with courts, electoral officials and civil servants repeatedly declining to participate in actions that would have meaningfully undermined electoral integrity. The question those scholars ask is whether that institutional resilience reflects genuine health or simply the fact that the people who were asked to cross bright lines declined to do so, which is a fragile safeguard because it depends on individual virtue rather than structural protection.

"I teach a course on comparative politics and I spend a lot of time on the concept of 'authoritarian legalism' -- the use of legal mechanisms to achieve anti-democratic outcomes. The brilliant and terrifying thing about it is that by the time you can clearly demonstrate it's happening, it's usually already worked. The courts you'd appeal to have been captured. The electoral rules have been changed. What's left?" r/AskPoliticalScientists · 11,847 upvotes

The comparative literature identifies several structural features that make democracies more or less vulnerable to this kind of erosion. Presidential systems are generally more vulnerable than parliamentary ones because they concentrate executive power and create winner-take-all dynamics that incentivize treating elections as existential conflicts. First-past-the-post electoral systems that produce two-party duopolies are more vulnerable than proportional representation systems because they create more extreme polarization and more zero-sum political competition. Young democracies with thin institutional legacies are more vulnerable than older ones with deep norms. Countries with high inequality are more vulnerable because economic desperation creates constituencies for strong leaders who promise to cut through institutional constraints.

Competitive Authoritarianism vs. Full Authoritarianism

Levitsky and Way introduced the concept of "competitive authoritarianism" to describe a regime type that has become increasingly common: systems that maintain the formal institutions of democracy (regular elections, opposition parties, independent press in some form, civil society organizations) while systematically tilting the playing field so that the incumbent almost always wins. The competition is real but it is not fair. The opposition can contest elections but faces so many disadvantages in media access, campaign finance, legal harassment of candidates and organizations, and voter suppression that winning requires overcoming enormous structural obstacles.

This regime type is particularly insidious because it can persist for decades while being genuinely ambiguous to outside observers. Incumbents in competitive authoritarian systems frequently win with large margins, which they cite as evidence of democratic legitimacy. They can point to the existence of opposition parties and a press that technically exists and technically criticizes them, even if that press faces regulatory harassment and its funding has been strangled. International election monitoring missions frequently issue reports with carefully worded language about "irregularities" and "concerns" that stop well short of calling elections fraudulent because the irregularities, while real and significant, don't meet the evidentiary standard for outright fraud.

Russia under Putin for most of his tenure represented competitive authoritarianism rather than full authoritarianism. Opposition parties existed, opposition figures ran for office, and some limited media criticism was tolerated. The system was rigged at multiple levels but it wasn't the Soviet system. The invasion of Ukraine in 2022 and its domestic political consequences appear to represent a transition toward something harder and more fully authoritarian, but for two decades Russia occupied this ambiguous middle zone that defied easy categorization. Hungary currently occupies a similar zone, which is why EU membership has proved no barrier to its democratic backsliding.

The Role of Polarization

One of the most important mechanisms enabling democratic erosion is political polarization, and specifically what Levitsky and Ziblatt call "mutual toleration failure" -- the collapse of the shared belief that political opponents are legitimate competitors rather than existential enemies. When political conflict becomes existential (when each side genuinely believes that losing power means not just policy setbacks but the destruction of the nation or their group's survival), the norms of restraint that protect democratic processes become much harder to maintain. Extraordinary measures start to seem justified because the stakes are cast as extraordinary.

There is a well-documented relationship in the comparative literature between high levels of partisan polarization and democratic backsliding. This relationship runs in both directions: polarization enables democratic erosion because it makes populations more willing to tolerate norm violations by their own side, and democratic erosion deepens polarization because authoritarian leaders actively cultivate the sense of existential threat that justifies their consolidation of power. Orban is a master at this. His political communications consistently frame Hungarian politics as a civilizational struggle, not an ordinary competition over policy. When ordinary policy competition has been reframed as a civilizational struggle, democratic constraints on power start to look like naive handicaps.

"The research on how polarization enables backsliding is genuinely frightening. People who would never accept anti-democratic behavior in the abstract will accept it when they believe the other side is an existential threat. And leaders who want to erode democracy have strong incentives to make people believe the other side is an existential threat. It's a self-reinforcing loop." r/Democracy · 9,284 upvotes

Milan Svolik at Yale has done particularly important work on this dynamic. In experimental research, he found that when voters are forced to choose between democratic principles and partisan outcomes, a substantial portion will sacrifice the democratic principles. Voters whose party violated democratic norms were significantly less likely to punish that party electorally when the policy stakes felt high. The implication is sobering: the democratic check of electoral accountability on anti-democratic behavior is weakest precisely when that behavior is most dangerous, because the conditions that produce anti-democratic behavior (high stakes, existential framing) are the same conditions that make voters most willing to overlook it.

Institutional Resilience and Its Limits

Not every democratic stress test produces backsliding. Comparative politics scholars have identified factors that seem to protect democratic resilience even under significant pressure. Strong civil society organizations that operate independently of both the state and political parties matter enormously because they provide organizational infrastructure for democratic resistance that doesn't depend on electoral success. An independent judiciary with real autonomy and genuine willingness to rule against the government matters because it represents a genuine veto point on executive overreach. A professional civil service with strong norms of nonpartisanship matters because it prevents the administrative machinery of government from becoming a tool of the ruling party. A free press with economic sustainability independent of government favor matters because it enables the information environment that democratic accountability requires.

The United States has all of these features but in varying and sometimes deteriorating forms. American civil society is genuinely robust and showed remarkable organizational capacity in the years after 2016. The federal judiciary demonstrated significant independence from executive pressure on multiple occasions. The civil service showed real resistance to pressure in key moments. But the economic sustainability of independent press continues to collapse, the judiciary faces legitimacy crises that undermine public confidence in its independence, and civil society organizations face increasing legal and financial pressure in some states.

Tom Ginsburg and Aziz Huq, in "How to Save a Constitution," identify what they call "constitutional retrogression" as distinct from full democratic collapse: a gradual degradation of democratic quality that falls short of outright authoritarianism but makes the system measurably less free, less fair and less responsive. Their argument is that constitutional retrogression can be stable -- it doesn't necessarily slide all the way to full authoritarianism -- but it represents a genuine loss that compounds over time. A system that has retrogressed moderately can retrogress further given the right conditions. The losses are real even when they stop short of the worst case.

What Ordinary Citizens Actually Experience

One of the most psychologically important aspects of democratic erosion is how invisible it is to ordinary people while it is happening. The mechanisms of erosion operate primarily through institutional processes that most people do not follow closely: judicial appointments, regulatory agency staffing, electoral commission decisions, changes to campaign finance rules, media ownership consolidation. These are not the stuff of compelling news coverage and they do not produce immediate visible changes in daily life. The person who woke up the day after Hungary's 2011 Fundamental Law went into effect did not experience a different daily existence. The buses ran. The shops were open. The government was not arresting random people in the night.

This is the detection problem that makes democratic erosion so dangerous. Because the immediate material experience of citizens doesn't change rapidly, the social and psychological pressure to sound alarm bells feels disproportionate to what people are personally experiencing. People who warn loudly about democratic backsliding are frequently accused of alarmism, hysteria and a failure of perspective. This social pressure toward normalcy is itself a mechanism of erosion -- it silences the early warnings that might mobilize resistance at a stage when resistance is still relatively easy.

"I lived in Hungary in 2009 and 2010 and came back in 2016. I'm telling you, nobody I knew in 2010 thought they were about to lose their democracy. They thought they were electing a right-wing government that would fix the economy. The Fidesz voter base was normal people who wanted normal things. By 2016 the system had changed so fundamentally that I could barely recognize it, but it happened so gradually that most of the people I knew had just... adapted. They had adjusted their sense of what normal was." r/europe · 16,492 upvotes

Hannah Arendt wrote about the banality of evil in a different context, but her core insight about how ordinary people participate in catastrophic processes by simply doing their jobs and not asking too many questions translates to democratic erosion. Most of the people who enable democratic backsliding are not ideological extremists who want authoritarianism. They are judges who make legally defensible rulings under pressure. They are civil servants who implement policies they find uncomfortable but not clearly illegal. They are journalists who soften their coverage slightly to protect their access. They are businesspeople who avoid publicly criticizing the government because regulatory retaliation is a real possibility. They are voters who prioritize policy outcomes over democratic process. Each individual choice is understandable. The aggregate produces something none of them would endorse if asked directly.

The Backsliding Recovery Problem

Can democratic backsliding be reversed? The historical record is not encouraging. The V-Dem Institute tracks what it calls "autocratization events" (periods of significant democratic decline) and "democratization events" (periods of significant democratic improvement). Their data shows that autocratization events that begin under an elected government are substantially harder to reverse than those that begin with an outright coup, precisely because the gradual nature of electoral authoritarianism makes it difficult to identify a clear rupture that would justify the kind of dramatic mobilization that coup attempts frequently generate.

There are genuine success stories. South Korea arrested two former presidents and managed significant institutional reform in the 2010s. Peru, despite enormous instability, has shown surprising institutional resilience with multiple presidents facing criminal accountability. Georgia in 2003 and Ukraine in 2004 and 2014 managed to reverse authoritarian consolidations through mass mobilization. But these cases required unusual combinations of factors: a mobilized civil society, a divided or weakened ruling elite, significant international pressure, and often a precipitating event dramatic enough to overcome the normalcy bias that protects incumbent governments.

Hungary is not on a reversal trajectory. The opposition did manage to unify for the 2022 election and ran a credible candidate, but lost decisively in part because the structural disadvantages they faced (gerrymandering, media access disparity, campaign finance rules, state resource use by the incumbent) were simply too large to overcome even with a unified campaign. The V-Dem Institute currently classifies Hungary as an "electoral autocracy," having crossed from a democracy designation in 2018. That classification change was debated, with some scholars arguing it was premature, but the trend direction is not seriously disputed.

What Protects Democracy

The research literature converges on several things that appear to genuinely protect democratic governance against backsliding, as opposed to things that merely feel protective.

Cross-cutting social identities matter. Societies where partisan identity is not perfectly correlated with ethnic, religious and class identity show more resilience to the existential framing that enables democratic erosion. When your neighbor of a different party is also your coworker, your cousin's spouse, and a member of your church, the existential threat narrative is harder to maintain. Societies with high levels of cross-cutting contact appear to punish anti-democratic norm violations more consistently than those with highly sorted partisan geographies. This connects directly to the polarization research discussed in the article on political polarization and the importance of contact under the right conditions.

Electoral system design matters more than most people realize. Countries using proportional representation systems have significantly lower rates of democratic backsliding than those using winner-take-all systems, likely because PR systems force governing coalitions that require negotiation, reduce zero-sum electoral dynamics and produce more ideologically moderate outcomes. This is a structural argument for electoral reform, not a guarantee, but the correlation is strong enough to take seriously.

An independent press with sustainable economics matters enormously. The correlation between local newspaper closure (documented in depth in the article on the death of local news) and measures of democratic accountability at the local level is well established. Governments whose actions go unreported face less accountability. Politicians whose corruption is documented face electoral consequences. The erosion of the press is not just a cultural loss. It is a structural weakening of democratic accountability mechanisms.

"I keep coming back to the point about mutual toleration. If you genuinely believe the other side will destroy the country if they win, you're going to rationalize almost anything to stop them. And that belief, once it takes hold at a mass level, is what makes democratic erosion possible. The problem isn't just the leaders who want to erode democracy. It's the millions of ordinary people who've been convinced their opponents are enemies rather than rivals." r/PoliticalPhilosophy · 13,104 upvotes

Finally, and most controversially in an era of partisan bitterness, the norm of mutual toleration itself requires active cultivation. This doesn't mean false equivalence or pretending all political positions are equally valid. It means maintaining the distinction between "people I disagree with profoundly on policy" and "people who must be destroyed." It means accepting that losing an election is bearable, that the other side will sometimes govern, that this is preferable to a system where elections determine everything permanently. It means, as Levitsky and Ziblatt put it, treating rivals as "loyal opposition" rather than existential enemies. This norm has been under genuine stress in many democracies, and the stress is not evenly distributed -- some political actors have worked much harder to destroy it than others -- but rebuilding it is a precondition for the other protections to function.

Democratic institutions are not self-sustaining mechanisms. They are agreements among people about how to manage disagreement and share power. Those agreements require continuous renewal, and the renewal has to happen at multiple levels simultaneously: in the behavior of political elites, in the expectations of voters, in the coverage choices of the press, in the professional norms of civil servants and judges, and in the willingness of ordinary citizens to pay attention to institutional processes that don't produce immediate visible changes in their daily lives. That's a lot of conditions to maintain simultaneously. But the alternative -- the slow hollowing out of the system until the formal shell remains and the substance is gone -- is something most people would recognize as catastrophic if it happened all at once. The challenge is recognizing it when it happens one incremental step at a time.
