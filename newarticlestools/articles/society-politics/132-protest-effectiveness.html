Does Protest Actually Work?

58,293 upvotes · by u/street_level_change · 12 min read
▲ 58,293 people found this relatable
Advertisement

After every major protest movement, the same debate resurfaces. Those who participated insist that protest was necessary, powerful and consequential. Critics argue that it accomplished little or nothing, or worse, that it backfired by alienating potential supporters. Those who are skeptical of any specific protest ask whether participants could have spent their time more effectively. The debate is usually conducted with more conviction than evidence, by people whose conclusions tend to follow their political priors rather than what the research actually shows.

The social science on protest effectiveness is genuinely complicated, and anyone who tells you that protest always works or never works is telling you something false. The research identifies specific conditions under which protest is more or less likely to produce the outcomes participants seek, and understanding those conditions matters enormously for anyone trying to create change rather than simply express their values, which are distinct projects that are sometimes confused with each other.

The Basic Evidence: Protest Has Changed Laws and Policies

The starting point for any honest assessment is the historical record, which is clear on one basic point: protest movements have achieved major policy changes in democratic societies. The civil rights movement produced the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. The women's suffrage movement produced the 19th Amendment. The labor movement produced the eight-hour workday, occupational safety regulations and collective bargaining rights. The antiwar movement contributed to the end of American military involvement in Vietnam. Same-sex marriage moved from legally impossible to constitutionally protected in the United States in approximately twenty years, driven largely by the sustained advocacy of LGBTQ+ activists.

These are real achievements that required sustained collective action and that produced concrete changes in law and policy that affected millions of people. They also involved far more than street protests: litigation, electoral organizing, coalition building, legislative lobbying, cultural work and sustained organizing over years and decades. The question of what role specific protests played in these larger movements, versus the other components of those movements, is genuinely difficult to answer and varies substantially by case.

The political scientist Erica Chenoweth's systematic comparative study of resistance campaigns, published with Maria Stephan in "Why Civil Resistance Works" (2011), provides some of the most important quantitative evidence on protest effectiveness. Their analysis of 323 violent and nonviolent campaigns between 1900 and 2006 found that nonviolent campaigns succeeded about 53 percent of the time, compared to 26 percent for violent ones. They also found that campaigns that achieved peak participation of 3.5 percent of the population were extraordinarily successful, with no historical case of failure once this threshold was reached.

"The 3.5 percent threshold finding changed how I think about organizing. It means change doesn't require everyone. It doesn't even require most people. It requires sustained, active participation from a meaningful minority plus broad sympathy from the larger population. Most movements that fail do so not because they can't win hearts and minds but because they can't sustain active participation long enough to create the political crisis that forces concession." — r/AskSocialScience, 34,284 upvotes

The Radical Flank Effect: When Does Militancy Help or Hurt?

One of the most debated questions in protest research is the "radical flank effect": whether the presence of more radical actors alongside moderate ones helps or hurts the moderate faction's prospects for winning concessions. The intuition behind both positions is plausible. More radical actors might make moderates look reasonable by comparison, making concessions to moderates politically easier for opponents to justify ("we're making a deal with the reasonable people to prevent the radical ones from prevailing"). Or more radical actors might alienate potential supporters and give opponents political cover to reject any movement-affiliated demand.

The historical evidence suggests the answer is highly context-dependent. The positive version of the radical flank effect appears to have operated in the civil rights movement, where Martin Luther King Jr.'s nonviolent campaigns benefited from the implicit contrast with more radical actors who made King's demands seem reasonable and his interlocutors willing to negotiate. Research by sociologists Herbert Haines and others found similar dynamics in several other successful movements.

The negative version appears to operate when radical tactics alienate the sympathetic middle of the population whose support movements need. Research by Omar Wasow on the 1960s found that nonviolent civil rights protests in counties near violent protests were associated with reduced Democratic vote shares in subsequent elections, suggesting that political violence associated with movements can produce electoral backlash that undermines the movement's legislative goals. His subsequent work on the 2020 Black Lives Matter protests found that violence associated with some protests was associated with modest shifts in public opinion away from the movement.

The implication is not that radical actors always hurt movements but that the relationship between militancy and outcome depends on specific factors: who the audience is, what concessions are being sought, what the political context looks like and whether radical tactics can be clearly distinguished from the mainstream movement or will be attributed to it regardless. Movements that maintain clear differentiation between their mainstream and radical wings while preserving the implicit threat that failure to concede to moderates will empower radicals have historically done better than movements that blur these distinctions.

The Disruption Question

A significant thread in protest research asks whether disruptive tactics, those that impose costs on bystanders and authorities rather than simply expressing a position, are more or less effective than conventional protest. The theoretical argument for disruption is straightforward: elected officials respond to political pressure, and disruption creates pressure that ordinary petition and lobbying cannot, by raising the cost of ignoring a movement's demands. The theoretical argument against is that disruption alienates potential supporters who might otherwise sympathize with a movement's goals but who experience the disruption as personally harmful.

The empirical evidence generally supports the disruption hypothesis when the disruption is clearly connected to the issue being protested and when the disrupted parties have the power to grant the movement's demands. The bus boycotts of the civil rights movement are the paradigm: Black consumers boycotted bus services in ways that directly cost the bus companies and indirectly pressured local authorities, while clearly communicating what change they were seeking. The disruption was experienced primarily by those with power to concede, not by uninvolved bystanders, and the connection between the tactic and the demand was clear.

Disruption that primarily affects uninvolved parties rather than those with power to concede, and disruption whose connection to the movement's demands is unclear to observers, tends to produce less favorable outcomes. Research on highway-blocking protests consistently finds that they generate hostility from the people directly disrupted, that this hostility extends to the movement's goals and that the political pressure produced is more likely to be mobilized against the movement than in its favor. The disruption calculus requires asking both "does this create political pressure on those who can grant our demands" and "does this produce sympathy or hostility in the audiences whose support we need."

The Numbers Question: Does Scale Matter?

The relationship between protest size and political outcomes is intuitive but complicated. Larger protests signal broader support, which should increase political pressure on decision-makers who respond to constituency preferences. Research by Andreas Madestam and colleagues, published in 2013, found that larger Tea Party rallies in a county on April 15, 2009 were associated with higher Republican vote shares in the 2010 midterms, suggesting a genuine mobilization and political effect from protest size. Similar research on other protest events has found measurable electoral effects from variation in protest size driven by weather conditions on the day of the protest (which provide a natural experiment for size, since weather affects turnout but not underlying political conditions).

But scale is not sufficient in the absence of sustained organization. The Women's March of January 2017 was the largest single-day protest in American history by many estimates, with roughly 3 to 4 million participants across the country. Its immediate political impact was minimal: it did not prevent any Trump administration initiative and produced no immediate legislative or policy response. Its longer-term electoral impact was more substantial: research found that counties with larger Women's March participation saw larger increases in Democratic voter registration and Democratic vote shares in subsequent elections, suggesting that the protest activated political engagement that was channeled into electoral work over time.

The distinction between protest as event and protest as organizing is important here. A protest that functions primarily as a one-time expression, however large, has a different potential for political impact than a protest that functions as a visible tip of sustained organizational infrastructure. The civil rights marches worked partly because they were the public face of extensive organizational work, legal strategy, voter registration campaigns and economic pressure that operated year-round. The question "did the march work" cannot be meaningfully answered by looking at the march alone.

"After the 2017 Women's March I was energized and then confused about what to do next. I marched, I felt connected to something bigger, and then it was over and nothing had changed. The protests that actually produced change were the ones with an 'and then what' — a plan, a structure, a way to turn the energy into sustained pressure. I learned to ask that question before I joined anything after that." — r/WomenInPolitics, 41,293 upvotes

International Comparisons and Regime Type

Protest effectiveness varies dramatically by political context. Chenoweth and Stephan's finding that nonviolent resistance succeeds about 53 percent of the time is an average across very different political settings. In consolidated democracies, protest movements have more institutional channels available to them and their success rates are generally higher. In authoritarian regimes, protest is both more dangerous and more dependent on specific mechanisms for success, particularly the ability to draw in the sympathies of regime elites and security forces.

The Arab Spring demonstrations of 2011 illustrate the complexity. The same period of protest activity produced democratic transition in Tunisia, regime survival with cosmetic reform in Morocco, civil war in Libya and Syria, military coup in Egypt and effective suppression in Bahrain. The differences cannot be explained by protest size, methods or commitment. They are explained by the different structural conditions in each country: the cohesion or fragmentation of elite power, the loyalty or ambivalence of security forces, the strength or weakness of civil society organizations, the economic vulnerability of the regime and the degree of international attention and pressure.

This contextual dependence is one of the most important and underappreciated findings in protest research. The same tactics in the same timeframe produced dramatically different results in different structural contexts. Activists who import tactics from successful movements without attending to the structural differences between those contexts and their own frequently discover that the tactics don't transfer.

What the Evidence Supports

Drawing these threads together, the research supports several conclusions about when protest is more likely to work. Protest is more likely to achieve policy goals when it is part of a broader movement with clear demands that institutional actors have the power to meet. When it achieves sufficient scale to signal genuine mass support rather than a committed minority. When it can sustain activity over time rather than peaking at a single event. When it includes organizational infrastructure that can channel engagement into electoral and legislative work. When the tactics used create pressure on those with power to grant demands rather than primarily on uninvolved bystanders. And when the movement can maintain strategic discipline across the range of actors in its coalition.

Protest is less likely to achieve policy goals when it prioritizes expression over pressure, when it lacks organizational infrastructure behind the visible events, when its tactics consistently alienate the sympathetic majority whose support it needs or when it operates in structural contexts where the mechanisms connecting protest to political response are absent or blocked. None of this means that protest that doesn't achieve immediate policy goals is worthless: it can build organizational capacity, develop leadership, create community among participants and shift cultural norms in ways that produce longer-term changes. But those are different goals from policy change, and conflating them produces confusion about what protest can and cannot do.
