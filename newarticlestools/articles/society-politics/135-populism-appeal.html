Why Populism Keeps Winning

67,518 upvotes · by u/vox_populi_watcher · 13 min read
▲ 67,518 people found this relatable
Advertisement

Every few years a populist movement wins an election that political scientists, pollsters and mainstream commentators had declared impossible, and the immediate aftermath follows a now-familiar script. Analysts struggle to explain what happened in terms of voter confusion, media failure, foreign interference, or the particular charisma of the winning candidate. Critics reach for words like "irrational," "dangerous" and "unprecedented." Defenders insist the voters were simply expressing legitimate frustrations. Both sides miss the more uncomfortable and more interesting question, which is why the frustrations that fuel populism are so persistent, so geographically widespread and so consistently underestimated by the people who get paid to understand politics.

Populism has been winning elections across radically different cultural and economic contexts for two decades now. Trump in the United States. Orban in Hungary. Bolsonaro in Brazil. Modi in India. Duterte in the Philippines. Meloni in Italy. Erdogan in Turkey. Morales in Bolivia. Chavez in Venezuela. These figures represent both left and right variants of populism, both democratic and authoritarian trajectories, both wealthy and developing country contexts. What they share is not an ideology in the conventional sense but a political logic: the claim that there is a corrupt, self-serving elite and a virtuous, betrayed people, and that the leader embodies and speaks for the latter against the former.

That logic keeps working because it is responding to something real. The academic consensus on populism is increasingly clear that it is best understood not as a disease infecting an otherwise healthy democratic body but as a symptom of democratic pathologies that preexist it. Understanding what populism is responding to is not the same as endorsing its proposed solutions, many of which are genuinely dangerous. But the habitual refusal to take the underlying grievances seriously is itself part of why populism continues to win.

What Populism Actually Is

Political theorist Cas Mudde, whose work on populism is probably the most widely cited in the academic literature, defines it as a "thin-centered ideology" that divides society into two homogeneous and antagonistic groups: a pure people and a corrupt elite, with politics understood as the expression of the general will of the people. It is "thin-centered" because it can attach to almost any substantive ideology -- nationalist, socialist, environmentalist, religious -- and amplify its logic through the people-versus-elite frame. This is why populism appears on both the left and the right, why it can be economically redistributive or regressive, why it can be secular or theocratic. The core logic is relational, not substantive.

Mudde distinguishes populism from nativism (the belief that the nation should be inhabited only by members of the native group) and authoritarianism (the belief in a strictly ordered society with severe punishment for deviants), both of which frequently accompany right-wing populism but are analytically separate. This distinction matters because it prevents conflating every populist movement with its most dangerous variants. Left-wing populism in Latin America and the anti-austerity movements in Southern Europe operate on the same basic populist logic without the nativist and authoritarian accompaniments. Understanding populism requires holding both the common structure and the specific variants in view simultaneously.

"The definition I keep coming back to is Mudde's: populism says there's a pure people and a corrupt elite and politics should express the will of the people. Once you have that frame in mind you see it everywhere, across the political spectrum. Bernie Sanders and Trump are both populists in this sense, even though they identify completely different elites and completely different peoples." r/PoliticalTheory · 12,847 upvotes

Ernesto Laclau, approaching populism from a left-theoretical perspective, had a more positive assessment. He argued in "On Populist Reason" (2005) that populism is not a pathology but the basic logic of democratic politics: the construction of a "people" out of a diverse collection of demands that lack other means of political expression. For Laclau, the question is not whether populism is good or bad but whose populism, serving whose demands, against whose elite. This view has its critics -- it can seem to dissolve important distinctions between democratic and authoritarian populism -- but it captures something important: the claim to speak for "the people" is not inherently anti-democratic. It depends entirely on what you do with it.

The Representation Deficit

The most compelling academic explanation for the persistence of populist appeal is what political scientists call the "representation deficit" -- the growing gap between what mainstream political parties offer and what large segments of the electorate actually want. This gap operates on multiple dimensions simultaneously.

On economic policy, mainstream center-left and center-right parties across the developed world converged dramatically during the 1990s and 2000s around a broadly neoliberal consensus: free trade, financial deregulation, fiscal restraint and skepticism of state intervention in the economy. This convergence made sense within the frameworks those parties used to understand economics, and it produced genuine benefits including low inflation and sustained (if unequally distributed) growth. But it also produced a large population of people who saw their economic position deteriorate -- manufacturing workers displaced by trade, communities hollowed out by deindustrialization, young people unable to afford housing in cities that had become globally connected and globally expensive -- who had no mainstream political home for their economic grievances. When someone came along and said the elites had sold them out for their own benefit, that narrative fit a real experience even if the causal account was simplified.

Thomas Piketty's analysis in "Capital and Ideology" adds an important dimension: the social composition of mainstream left parties has fundamentally changed. The traditional left coalition of working-class voters and intellectual professionals has fragmented, with the educated professional class increasingly dominating center-left parties both in terms of voter share and party leadership. Piketty documents this trend across multiple European countries and the United States: what he calls the "Brahmin left" (high-education, high-status voters and leaders) now defines center-left parties in ways that have alienated large portions of their traditional working-class base. The result is a political landscape where working-class voters without college degrees increasingly feel politically homeless, which makes them available to whoever credibly claims to represent them against everyone else.

"The thing about the 'representation deficit' framing is that it forces you to ask what exactly mainstream parties stopped representing. And the honest answer is: the economic interests and cultural sensibilities of people without college degrees who live outside major metro areas. Those are a lot of people to write off, and they noticed." r/AskEconomics · 9,473 upvotes

On cultural policy, a parallel convergence happened. Mainstream parties across the political spectrum largely adopted a liberal cosmopolitan consensus on questions of immigration, multiculturalism, gender and sexuality that was genuinely supported by educated urban voters but moved significantly ahead of majority opinion on several dimensions among older and rural populations. This produced a second dimension of representation deficit: large groups of people who felt that their cultural anxieties (about national identity, about rapid demographic change, about the pace of social transformation) were not just unaddressed but actively stigmatized as backward and morally disqualifying. The combination of economic and cultural dismissal is explosive.

Status Threat and the Psychology of Resentment

Economic explanations for populism are necessary but not sufficient. The empirical research consistently finds that the strongest predictors of right-wing populist support are not simply income or employment status but measures of relative status and what sociologists call "status anxiety" -- the fear of losing one's social position, of being displaced in the social hierarchy by groups that previously occupied lower positions. Arlie Hochschild's "Strangers in Their Own Land," based on years of ethnographic fieldwork among Louisiana Tea Party supporters, identifies what she calls a "deep story" that her subjects inhabited: a sense of having waited in line their whole lives, followed the rules, done what they were supposed to do, only to watch others cut the line with the active assistance of a government that was supposed to serve them.

This deep story is not primarily about absolute economic deprivation. Many of Hochschild's subjects were not poor by objective measures. It is about a perceived disruption of a moral order in which their patience and rule-following deserved recognition and reward. The anger is about perceived violation of desert, about the sense that the game has been rigged against people like them. This is a distinct grievance from straightforward economic anxiety, and it responds to different political messaging. Economic policies that improve material conditions do not necessarily address it. What addresses it is recognition: the sense that someone sees them, acknowledges their worth, and is fighting on their behalf against the forces they feel are degrading them.

Jonathan Haidt's moral foundations framework is relevant here. His research suggests that conservatives weight a broader range of moral foundations than liberals, including loyalty, authority and sanctity alongside the care and fairness foundations that tend to dominate liberal moral reasoning. Populist leaders who speak the language of loyalty (to the nation, to "real" members of the community), authority (strong leadership, respect for tradition) and sanctity (the purity of the homeland against corrupting foreign influence) are activating moral intuitions that mainstream liberal politics has often dismissed as atavistic but that are genuine and widespread. Dismissing them as mere bigotry forecloses understanding what is actually driving support.

The Elite Credibility Collapse

Populism's claim that the elite is corrupt and self-serving requires a credible target. The last two decades have supplied many. The 2008 financial crisis, in which financial institutions that had engaged in reckless and in many cases fraudulent behavior were bailed out with public money while millions of ordinary people lost their homes, jobs and savings, was a defining credibility event for mainstream political and economic institutions in the developed world. The executives who ran the institutions that nearly destroyed the global economy received bonuses. Almost none faced criminal accountability. The message received by a large portion of the public was that the system of rules that applies to everyone else does not apply to the people who run things.

This perception did not emerge from nowhere or from propaganda alone. It was rooted in a genuine double standard. And it came on top of other credibility-destroying events: the Iraq War and the intelligence failures or manipulations that justified it, the child sexual abuse scandal in the Catholic Church and its systematic cover-up by institutional authorities, the revelations about mass government surveillance programs, the Panama Papers and Pandora Papers documenting the offshore wealth-hiding infrastructure used by the global elite, the various pharmaceutical pricing scandals, the college admissions scandal that revealed elite families gaming systems that supposedly reward merit. Each of these events, in different ways, provided empirical grounding for the populist narrative that the people who run things operate by different rules and actively work to protect their position at everyone else's expense.

"You can't understand the appeal of 'drain the swamp' without understanding that for a lot of people it wasn't an abstraction. They watched the banks get bailed out. They watched nobody go to prison. They watched their community fall apart while the people who caused it were fine. When someone comes along and says 'the system is rigged and I'm going to blow it up,' that's not crazy. It's a completely legible response to what they actually observed." r/Economics · 14,829 upvotes

Yascha Mounk's "The People vs. Democracy" traces how the combination of rights without democracy (technocratic governance that overrides democratic preferences in the name of expertise) and democracy without rights (majoritarian populism that overrides minority protections) has squeezed the center that previously held liberal democratic governance together. The technocratic turn in governance -- the delegation of major decisions to independent central banks, international bodies, courts and regulatory agencies that are deliberately insulated from democratic accountability -- produced real benefits in policy stability but also produced a democratic deficit that populists successfully exploit. When people are told that the important decisions cannot actually be made by the people they elect, the populist who promises to give power back to the people is making an offer that addresses a real problem, even if their proposed solution creates worse ones.

Left Populism and Right Populism

The left variant of populism targets economic elites specifically: corporations, financial institutions, billionaires and the political class that serves them. Bernie Sanders, Jeremy Corbyn and the Podemos movement in Spain represent this variant. The enemy is the billionaire class, the Wall Street donor class, the political establishment that sold out working people for corporate money. The "people" being championed are defined primarily in economic terms: working people, the ninety-nine percent, those who have been left behind by an economic system that works for the few at the expense of the many.

Right populism targets a different elite: a cultural and cosmopolitan elite that is described as out of touch with ordinary values, contemptuous of traditional ways of life, more loyal to international institutions and global flows than to the nation and its people. This elite includes academics, journalists, NGO workers, international civil servants and what Orban calls "the Soros network" -- a deliberately vague reference that serves to organize diffuse resentments around a specific symbolic target. The "people" in right populism are typically defined in ethnocultural terms: the native population, the "real" Americans or Hungarians or Brazilians, as opposed to immigrants, cosmopolitan elites and their allies.

This difference in target and constituency explains why left and right populism have such different implications for democratic governance. Left populism, when it wins, tends to expand the formal political inclusion of previously excluded groups and challenge economic elites. Right populism, when it wins, tends to contract political inclusion (particularly for minority groups) and challenge cultural and political elites while often protecting or expanding the privileges of economic elites. These are not symmetrical phenomena even though they share the same basic populist logic. The risks to democratic institutions from right populism have proved significantly larger in practice, as the comparative record of the last two decades demonstrates.

The Charisma Problem and the Structural Problem

A common mistake in analyzing populist success is to over-attribute it to the personal charisma or media savvy of individual leaders. Trump's reality television experience, Orban's calculated manipulation of Hungarian media, Bolsonaro's provocateur social media presence -- these are real factors in their success but they are not the primary explanation. The structural conditions that produce populist demand predate and outlast any individual leader. If Trump had not run in 2016, the conditions that made his victory possible would still have been present. Someone else would eventually have activated them.

This matters for thinking about how to respond to populism. Focusing primarily on defeating individual populist leaders, while often necessary, does not address the conditions that produce populist demand. Beating Trump did not eliminate the representation deficit, the status anxiety, the elite credibility collapse or the cultural dismissal that his support drew upon. Beating Corbyn or Sanders in party primaries did not make center-left parties more credibly representative of working-class voters. The structural conditions have to be addressed directly, which requires mainstream parties and institutions to grapple honestly with what they have failed to deliver and who they have failed to represent.

"The 'it's all about charisma' explanation is comfortable because it means you just have to find the right counter-charismatic leader. The structural explanation is uncomfortable because it means you have to actually change what your party stands for and who it serves. Guess which explanation establishment parties prefer." r/PoliticalScience · 10,293 upvotes

Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart's "Cultural Backlash" proposes one of the more comprehensive frameworks: they argue that populism represents a backlash against the long-term value shift toward post-material, post-traditional values documented in the World Values Survey. As societies become more educated and economically secure, younger cohorts reliably shift toward more individualist, expressive and cosmopolitan values. But this shift is not uniform, and the older and less-educated portions of the population who haven't made this shift increasingly find themselves living in a culture whose official values feel alien and whose institutions treat their preferences as embarrassing relics. Right-wing populism is the political expression of that backlash. Crucially, Norris and Inglehart argue the backlash is losing in the long run -- demography favors the post-material shift -- but losing slowly enough that it will continue to produce significant political disruption for decades.

What Populism Gets Right and Gets Wrong

Intellectual honesty about populism requires acknowledging what it gets right. Populism is correct that many democratic systems have developed significant representation deficits. It is correct that elites frequently protect their own interests at public expense and face less accountability than ordinary citizens for doing so. It is correct that technocratic governance has removed important decisions from democratic accountability in ways that concentrate power among credentialed experts while reducing democratic agency. It is correct that mainstream parties in many countries have failed large portions of their traditional constituencies. These are real problems, not projections or delusions.

What populism characteristically gets wrong is the proposed solution. The people-versus-elite frame is inherently simplifying in ways that misdiagnose complex problems. Most policy challenges don't have solutions that a unified "people" can simply will into existence against corrupt obstruction. Economic inequality, climate change, public health, housing affordability and technological disruption are genuinely difficult problems that resist simple solutions and require complex governance involving technical expertise, negotiation among competing interests and tolerance for uncertainty and tradeoffs. Populism's hostility to these complexities -- its tendency to explain failure as elite betrayal rather than genuine difficulty -- leads to governance that substitutes scapegoating for problem-solving.

The enemy identification logic is also dangerous. Once politics is framed as a war between the pure people and the corrupt elite, the definition of who counts as "the people" becomes politically consequential in ways that almost always end in the exclusion of some group. Ethnic minorities, immigrants, intellectuals, cosmopolitans, the educated class -- each of these groups has served as the external enemy in various populist movements, and the logic that justifies excluding them from the category of "real" citizens is corrosive to democratic pluralism.

"The tragedy of populism is that it correctly identifies real problems -- the representation deficit, elite self-dealing, the capture of institutions -- and then proposes solutions that make all of those problems worse. A strong man who says he's fighting for you is still a strong man who has concentrated power. The cure is the disease in a different form." r/Democracy · 11,739 upvotes

Cas Mudde's conclusion is that the appropriate response to populism is neither dismissal nor accommodation but what he calls "liberal democratic response": taking the legitimate grievances seriously while defending the procedural and rights-based protections that populism threatens. This means genuinely addressing representation deficits, reducing the distance between governing elites and ordinary citizens, making democratic governance more responsive and less technocratic where that is possible without sacrificing rights and rule of law, and reforming institutions that have demonstrably failed those they are supposed to serve. It does not mean abandoning minority rights, abandoning institutional checks on power, or pretending that simple solutions exist for complex problems. The line between these responses is not always easy to walk, but walking it is the work that liberal democracies need to do if they are going to remain viable in conditions that are clearly producing sustained populist pressure.

The fact that populism keeps winning is not a mystery if you understand what it is responding to. It is a diagnostic signal from populations that feel unseen, unrepresented and betrayed. The signal contains real information. The question is whether the institutions and parties that constitute mainstream democratic governance are capable of receiving that information honestly and acting on it, or whether they will continue to explain away each populist victory as an aberration caused by charisma, propaganda or voter irrationality. The second response has not worked for twenty years. There is no particular reason to think it will start working now.
