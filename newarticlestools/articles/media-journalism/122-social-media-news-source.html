Social Media as a News Source: What We Gain and What We Lose

46,183 upvotes · by u/feed_reader · 12 min read
▲ 46,183 people found this relatable
Advertisement

In 2023, Pew Research Center found that roughly half of American adults reported getting news from social media at least sometimes, with about a quarter saying they get news there often. Among adults under thirty, the proportions are even higher. The platforms that most people use as their primary window onto current events are not news organizations. They are advertising platforms that have discovered news to be a highly effective category of content for generating engagement.

This shift is widely acknowledged and widely lamented, but the critique of social media as a news source is frequently conducted at a level of abstraction that obscures what is actually happening. "Filter bubbles," "echo chambers," "misinformation" and "fake news" are phrases invoked so routinely that they have lost much of their explanatory power. A more granular account of what social media does and doesn't do as an information environment helps clarify both the genuine problems and the ways in which the standard critique is incomplete or wrong.

The Gatekeeping Function and What Replaced It

Traditional journalism operated a gatekeeping function that has been misunderstood in both its defenses and its critiques. Editors, publishers and broadcast executives decided what was and wasn't news: which stories merited coverage, how prominently, with what framing and what level of verification. This gatekeeping was imperfect in ways that are well documented. It was systematically biased toward the concerns and perspectives of the mostly white, mostly male, mostly college-educated, mostly upper-middle-class people who ran news organizations. It excluded voices, communities and events that fell outside those frameworks. The critique of traditional media gatekeeping is substantially valid.

But the gatekeeping function also did things that social media does not do. It provided some minimal quality filter: stories that went through an editorial process were, on average, more verified, more contextualized and more carefully worded than the unmediated content that now competes with them for attention. It provided a shared agenda: people who read the same newspaper or watched the same news broadcast had a common set of stories and frames, which made civic discourse possible in ways that require some minimum of shared reality. And it provided the epistemic authority of professional accountability, which is imperfect but real: a journalist who gets things wrong consistently loses their job, and a publication that gets things wrong consistently loses its audience.

Social media replaced gatekeeping not with better gatekeeping but with algorithmic curation by engagement maximization. The new gate is not editorial judgment but the revealed preferences of millions of users, which consistently reward emotional intensity, novelty, moral clarity and conflict over accuracy, nuance, complexity and context. This is not a neutral replacement. It is a replacement with specific biases that produce specific distortions.

Not from fringe sources — from major outlets whose stories had been shared in ways that stripped context. The headline that got shared was often genuinely misleading even when the article it linked to was accurate. I was forming opinions based on the shareable version of events, not the actual events." — r/news, 41,738 upvotes

The Filter Bubble: What the Research Actually Shows

Eli Pariser's 2011 book "The Filter Bubble" popularized the idea that algorithmic personalization was creating information silos in which people only encountered content that confirmed their existing views, never confronting challenging information or perspectives. This thesis became enormously influential and is still widely cited. But the research that has followed has produced a considerably more complicated picture.

Eytan Bakshy and colleagues at Facebook published a 2015 paper in Science that found that the Facebook News Feed algorithm did reduce exposure to cross-cutting political content, but by a smaller margin than human choice did. People chose to engage with content that confirmed their views much more aggressively than the algorithm selected for it. The filter bubble, in other words, was partly real but considerably smaller than commonly assumed, and the human contribution to it was larger than the algorithmic contribution.

More recent research by researchers including Chris Bail has complicated the filter bubble thesis further. Bail's 2018 study found that exposing people to opposing political views on social media via a bot actually increased political polarization rather than reducing it. People who were shown more cross-cutting content became more extreme, not less, because the content they encountered from the other side was typically the most shareable content, which was also typically the most extreme and inflammatory. The cure for the filter bubble, it turned out, was not obviously better than the disease.

What the filter bubble research suggests is not that algorithmic curation has no effect on exposure diversity but that the relationship between exposure diversity and epistemic quality is not straightforward. Exposure to more diverse content does not automatically produce better-informed citizens if the content encountered is disinformation, extreme rhetoric or emotionally manipulative framing. What matters is not just the range of content encountered but its quality, and quality is precisely what engagement-optimization algorithms are not selected to provide.

The Misinformation Problem: Scale and Velocity

The most serious epistemic problem with social media as a news source is not filter bubbles but misinformation at scale. The MIT Media Lab's Soroush Vosoughi and colleagues published a landmark 2018 study in Science analyzing 126,000 news stories shared on Twitter from 2006 to 2017. Their findings were striking: false news spread faster, reached more people and penetrated farther into social networks than true news did, by a substantial margin. True news stories took about six times as long as false ones to reach 1,500 people. False stories were 70 percent more likely to be retweeted than true ones.

The mechanism, the researchers concluded, was not primarily bot behavior but human behavior. People shared false news more because it was more novel and more emotionally arousing than true news, which is on average more mundane. Falsehoods that confirm fears, validate grievances or offer dramatic revelations spread because sharing them satisfies emotional needs that accurate but boring information doesn't. The social media architecture that rewards novelty and emotional arousal systematically advantages misinformation over accurate reporting.

The consequences during high-stakes events have been well documented. During the COVID-19 pandemic, health misinformation spread through social media so rapidly and extensively that the WHO used the term "infodemic" to describe the parallel crisis. During the 2020 US election, false claims about voting procedures, ballot security and election administration reached tens of millions of people, with measurable effects on beliefs about election integrity that persisted long after the claims had been debunked. The debunking itself, which received much less engagement than the original false claims, barely registered in most users' feeds.

The Speed Problem and the Context Collapse

Social media news operates at a speed that is fundamentally incompatible with the verification processes that produce accurate journalism. Traditional news organizations developed their verification standards partly in response to the competitive pressure of not wanting to be scooped, but also in recognition that being wrong is expensive: it damages reputation, creates legal exposure and reduces the trust that makes journalism valuable. These incentives have been significantly weakened in social media news distribution.

The person who posts first, regardless of accuracy, captures the initial audience. Corrections, if they come, reach a fraction of the people who saw the original. The first version of a story is the one that forms most people's understanding of the event, and the first version circulating on social media is often the least accurate because the verified, contextualized version takes longer to produce. This creates a systematic bias in social media news toward wrong first impressions over corrected later understandings.

Context collapse is a related but distinct problem. Social media strips context from content systematically. A clip of a speech shared without the surrounding speech, a statistic shared without methodology or comparison context, a quote shared without the conversation it emerged from, a photo shared without the circumstances in which it was taken: these partial pieces of information are not simply less informative than the full context. They are often actively misleading because the partial version implies a meaning that the full version doesn't support. The sharing incentives of social media reward the shareable fragment over the complete picture, producing an information environment in which comprehensible fragments substitute for accurate understanding.

"I've started trying to read the original source before I share anything. Not the tweet about the study or the article about the tweet. The actual study. What I found is that the study usually says something much more qualified and uncertain than the tweet implies. 'Researchers find possible link between X and Y in small observational study' becomes 'Scientists prove X causes Y.' I've mostly stopped sharing news because I can't trust my own ability to know what's actually being said." — r/skeptic, 36,294 upvotes

What Social Media Does Well as a News Environment

The case against social media as a primary news source is strong, but fairness requires acknowledging what it does well, because the alternatives are not obviously better in every dimension. Social media has broken stories that traditional journalism missed or actively suppressed. The Arab Spring demonstrations, the spread of mobile footage of police violence and the #MeToo movement all traveled primarily through social media networks before reaching traditional journalism, and in each case the social media distribution performed a genuine democratic function that institutional journalism had failed to perform.

Social media also provides access to primary sources that traditional journalism intermediates. A citizen who can follow scientists, government officials, business leaders and local officials directly on Twitter or LinkedIn has access to information that previously passed through editorial filters before reaching them. Whether the information is accurate and well-contextualized depends on the source, but the ability to encounter primary sources directly rather than only through reporters' interpretations has real value for people with the skills to evaluate what they're reading.

The diversity of voices that social media enables is also a genuine gain. Perspectives, communities and experiences that traditional journalism systematically underrepresented now have platforms through which they can reach audiences. The ability of Black Twitter to shape coverage of racial justice issues, the distribution of LGBTQ+ voices and experiences through social networks, the reach of disability community reporting on policy that affects them: these are real expansions of the information environment that matter for people who were previously excluded from it.

Navigating the Current Landscape

Given that social media is where most people get news and that this is unlikely to change dramatically in the near term, the practical question is how to consume it in ways that produce better rather than worse understanding. The research on this suggests a few consistent principles. Treating social media as a notification system rather than an information system, using it to discover that stories exist and then following up with longer, more substantively reported coverage, produces better-informed consumers than using it as a primary source. Actively seeking out primary sources rather than coverage of primary sources reduces the distortion introduced by intermediaries. Pausing before sharing, specifically asking whether the claim being shared has been verified and whether the framing reflects the underlying reality, reduces participation in misinformation spread.

None of this addresses the structural problem, which is that most people don't and won't engage in deliberate epistemic hygiene in their daily information consumption, any more than most people count calories carefully every day. The information environment shapes behavior largely through defaults, and the defaults of social media news consumption are set by engagement maximization, not by accuracy optimization. Changing those defaults, whether through regulation, platform design choices, algorithmic transparency requirements or cultural norms, remains the essential problem that individual media literacy cannot solve at scale.
