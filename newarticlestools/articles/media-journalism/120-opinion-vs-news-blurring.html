When Opinion Became the News

43,918 upvotes · by u/news_analysis · 12 min read
▲ 43,918 people found this relatable

There is a distinction that journalism schools teach and that most working journalists understand in principle: the difference between a news story, which reports what happened, and an opinion piece, which argues what it means or what should be done about it. This distinction has never been perfectly maintained in practice. Reporters choose which stories to cover, which sources to interview, which facts to include and which to omit, and all of those choices reflect judgments that are not purely factual. The pretense of perfect objectivity was always somewhat fictitious.

But there is a meaningful difference between the imperfect objectivity of traditional reporting and the systematic blurring of news and opinion that characterizes much contemporary journalism. And there is growing evidence, both from public opinion research and from analysis of how media coverage has changed, that this blurring is not simply a matter of perception shaped by political bias but a real and measurable transformation in what mainstream news organizations actually produce.

How Pew Research Quantified the Blur

The Pew Research Center has conducted several studies attempting to measure the news-opinion distinction empirically, and their findings are striking. A 2020 study asked Americans to sort fifty news statements into "factual" and "opinion" categories. Only 26 percent of Americans correctly identified all five of the opinion statements as opinion. Among those who scored high on political awareness and digital news savvy, performance was better but still far from perfect. The study found that partisan identity was a strong predictor of how people classified statements: people were far more likely to classify a statement as factual when it aligned with their political views and as opinion when it didn't, regardless of the statement's actual epistemic status.

A different Pew study published in 2022 analyzed content on major news websites and found that a significant and growing proportion of content on the news pages (not opinion sections) of major outlets contained interpretive and evaluative language previously associated with opinion journalism. Words that indicate the author's assessment rather than simply reporting events, including descriptors like "failed," "promised" and "claimed" versus "said," appeared at substantially higher rates in recent years than in equivalent coverage from a decade earlier. The boundary between the reporter's voice and the story's facts had moved.

"I used to be able to tell the difference between a news article and an opinion piece just by reading the first paragraph. Now I often can't. The news article tells me what I should think about what happened. The opinion piece tells me what I should think about what happened. The only difference is where on the website they appear and whether there's an author photo." — r/news, 38,241 upvotes

Cable News and the Opinion-Entertainment Hybrid

Television journalism underwent the most dramatic version of this transformation, and it is worth tracing the specific mechanism. When CNN launched the twenty-four-hour news cycle in 1980, it faced an immediate problem: there was not enough news happening at any given moment to fill twenty-four hours of original reporting. The solution was discussion, analysis, punditry and eventually the formal interview and debate format that came to dominate cable news programming. These formats are not journalism in any traditional sense. They are performance, in which guests argue positions and hosts either facilitate or participate in the argument.

The audience learned to consume these formats as news. The person appearing on CNN with a chyron identifying their affiliation was processed as an authoritative information source, regardless of whether they were reporting facts, offering analysis or simply advocating a political position. The authority signals of the news broadcast, the serious demeanor, the graphics, the studio setting, the framing as current events, transferred to content that had little in common with the original meaning of news.

Fox News extended this logic deliberately and profitably. Its prime time lineup, which consistently dominated cable news ratings for years, was almost entirely opinion and advocacy programming. Tucker Carlson, Sean Hannity and Laura Ingraham were not reporters. They were commentators, and in some cases active political operatives. Yet they appeared on a network called "Fox News" with all the authority signals of news broadcasting, and surveys consistently found that their viewers trusted their content as news to a higher degree than viewers of more explicitly news-oriented programming.

The legal dimension of this is illuminating. In the defamation lawsuit brought by Karen McDougal against Tucker Carlson's show, Fox News's lawyers successfully argued that Carlson's statements could not be defamatory because "any reasonable viewer" would understand that Carlson was offering opinion rather than reporting facts. The judge accepted this argument. The network that had built its brand on being a trusted news source successfully defended itself against a defamation claim by arguing that no reasonable person would trust its prime time content as factual. Both things were simultaneously true: the audience treated the content as news and the network was legally correct that it was opinion.

The Analysis Explosion in Print and Digital Journalism

Opinion-news blurring is not confined to cable television or explicitly partisan outlets. The proliferation of "analysis" pieces in mainstream print and digital journalism represents a more subtle version of the same shift. Analysis is a legitimate journalistic form: it uses reporting to provide context and interpretation beyond what a straight news story can offer. But the line between analysis and opinion is narrow, and the incentives of digital journalism push consistently toward crossing it.

Analysis generates more engagement than straight news. An article explaining what an event means, who won, what the implications are, provides something a bare recitation of facts doesn't: a framework for understanding, a conclusion to agree or disagree with and an emotional response. These are the things that generate clicks, shares and comments. An analysis piece headlined "What Biden's speech reveals about the future of the Democratic Party" will consistently outperform a news piece headlined "Biden delivers speech to Congress." Publishers know this and have responded by dramatically increasing the proportion of their content that falls into the analysis-opinion spectrum.

The New York Times provides an instructive case. Its "news analysis" label covers an enormous range of content, from genuinely analytical pieces that synthesize reporting and context to pieces that are essentially editorials labeled as analysis. The Washington Post's "Perspective" section occupies similar contested territory. Neither publication is doing anything unusual; they are doing what digital media economics reward. But the cumulative effect on readers is that the distinction between what the paper knows and what the paper thinks is consistently obscured.

"I've had the experience of reading what I thought was a news story about a policy debate, getting to the end and realizing I had been told what to conclude rather than given information to form my own view. The story had made its choice of sources, its selection of details and its framing all point in one direction, but because it was labeled 'news' rather than 'opinion' I'd been reading it as if it were neutral. The label really does change how I process the information." — r/media_criticism, 24,837 upvotes

Social Media and the Collapse of Editorial Distance

The rise of journalist Twitter (now journalist X) introduced a new mechanism for opinion-news blurring: the same journalist who maintains formal neutrality in their published work expresses strong personal opinions constantly in their social media presence. Readers follow journalists on social media and form accurate impressions of their political views, then return to read their news coverage with that context fully activated. The "view from nowhere" pose of the news article becomes harder to maintain when the journalist's voice is fully visible two clicks away.

This creates a genuine dilemma. One argument is that the traditional pretense of journalist objectivity was always fictitious, and that journalists expressing their views openly is more honest than disguising them. A reader who knows a reporter's priors can discount accordingly; a reader who believes a reporter has no priors cannot. On this view, journalist social media transparency is epistemically better than the false objectivity it replaces.

The counterargument is that transparency about individual bias does not solve the structural problem. Editors who see that their reporters have strong political views on a story they're covering should assign different reporters. Publications that employ reporters whose social media presence is essentially partisan advocacy should rethink whether those reporters can produce journalism that serves audiences who don't share their politics. The solution to bad objectivity norms is better objectivity norms, not the abandonment of the project of neutrality entirely.

What seems clear from research on audience perception is that journalists' social media behavior damages credibility across the political spectrum, including among audiences who share the journalist's politics. People who want news want something that feels like information rather than advocacy, and journalists who constantly advocate on social media undermine the authority that makes their reporting feel like news rather than opinion. This is a self-defeating dynamic that the journalism profession has been slow to address.

The "View from Somewhere" and Its Legitimate Uses

Not all opinion-journalism blurring is problematic. There is a tradition of journalism that does not pretend to be objective but is nonetheless honest and valuable. Gonzo journalism, first-person narrative journalism, advocacy journalism in the service of documented injustice, international reporting from conflict zones where formal neutrality between aggressors and victims would be a moral failure: these are all forms that mix the reporter's presence and perspective with factual reporting. They can be excellent journalism even though they cannot be called neutral.

The problem is not that perspective and reporting can coexist. It is that the blurring of the two has happened in ways that are not always clearly labeled, that serve commercial rather than journalistic purposes and that leave audiences unable to calibrate appropriately. When a news article presents a contested political judgment as an established fact, when an analysis piece works backward from a predetermined conclusion to selectively presented evidence or when a news outlet's "news" section systematically frames stories in ways that serve a political audience, the blurring is doing harm.

The solution is not to ban analysis, eliminate perspective or return to a mythologized golden age of pure objectivity. It is to be honest about what different kinds of journalism are doing, to label them clearly, to hold them to the appropriate standards for their type and to develop in readers the skills to navigate content that falls across the news-opinion spectrum. That last point, media literacy, turns out to be the only durable solution, because the incentive pressures that produced the blurring are not going away.

What Readers Actually Want and What They Need

Survey research on audience preferences produces a consistent finding that is also a consistent puzzle: audiences say they want straight news and believe reporters should not express personal views, but they engage more with opinionated content. The preference stated in surveys and the behavior revealed by clicks are in systematic conflict. This conflict is not evidence of hypocrisy but of a genuine ambivalence: people want information they can trust and they also want to have their existing views confirmed and their enemies condemned, and these two desires are in tension.

The commercial media system has resolved this tension by giving audiences what they engage with rather than what they say they want, because engagement is what generates revenue. This is a reasonable business decision and a disastrous civic one. A democracy depends on citizens who can form views based on accurate information, evaluate competing claims against evidence and update their positions when the evidence warrants it. A media system that consistently gives citizens confirmation and condemnation instead of information and evidence is producing people less capable of doing these things, and producing them in large numbers.

The long-term consequences of a public that cannot distinguish news from opinion, or that has given up trying, extend well beyond media criticism. Political decisions made on the basis of opinion-as-news, foreign policy judgments formed by partisan advocacy disguised as reporting, economic choices shaped by outrage coverage of trivial events rather than accurate information about genuine economic conditions: these are real governance problems with real consequences. The blurring of news and opinion is not an aesthetic complaint about journalistic standards. It is a structural problem with democratic self-governance that deserves to be taken as seriously as it is.
