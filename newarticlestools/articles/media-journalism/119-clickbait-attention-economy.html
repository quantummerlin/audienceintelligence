The Clickbait Economy and What It Does to Us

48,726 upvotes · by u/attention_deficit · 12 min read
▲ 48,726 people found this relatable
Advertisement

The term "clickbait" has become so ubiquitous that it has lost most of its specificity. People use it to describe any headline they find annoying, any article that disappointed their expectations or any media outlet whose editorial politics they disagree with. But there is a more precise and more useful definition: clickbait is content engineered to maximize the probability of a click regardless of whether clicking delivers value to the person who clicked. The gap between what a headline promises and what the content delivers is the defining feature, and that gap is not accidental. It is a deliberate product of how digital media economics work.

Understanding the clickbait economy requires understanding what it replaced and why the replacement happened so quickly. It also requires taking seriously the downstream consequences for how we think, what we know and what kind of public discourse is possible when the information environment is structured around engagement maximization rather than anything resembling the democratic ideals that journalism schools still teach.

The Promise Economy: How Headlines Learned to Lie

Traditional print journalism had a different relationship with its headlines. A headline in a newspaper was not trying to make you click; it was trying to summarize what an article contained so that you could decide whether you wanted to read it. The relationship between headline and content was representational: the headline stood for the article. Misleading headlines existed, but they were journalistic failures rather than features.

Digital publishing economics changed this relationship structurally. When revenue depends on pageviews, the metric that determines whether a piece of content succeeded is whether someone clicked on it. Whether they found it valuable after clicking is economically irrelevant; most digital advertising is served on page load, not on reader engagement. A reader who clicks, spends three seconds on the page and leaves disgusted generates the same revenue as a reader who clicks, spends twenty minutes and learns something. This economic structure does not incentivize content quality. It incentivizes click probability.

The optimization of click probability is a solved problem for anyone who pays attention to what generates engagement. Headlines that trigger curiosity gaps perform better than headlines that summarize. "You won't believe what happened next" outperforms "What happened next." Headlines that trigger negative emotions, particularly anger and anxiety, outperform headlines that trigger neutral or positive ones. Headlines that promise revelation ("The secret the X industry doesn't want you to know") outperform headlines that promise information ("How X industry works"). Personal address ("This is what your Y habit is doing to your brain") outperforms general framing ("What habits do to brain function").

"I worked at a digital media outlet for three years. We A/B tested headlines constantly. The results were consistent and depressing. The more the headline exaggerated the significance of the story, the more it got clicked. We knew the story didn't deliver what the headline promised. We did it anyway because the numbers demanded it. I quit partly because of that." — r/journalism, 29,847 upvotes

The A/B testing culture in digital media industrialized what had previously been an editorial judgment into an automated optimization process. Instead of a human editor deciding which headline better represented the story, algorithms tested multiple versions against live audiences and promoted whichever generated more clicks. The human judgment was replaced by revealed preference data, and revealed preference in this context consistently rewards manipulation over accuracy.

The Curiosity Gap and How It Was Weaponized

The psychological mechanism that clickbait exploits most consistently is what George Loewenstein described in his 1994 paper "The Psychology of Curiosity" as the information gap. Curiosity, Loewenstein argued, arises when we become aware of a gap between what we know and what we want to know. The awareness of not-knowing is itself uncomfortable, and the relief of that discomfort is the reward that curiosity-satisfying information provides.

Clickbait headlines engineer the awareness of not-knowing without necessarily providing the relief. "The one thing doctors don't tell you about" creates an information gap: there is something I don't know that I should know. Clicking is the necessary step toward closing that gap. But the article on the other side frequently doesn't close it, either because the "one thing" turns out to be trivial, already known or simply vague enough to be impossible to verify as genuinely informative. The headline borrowed against a psychological debt that the content couldn't pay.

Upworthy, which launched in 2012, operationalized this principle into a systematic method. Co-founder Eli Pariser later became famous for warning about filter bubbles, but Upworthy's content strategy in its early years was a masterclass in curiosity gap manipulation. Their internal guidelines, which leaked, advised writers to find the "curiosity gap sweet spot" and to "make a promise" in the headline that "the article will emotionally and intellectually pay off." The problem was that the metrics measuring success captured the click, not the payoff. Headlines that promised more than the content could deliver still counted as successes by the only metric that mattered commercially.

Outrage as a Business Model

If curiosity gaps explain the structure of many clickbait headlines, outrage explains much of the content. Research consistently finds that negative emotions, and anger in particular, generate more online engagement than positive or neutral emotions. A 2017 study by William Brady and colleagues published in Nature Human Behaviour analyzed 563,000 tweets and found that each additional moral-emotional word in a tweet increased its retweet rate by approximately 20 percent. Words that trigger moral condemnation travel faster and farther than words that don't.

The outrage economy extends well beyond Twitter. Media outlets covering politics have discovered that framing coverage around conflict, threat and moral violation produces more engagement than covering governance, policy processes or political complexity. "Democrats and Republicans disagree about budget" generates fewer clicks than "Republicans are trying to destroy X" or "Democrats just revealed their plan to end Y." The conflict frame, the threat frame and the revelation frame consistently outperform the information frame.

The consequences for political discourse are significant. Citizens who consume primarily outrage-optimized political media develop a systematically distorted picture of what politics actually is: a continuous emergency, a perpetual battle between forces of good and evil, a series of revelations about the hidden intentions of political opponents. This picture is not simply inaccurate in its details. It is inaccurate in its fundamental structure. Most of political governance is bureaucratic, technical, slow and boring, and the vast majority of policy decisions involve genuine tradeoffs between competing goods rather than clear choices between virtue and corruption. An outrage-optimized media cannot represent this reality because it cannot generate engagement from this reality.

"I realized I didn't actually know anything about how policy got made. I knew who to be angry at. I knew the villains. I could tell you all about the political drama. But I couldn't explain how a bill actually becomes law in any practical detail, what federal agencies actually do, or why the things I supposedly cared about weren't changing despite decades of attention. The outrage machine had replaced actual political understanding." — r/PoliticalDiscussion, 44,182 upvotes

The Aggregation Problem and Original Reporting

One of the less visible consequences of the digital publishing economy is the dramatic decline in original reporting relative to aggregation, commentary and curation. Producing original journalism is expensive. It requires reporters with expertise, time to develop sources and conduct interviews, editors to verify facts and legal counsel when stories are contested. Aggregating, summarizing and commenting on journalism produced by someone else is much cheaper.

The economic structure of digital media strongly favors the cheaper option. An aggregated summary of someone else's scoop generates almost as many clicks as the scoop itself, because most readers don't know or care who did the original reporting. The outlet that invests in investigative journalism sees a fraction of the traffic benefit that the original investment would justify, because dozens of aggregators capture much of the audience for the story without bearing any of the cost.

The result is a news ecosystem increasingly parasitic on the original reporting of a declining number of institutions that can still afford to do it. The New York Times, Washington Post, Wall Street Journal and a handful of other well-funded outlets still produce significant original journalism. Thousands of websites profit from the traffic those stories generate without contributing to the underlying work. Local newspapers and mid-size regional outlets, which once provided most original reporting on local government, courts and civic affairs, have been particularly devastated, losing both advertising revenue and the audience that followed the best stories to national platforms.

The Physiological Cost

Beyond its effects on information quality and political discourse, the clickbait economy has measurable effects on the people who consume it. A media environment structured around anxiety and outrage triggers physiological stress responses that are not well-suited to the context. The amygdala's threat detection system, which evolved to respond to immediate physical dangers, does not distinguish between a headline announcing a predator near your shelter and a headline announcing a political threat three thousand miles away. Both produce cortisol release, elevated heart rate and the cognitive narrowing associated with threat response.

Chronic activation of this system, which is what daily consumption of outrage-optimized media produces, has documented effects on cognitive function. Threat response narrows attention and prioritizes pattern matching for familiar dangers over careful analysis of novel information. It reduces tolerance for ambiguity and nuance. It makes binary thinking feel more natural and complex thinking feel effortful. These are not the cognitive states that support good political judgment, careful evaluation of evidence or productive engagement with people who have different views.

Research by Britt Wray and others on "doom scrolling" behavior documents something most heavy news consumers recognize: the compulsive consumption of negative content despite the fact that it generates distress rather than relief. The stress of not-knowing whether things are worse than you thought provides a psychological pull toward more consumption, and outrage-optimized media is specifically designed to maintain this state of incomplete anxiety by always producing the next alarming thing before the current one can be fully processed.

Platform Amplification and the Distribution Layer

Individual media outlets did not create the clickbait economy alone. Social media platforms amplified and entrenched it by making algorithmic distribution the primary mechanism through which content reached audiences. When Facebook's News Feed algorithm prioritized content that generated high engagement, and when engagement was measured primarily by likes, shares and comments, the selection pressure on publishers was immediate and severe: produce content that generates these responses or lose distribution.

Facebook's algorithm changes between 2015 and 2018 were particularly consequential. The platform's shift toward "meaningful social interactions" in 2018, which deprioritized passive content consumption in favor of content that generated comments and shares, accelerated the outrage optimization of political content. Comments are generated by content that provokes strong feelings, and strong feelings in political contexts tend to be negative ones. Publishers who adapted to this incentive produced more provocative content; publishers who didn't adapt lost traffic.

The platform's own internal research, portions of which became public through various leaks and regulatory proceedings, acknowledged the connection between engagement optimization and the spread of divisive content. A 2018 internal presentation, reported by the Wall Street Journal, noted that the platform's algorithms specifically amplified content that produced strong emotional reactions, and that this amplification was making the platform's content more divisive and extreme over time. The response was not to change the algorithm fundamentally but to make incremental adjustments that reduced the most obvious harms while preserving the core engagement model.

"The thing I keep coming back to is that no one set out to destroy the information environment. Every individual decision made sense from the inside. Publishers optimized for clicks because clicks paid the bills. Platforms optimized for engagement because engagement drove advertising. Readers clicked on the things that felt most emotionally significant. The outcome is the product of perfectly rational individual decisions that are collectively irrational. That makes it very hard to fix." — r/tech, 33,614 upvotes

Slow News and Alternatives

Various responses to the clickbait economy have emerged, most of them operating at the margins. The "slow journalism" movement, associated with publications like De Correspondent in the Netherlands and Delayed Gratification in the UK, explicitly rejects the continuous news cycle in favor of longer-form, more thoroughly reported pieces published on longer timescales. These outlets have demonstrated that audiences will pay for quality when the alternative is clearly worse, but their reach is limited and their business models depend on reader subscriptions that require deliberate effort to maintain.

Newsletter journalism, which grew dramatically between 2018 and 2023, represents another partial response. Writers who publish directly to subscriber email lists can bypass the platform distribution layer entirely, eliminating the engagement optimization pressure that shapes social media content. The newsletter economy has produced some excellent journalism and significant income for individual writers, but it has also produced an enormous amount of content of highly variable quality, and it has done nothing to address the problem of scale: the people who seek out quality newsletters are already motivated news consumers, and the people most harmed by the clickbait economy are those who are not.

The structural problem remains that good journalism and clickbait journalism compete for the same limited attention, and clickbait wins in unregulated competition because it is optimized for competition rather than quality. Interventions that have shown modest success in other attention-economy contexts, ranging from algorithmic transparency requirements to digital literacy education to different default platform settings, have been inadequately implemented in the journalism context. Until the business model that rewards click generation without regard to reader value is substantially reformed, the incentive to produce clickbait will remain overwhelming for most publishers, regardless of their stated editorial principles.
