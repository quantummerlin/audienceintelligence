Why Nobody Trusts the News Anymore

61,384 upvotes · by u/skeptical_reader · 13 min read
▲ 61,384 people found this relatable
Advertisement

In 2023, Gallup reported that trust in newspapers had fallen to 18 percent and trust in television news had fallen to 14 percent, both all-time lows in surveys stretching back to the 1970s. To put those numbers in context: more Americans expressed confidence in the criminal justice system, which has itself been the subject of sustained credibility crises, than in the news media. Among Republicans the numbers were even more dramatic, with television news trust at 5 percent. But the collapse is not simply a partisan phenomenon. Trust has declined among Democrats too, from 70 percent in 2016 to 58 percent in 2023, and among independents it has been in free fall for the better part of two decades.

The standard explanation, offered most enthusiastically by those who benefit from it, is that media distrust is the product of political manipulation. Conservatives, the argument goes, have spent decades attacking journalists as enemies of the people, and those attacks have been effective. There is something to this. Research by Jonathan Ladd documents a meaningful relationship between elite partisan cues and news trust decline. But it does not explain the breadth of the decline across political groups, the international dimension of the phenomenon (trust in media is declining in most Western democracies regardless of their specific political histories) or the ways in which the media's own behavior has contributed to the problem it is now experiencing.

The Long History of a Short Collapse

American journalism has always had critics and has never enjoyed universal trust. The yellow journalism of the late nineteenth century, the propaganda participation of World War One and Two, the McCarthy-era timidity of many mainstream outlets, the cheerleading for the Vietnam War that finally cracked in the late 1960s: media credibility has a complicated history. What Gallup's data shows is not that trust was high and then fell but that a period of relatively elevated trust, roughly the 1960s through the mid-1970s, was followed by a long gradual decline that accelerated sharply in the 1990s and again after 2015.

The Watergate era, paradoxically, represents the high point of public trust in journalism. The Washington Post's exposure of the Nixon administration's crimes seemed to confirm that an adversarial press was essential to democratic accountability. Journalism schools saw enrollment surge. Investigative reporting was glamorized in films. The press briefly enjoyed the cultural status of a democratic institution rather than simply a commercial enterprise.

What followed was partly the inevitable regression from a high point and partly the beginning of structural changes that would fundamentally alter the media landscape. Cable news launched in 1980 with CNN and transformed what had been a brief, expensive commodity, network news broadcast, into a continuous product that needed to fill twenty-four hours. That need for constant content began pushing news toward the things that generate engagement rather than the things that serve a democratic public interest. The categories are not mutually exclusive, but they are also not the same.

"I grew up with Walter Cronkite and Edward R. Murrow as the cultural image of what journalists were. Then I watched cable news turn into what it is now and I genuinely don't think those are the same profession anymore. What cable news does is not journalism. It's political performance for an audience that has already decided what it believes." — r/journalism, 34,819 upvotes

The Internet, the Business Model and the Race to the Bottom

The decline of advertising revenue that accompanied the internet's rise is the most consequential structural change in the history of American journalism, and it is still playing out. In 2000, newspaper advertising revenue totaled roughly $48 billion. By 2020 it had fallen to approximately $8.8 billion. That is not a gradual adjustment. It is the effective destruction of the financial model that had sustained local and national journalism for a century.

The mechanism was simple. Classified advertising, which had accounted for roughly 40 percent of newspaper revenue, migrated essentially entirely to Craigslist and then to a constellation of specialized platforms. Display advertising followed digital audiences online and then concentrated on Google and Facebook rather than news publishers. Publishers retained the cost structure of producing news while losing the revenue stream that had financed it.

What replaced the old model was a digital advertising economy based on pageviews and clicks. This created incentives that pointed directly away from the kind of journalism that builds trust. Careful, well-sourced, context-rich reporting on complicated subjects generates fewer clicks than provocative headlines, outrage-inducing framings and coverage of the perpetual drama of political conflict. Editors who understood that their publication's long-term credibility depended on doing the former found themselves under pressure from business-side executives who understood that the publication's short-term survival depended on doing the latter.

The result was a journalistic culture under constant pressure to produce more content faster, with fewer resources and with metrics that consistently rewarded sensationalism over substance. Beat reporters who had developed genuine expertise in specific areas found their positions eliminated in round after round of layoffs as publications shed costly specialists and replaced them with generalist content producers who could turn around five articles a day. The expertise that had made coverage trustworthy was a casualty of the business model transformation.

The Fox Effect and Its Imitators

Roger Ailes launched Fox News in 1996 with the explicit goal of creating a media outlet that would serve the conservative movement's political interests while generating substantial profit. He understood, earlier than most, that the existing network news model was leaving a large and underserved audience on the table. Conservatives who felt that mainstream media had a liberal bias (a perception with some validity in terms of the demographics of working journalists, whatever one thinks about its effect on coverage) had no alternative. Fox News built its initial audience by being that alternative.

What Fox News discovered was that advocacy journalism, in which the outlet's role is not to inform viewers but to confirm their existing beliefs and intensify their political identity, was enormously profitable. Viewers who tune in to have their views validated watch more than viewers who tune in to be informed, because validation is a richer emotional experience than information. Fox's ratings success prompted MSNBC to develop a roughly mirror-image model aimed at liberal viewers. The result was not a balanced marketplace of competing perspectives but a media landscape in which the most commercially successful operations were the ones least committed to informing their audiences.

Gabriel Sherman's biography of Roger Ailes and subsequent reporting on Fox's internal operations documented the degree to which editorial decisions were driven by political considerations and audience management rather than news judgment. The network's coverage of the 2020 election, and particularly its internal communications revealed during the Dominion Voting Systems lawsuit, provided extraordinary documentary evidence of executives and hosts privately acknowledging the falsity of claims they were broadcasting publicly. The Dominion settlement of $787.5 million, the largest defamation settlement in American media history, confirmed that Fox had knowingly broadcast false information to its audience.

"What I find interesting is that Fox viewers don't feel betrayed by the Dominion revelations. The internal texts showing hosts knew the election fraud claims were false just don't register the way you'd expect if news consumption were about truth-seeking. It confirms that a significant portion of what we call news consumption is actually identity consumption. You watch Fox because watching Fox is part of who you are." — r/mediaanalysis, 27,463 upvotes

The False Balance Problem and Its Opposite

Mainstream journalism has its own distinct credibility problems, different in kind from Fox's deliberate disinformation but real nonetheless. The doctrine of journalistic objectivity, as it developed in the twentieth century, produced a set of professional practices that have often served truth poorly while appearing to serve it well.

The "both sides" approach to coverage, in which every story presents competing claims as equally credible regardless of whether they are, has probably done more damage to public understanding than any other single journalistic convention. When climate science is presented as a "debate" between climate scientists and industry-funded skeptics, readers are misled about the actual state of evidence. When vaccine safety is covered as a controversy between medical consensus and fringe claims, the framing itself confers false equivalence. Presenting two positions as though they deserve equal epistemic weight when they manifestly don't is not neutral. It is inaccurate.

At the same time, the move away from false balance has sometimes overcorrected into coverage that lacks genuine intellectual rigor and treats the reporter's political priors as editorial judgment. The tendency to frame every story through the lens of power, identity and social harm, which has become more prominent in mainstream newsrooms over the past decade, serves some stories well and others badly. Science coverage that leads with social justice implications rather than scientific methods. Economic stories filtered through moral frameworks that predetermine the conclusion. These are also departures from the epistemic standards that make journalism trustworthy, even though they come from a different political direction than Fox's departures.

The public, which is less sophisticated about these distinctions than media critics but often better at detecting when they're being told what to think rather than given information they can use, has responded by trusting less. This is in some respects a rational response. The press has given people genuine reasons to be skeptical, and the appropriate response to having genuine reasons for skepticism is skepticism.

High-Profile Failures and Their Long Shadows

Specific high-profile journalism failures have provided concrete justification for distrust that extends well beyond the individual cases. The New York Times's promotion of anonymous sourcing about Iraqi weapons of mass destruction, which contributed to public support for an invasion that turned out to be based on false premises, was a catastrophic credibility event whose effects are still visible in polling data. The Rolling Stone story about campus rape that turned out to be based on a fabricated account, published without basic verification, provided opponents of journalism with a vivid example of progressive narrative driving standards. The coverage of the Covington Catholic students, in which nearly every major outlet reported a story that turned out to be essentially wrong based on a decontextualized video clip, demonstrated the failure modes of social-media-driven news judgment.

None of these failures mean that journalism is uniformly bad or that distrust is warranted across the board. The same period produced extraordinary investigative work: the Panama Papers, the Harvey Weinstein reporting, the exposure of the opioid manufacturers' deception, the documentation of abuses at Catholic institutions. But these successes tend not to generate the lasting impressions that failures do, because the failures confirm a pre-existing skepticism that the successes don't dislodge.

Research on how people update their beliefs in response to new information consistently finds asymmetric updating: people readily incorporate information that confirms their existing views and require significantly more evidence to update away from those views. A single dramatic failure sits in memory and shapes ongoing interpretations in ways that a hundred careful successes don't counter. The media has had enough dramatic failures to give this asymmetry significant material to work with.

The Social Media Displacement and What It Costs

When people stop trusting institutional journalism, they don't stop consuming information. They shift to sources that feel more trustworthy, which in practice usually means sources that feel more like them: individual voices on social media, podcasters and YouTube creators who have built personal relationships with their audiences, partisan outlets that confirm existing beliefs. The epistemological consequences of this shift are not well understood but are likely significant.

Reuters Institute's Digital News Report, which surveys news consumption in forty-six countries annually, documents a consistent pattern: declining trust in mainstream news, rising reliance on social media for news, and high rates of news avoidance (choosing not to engage with news because it is unpleasant, confusing or untrustworthy). In 2023, 36 percent of respondents reported actively avoiding news sometimes or often, up from 29 percent in 2017. News avoidance is highest among younger adults and tends to concentrate in countries where political polarization and media polarization are most advanced.

The alternative information ecosystem that has grown to fill the space vacated by trusted journalism is not uniformly worse. Some independent journalists doing excellent work have found audiences precisely because institutional journalism failed them. But the average quality of the information environment has probably declined, because institutional journalism at its best, whatever its failures, had professional standards, editorial oversight, legal accountability for defamation and institutional memory. Most of its replacements have none of these things, and many of them are not trying to do journalism in any meaningful sense but are doing audience capture: telling an audience what it wants to hear in exchange for subscription revenue or advertising.

"I subscribe to three local news newsletters, two investigative journalism nonprofits and two podcasts. My information diet is probably better than it was when I was passively consuming cable news. But I'm a person who cares about this and has the time and resources to curate actively. Most people don't. The collapse of trusted institutional news is a problem for democracy even if it's fine for me personally." — r/news, 41,293 upvotes

The International Dimension

Media trust collapse is not uniquely American, which complicates the argument that it is primarily the product of American political dynamics. The Reuters Institute data shows declining trust in media across most Western democracies. The UK, which has a different media landscape and different political history than the United States, shows similar trust declines. So do France, Germany and Australia.

The factors that transcend national contexts point toward structural causes: the same advertising revenue collapse, the same shift to digital platforms, the same incentive structures that reward engagement over accuracy and the same competitive pressure from social media and partisan alternatives. The specific form that media distrust takes varies by country, shaped by local politics, but the underlying trend appears to be driven by transformations in the information economy that are similar across the developed world.

Finland and some Nordic countries are partial exceptions, maintaining higher media trust than most of their peers. These countries have stronger public broadcasting traditions, higher baseline levels of institutional trust, lower political polarization and better media literacy education. The Finnish model, which includes explicit media literacy curriculum in schools from early childhood, consistently produces citizens better equipped to evaluate information quality. That it also correlates with higher media trust suggests that the relationship between media literacy and trust is not simply that people who understand media are more credulous about it, but that people who can distinguish good journalism from bad are better able to recognize and value the good.

Can Trust Be Rebuilt?

The question of whether institutional journalism can recover trust is partly a question about what caused the loss and partly a question about structural constraints. If the loss is primarily due to political manipulation, trust can potentially be rebuilt by demonstrating independence from political pressures through coverage that defies partisan expectations. If the loss is primarily due to real failures of journalistic practice, trust can potentially be rebuilt by fixing those practices. If the loss is primarily structural, a product of business model transformation that has produced systematically worse journalism, the path to rebuilding trust requires solving the underlying business problem, which no one has yet convincingly done.

The nonprofit journalism model, which has grown significantly over the past fifteen years with outlets like ProPublica, The Texas Tribune, The Marshall Project and hundreds of smaller local operations, represents one structural response. Freed from advertising pressure and quarterly earnings demands, nonprofit outlets can more consistently prioritize journalistic quality over engagement metrics. Early evidence suggests they are producing proportionally more impactful investigative work than their for-profit counterparts. But they remain a small fraction of total news production and their long-term funding sustainability is not established.

Subscription models, which have grown significantly at outlets like The New York Times and The Atlantic, create a different alignment of interests: the outlet's revenue depends on reader satisfaction rather than advertiser satisfaction, which might be expected to improve quality. The evidence on this is mixed. Subscription revenue has enabled some investments in quality, but it has also created incentives to give subscribers what they want rather than what they need, which for a politically engaged subscriber base can mean confirming their priors rather than challenging them.

What is probably true is that trust will rebuild, if it does, locally rather than nationally. National media is too caught up in the dynamics of political polarization to easily disentangle itself from partisan identity. Local journalism, which is less implicated in the national culture war and which covers topics where readers can more directly evaluate accuracy against their own experience, has a shorter path to trust recovery. This is one reason why the collapse of local news is arguably the most consequential aspect of the current media crisis, and why rebuilding it is arguably the most important journalism project of the current moment.
