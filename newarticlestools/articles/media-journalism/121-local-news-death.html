The Death of Local News

54,293 upvotes · by u/hometown_paper · 13 min read
▲ 54,293 people found this relatable
Advertisement

Between 2005 and 2023, the United States lost more than a quarter of its local newspapers. According to the Northwestern University Medill Local News Initiative, the country shed over 2,900 newspapers during that period, with the losses concentrated in smaller communities, rural areas and lower-income urban neighborhoods. By 2023, roughly half of all counties in the United States had only one local news source, and about 200 counties had no local news source at all. Those counties are called news deserts, and the people who live in them have lost something whose value becomes most visible only after it is gone.

The financial mechanism is familiar by now: classified advertising moved to Craigslist, display advertising moved to Google and Facebook, readers migrated online but didn't pay for digital content and the print advertising that had subsidized newsrooms for a century evaporated faster than any replacement revenue could be built. Local newspapers were particularly vulnerable because their business models depended heavily on the classified advertising that disappeared earliest and most completely, and because their small subscriber bases and limited geographic reach made them less attractive to the national digital advertisers that replaced print revenue for larger outlets.

What is less familiar, and more important, is a clear account of what actually disappears when local journalism disappears. The losses are not abstract. They are specific, documented and in several cases quantifiable.

What Gets Covered When There Are Reporters to Cover It

Local government is the most immediate casualty of local news decline. City councils, county commissions, school boards, zoning boards, water districts and the hundreds of other civic bodies that make decisions affecting people's daily lives operate almost entirely outside the attention of national journalism. When local reporters cover them, public officials have reason to prepare carefully, avoid self-dealing and justify their decisions in public terms. When local reporters disappear, they don't.

The research on this relationship is among the most rigorous in the media economics literature. A 2018 study by Pengjie Gao, Chang Lee and Dermot Murphy found that municipalities in areas that lost local newspaper coverage experienced significant increases in government borrowing costs, with a measurable increase in municipal bond yields after newspaper closures. The mechanism, the authors concluded, was reduced accountability: with fewer journalists watching, local governments made worse financial decisions, and bond markets priced in the higher risk of corruption or incompetence that came with reduced oversight.

A 2019 study by James Snyder and David Stromberg found that congressional districts with less local news coverage had representatives who were less responsive to their constituents' interests, as measured by voting alignment between member behavior and district-level public opinion. Representatives from low-coverage districts also received fewer grants for their districts and were less likely to sit on committees relevant to their constituents. The mechanism was straightforward: legislators who knew they were not being watched locally had less reason to do the locally responsive work that produces the coverage that produces electoral accountability.

"When our local paper folded, the city council stopped holding public comment periods that were meaningful. They'd technically hold them but schedule them at 7am on a Tuesday with no notice. Corruption that had been marginal became blatant. A city employee embezzled $400,000 and it took two years for anyone outside the finance department to notice. That would never have happened when we had a reporter at every council meeting." — r/localgovernment, 47,382 upvotes

The School Board Problem

School boards are perhaps the clearest example of what low-scrutiny local governance produces. School board meetings were almost universally underreported even when local newspapers existed, covered perfunctorily if at all by papers that devoted limited resources to the most important governance decisions affecting most families. As local news has declined, school board coverage has essentially disappeared in most communities.

The results have been visible in recent years. School board races that once attracted little attention and were decided by tiny electorates became targets for organized outside campaigns precisely because the combination of significant power (control over curriculum, hiring, budgets and policies affecting every child in a district) and minimal scrutiny made them attractive to any organized group willing to invest modest resources. The absence of local journalism that could provide context, track records and accountability reporting made it much harder for ordinary parents to evaluate what was happening or who was asking for their votes.

This is not a partisan observation. School boards in communities across the political spectrum have been captured by organized interests, whether right-wing advocacy groups, teachers unions or ideological currents of various kinds, partly because the lack of scrutiny made capture easy. The problem is not the political direction of any particular capture but the structural vulnerability that low-scrutiny governance creates for any organized interest with the resources and motivation to exploit it.

The Zombie Newspaper Problem

Not all local news loss happens through outright closure. A significant and growing portion of the country is served by what researchers call "ghost newspapers" or "zombie newspapers": publications that nominally exist, maintain a web presence and publish some content but have lost so much staff that they can no longer perform the basic functions of local journalism. A paper that once employed fifteen reporters covering a community of 100,000 people now employs two, who spend most of their time aggregating press releases, covering planned events and generating content that doesn't require the reporting relationships, source development and institutional knowledge that made the paper useful.

Hedge fund ownership has accelerated this dynamic significantly. Alden Global Capital, which owns hundreds of newspapers through its MediaNews Group and Tribune Publishing holdings, has become the paradigm of financial extraction from local journalism. Alden's strategy has been consistently to acquire local papers at distressed valuations, extract cash through layoffs and real estate sales, run the publications on skeleton crews that produce minimal original journalism and sell or close them when they are no longer profitable. The communities served by these papers often don't notice the decline immediately, because the publication still exists, but the journalism that made the publication valuable has been systematically stripped away.

The business logic is coldly coherent. Local newspapers have loyal print subscribers, often older readers who will continue paying for a familiar product even as its quality declines. The marginal cost of cutting staff is immediate and the revenue loss from deteriorating quality is gradual and hard to attribute directly to any specific cut. A paper can be profitably milked for years while providing essentially no journalism, so long as the circulation revenue outlasts the reputation built by decades of prior coverage.

"I subscribed to my local paper for twenty years. I kept subscribing even after Alden bought it because I thought supporting local journalism mattered. Then I realized I was reading eight AP wire stories, three press releases rewritten as news, two social media aggregation pieces and one actual local story per day. I wasn't supporting local journalism. I was funding a cash extraction scheme. I canceled." — r/frugal, 33,847 upvotes

The Hyperlocal Replacement and Its Limits

The standard response to concerns about local news decline is that the internet enables hyperlocal news in forms that weren't previously possible: neighborhood Facebook groups, Nextdoor, local subreddits, civic bloggers, community newsletters and various digital native local news startups. These are real and sometimes valuable. But the evidence that they replace traditional local journalism is weak, and in several important respects they make the problem worse.

Neighborhood social media groups are not journalism. They are conversation spaces that can occasionally surface genuine community information but that operate without editorial standards, fact-checking, source development or the accountability relationships that make journalism useful. They amplify rumor as readily as fact, they skew dramatically toward homeowners and away from renters and transient populations, and they are highly susceptible to the platform's algorithmic amplification of conflict and outrage. A study of Nextdoor usage consistently found racial profiling behavior that the platform has struggled to address: the "suspicious person" report category disproportionately targeted Black and Latino residents, a pattern that a local newspaper covering the same community would presumably not reproduce systematically.

Digital native local news startups have shown genuine promise in cities large enough to support them. The Texas Tribune, Baltimore's The Brew, The City in New York and dozens of similar operations produce excellent journalism. But they are concentrated in larger cities where the funding base and potential audience can support professional journalism, and they have not emerged in the smaller communities and rural areas where local news loss is most acute. The journalism deserts that most need replacement are precisely the ones least attractive to the digital entrepreneurs and philanthropists funding local news innovation.

The Polarization Connection

One of the more surprising findings in the local news research literature is the relationship between local news decline and political polarization. Several studies have found that counties that lose local newspapers experience measurable increases in partisan polarization in subsequent election cycles, with voters shifting toward more extreme positions and split-ticket voting declining.

The proposed mechanism is that local news, when it exists, anchors political discourse in local issues, local personalities and local outcomes that are relatively independent of national partisan frames. A story about the local water board's financing decisions or the school district's test scores is a story about concrete local reality, not about national ideological identity. When local news disappears and national news and social media fill the information vacuum, political identity becomes entirely framed by national partisan conflict. Voters who have no local information ecosystem to relate to are left with only national partisan frames, which are systematically more extreme, more identity-based and less connected to actual governance than local frames.

This is a significant finding because it suggests that local news decline is not just a journalism problem or even just a governance problem. It is a democracy problem at the most fundamental level. The polarization that makes national governance increasingly dysfunctional may be substantially driven by the collapse of the local information environments that once provided anchoring in shared local reality.

What Rebuilding Requires

The solutions being tried are various and the evidence on their effectiveness is still early. Nonprofit local news organizations funded by foundation grants and individual donations represent the most robust model so far, but their long-term financial sustainability is unproven and their current scale is nowhere near adequate to replace what has been lost. The Institute for Nonprofit News tracked more than 300 nonprofit local news organizations in 2023, collectively employing fewer than 3,000 journalists, against the tens of thousands of local news positions that have been eliminated over the past two decades.

Public funding for local journalism, common in Western Europe through public broadcasting models and press subsidies, remains politically difficult in the United States because of concerns about government influence on content. The BBC model, a public broadcaster funded by a license fee and governed by an independent trust, has no American equivalent, and proposals for anything resembling it tend to generate immediate objections that they would create a state-funded propaganda arm. These concerns are not entirely without merit, but they have served primarily as reasons to do nothing while the information infrastructure of democracy deteriorates.

The most promising recent development may be the Rebuild Local News coalition's advocacy for federal tax credits for local news subscriptions and for local news employment, modeled roughly on federal film production tax credits. Several states have enacted or are considering direct subsidies for local journalism employment. Canada introduced a Local Journalism Initiative in 2019 that has funded hundreds of local journalism positions in news deserts. These policy instruments don't solve the underlying business model problem, but they can extend the viability of local news organizations while longer-term models are developed.

What seems clear from the research is that the communities that have lost local journalism cannot simply wait for the market to provide a replacement. The market has clearly demonstrated that it will not. What remains is whether democratic self-governance is valuable enough to justify the public investment required to sustain the journalism that makes it possible. That is a political question, and answering it requires exactly the kind of informed local civic discourse that local journalism used to support.
