The Case for Harm Reduction: What the Evidence Actually Says

In 1988, a needle exchange program opened in Tacoma, Washington, run by a public health worker named Dave Purchase out of a card table in a parking lot. Purchase operated the exchange without official permission, simply because he believed that preventing the transmission of HIV among people who inject drugs was more important than the political controversy that needle exchanges generated. He was right about the controversy. He was also right about the public health. The Tacoma program became one of the most studied needle exchange programs in the early literature, and its results were consistent with what every subsequent well-designed study has found: needle exchanges reduce HIV transmission, reduce hepatitis C transmission, do not increase drug use in the surrounding community and serve as a bridge to treatment for a significant proportion of participants.

Harm reduction is a public health philosophy and a set of specific interventions that share a common premise: since drug use will occur regardless of its legal status, interventions that reduce the health consequences of drug use save lives even when they do not eliminate the drug use itself. The philosophy sounds self-evidently practical in this framing. In the actual politics of drug policy, it is genuinely controversial, not because the evidence is disputed but because it conflicts with a moral framework in which any accommodation of drug use constitutes an endorsement of it, and in which the suffering of people who use drugs is considered, to some degree, an appropriate consequence of their choices.

The conflict between the evidence base for harm reduction and the political reception of that evidence is one of the cleaner illustrations available of how values shape policy in ways that override empirical analysis. The harm reduction literature is unusually robust for public health interventions. The political resistance to harm reduction is not primarily about the evidence. It is about whether keeping people who use drugs alive and healthier is a legitimate goal of public health policy, which turns out to be a more contested question than it should be.

Needle Exchange: The Evidence Foundation

Needle and syringe programs (NSPs), also called syringe services programs (SSPs) in current public health usage, have accumulated one of the largest evidence bases of any harm reduction intervention. A 2014 systematic review in The Lancet examined data from studies across multiple countries and found that NSPs reduced HIV incidence among people who inject drugs by approximately 50 percent, with additional evidence of reduced hepatitis C transmission. A subsequent CDC analysis of the US context found that NSPs were associated with a 50 percent reduction in HIV incidence among participants and estimated that the country had prevented tens of thousands of HIV infections through syringe service programs since their introduction.

The "do needle exchanges increase drug use" question has been studied repeatedly and answered consistently: they do not. Studies examining drug use rates in communities before and after NSP implementation have found no increase in injection drug use attributable to the programs. Studies comparing drug use rates in cities with and without NSPs have found no difference attributable to program availability. The concern that providing clean needles signals social approval of drug use, thereby increasing uptake, has not been supported by any rigorous empirical investigation. The concern may be intuitively compelling, but intuitive plausibility is not the same as empirical support.

NSPs also function as a point of contact between people who use drugs and health services they would not otherwise access. Research has consistently found that NSP participants are more likely to enter drug treatment, more likely to receive HIV testing and more likely to be linked to medical care for other conditions than people who inject drugs without NSP contact. The needle exchange is not, for most participants, simply a place to obtain clean equipment. It is often the most accessible, least stigmatizing health service point in their social environment, and its staff often become the primary healthcare contact for people who have been driven out of or away from conventional healthcare by stigma and mistreatment.

Naloxone: The Intervention That Should Be Everywhere

Naloxone is an opioid antagonist medication that reverses opioid overdose within minutes when administered intranasally or by injection. It is inexpensive, safe, non-addictive and effective at preventing death from opioid overdose in a condition where the principal cause of death is respiratory depression that takes several minutes to become irreversible. The evidence base for naloxone distribution is not contested. The pharmacology is well-understood. The only genuine debates in the literature concern optimal distribution strategies, not whether naloxone should be distributed.

Despite this, naloxone access has been systematically restricted in the United States through a combination of regulatory barriers and political resistance. For most of its history, naloxone was a prescription-only medication, requiring a physician visit to obtain. Since emergency opioid overdoses typically occur in contexts where the person or their companions do not have immediate access to a physician, this restriction created a situation in which the medication that could prevent the death was legally inaccessible to the people who most needed to have it. Advocacy efforts gradually expanded access through standing orders (allowing pharmacists to dispense without an individual prescription), over-the-counter availability in some states and, ultimately, the FDA's approval of over-the-counter naloxone in 2023.

The "does naloxone increase risky drug use" argument, sometimes called the "moral hazard" critique, has been raised against naloxone distribution as it has been raised against virtually every harm reduction intervention. The empirical evidence does not support it. Studies examining drug use patterns in communities with expanded naloxone access have not found increased drug use or risk-taking behavior. The behavioral economics argument underlying the moral hazard concern assumes that people who use drugs are making explicit expected-value calculations that incorporate overdose risk, a model of drug use behavior that the addiction research literature does not support. People in the grip of addiction are not carefully weighing mortality statistics against the pleasure of drug use. They are responding to cravings that the moral hazard model simply does not describe.

A separate and well-founded criticism of naloxone policy concerns pricing. Narcan nasal spray, the branded naloxone product, was priced at approximately $140 for a two-dose pack through much of the opioid epidemic, placing it out of reach for the populations most likely to need it. The cost was not driven by manufacturing complexity; generic injectable naloxone was available for approximately $20. It was driven by pharmaceutical pricing strategy applied to a life-saving medication whose market included people in crisis with limited purchasing power. The pricing controversy contributed to pressure that eventually produced the generic nasal spray option and over-the-counter availability, but the years of high pricing during the epidemic's peak represent a preventable delay in a policy that would have saved lives at essentially any price point.

Supervised Consumption Sites: The Evidence Nobody Wants to Discuss

Supervised consumption sites (SCS), also called supervised injection facilities or drug consumption rooms, are facilities where people can use pre-obtained drugs under medical supervision, with clean equipment and staff trained to respond to overdoses. They exist in more than 100 facilities across Canada, Europe and Australia. Not a single person has died of an overdose inside a supervised consumption facility anywhere in the world.

That statement is worth dwelling on. In facilities where people use drugs that carry significant overdose risk, under conditions designed to make overdose survivable, no one has died. This is not because supervised consumption sites prevent drug use or addiction. It is because they ensure that if an overdose occurs, medical response is immediate. The complete absence of in-facility overdose deaths across decades of operation in multiple countries represents a public health achievement of considerable significance, achieved by an intervention whose political acceptability in the United States remains essentially zero at the federal level.

The Vancouver Insite facility, opened in 2003, is among the most comprehensively studied supervised consumption sites. Research published in the New England Journal of Medicine, The Lancet and other major journals has documented that Insite reduced overdose mortality in the surrounding neighborhood by approximately 35 percent following its opening, increased treatment uptake among participants, did not increase drug use or drug-related crime in the surrounding area and was cost-effective by standard public health economic analysis. The Canadian Supreme Court ruled unanimously in 2011 that the federal government could not close Insite without violating the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, affirming that the site provided health services whose denial would threaten the life and security of its clients.

"I used at Insite for two years before I was ready to think about treatment. During those two years the staff never pressured me, never judged me and saved my life twice. When I finally asked for help with treatment, they helped me that same day. I don't know what recovery would have looked like if I'd been using in an alley instead." r/Harm_Reduction · 53,917 upvotes

The United States has moved cautiously in the direction of supervised consumption. New York City opened two officially sanctioned consumption sites in 2021, operated by nonprofit organizations OnPoint NYC. By 2023, the sites had overseen more than 130,000 visits, reversed thousands of overdoses and not experienced a single in-facility death. The federal government under the Biden administration declined to prosecute the sites, effectively tolerating them through non-enforcement, but no federal legal authorization exists and the sites operate under ongoing legal and political uncertainty.

Drug Checking Services and Fentanyl Test Strips

The contamination of the illicit drug supply with fentanyl and increasingly with novel synthetic opioids like nitazenes has created a situation in which people using drugs cannot reliably know what they are taking. This is not, as it is sometimes framed, a problem of people choosing to take dangerous substances. It is a problem of people taking substances they believe to be cocaine, MDMA or heroin and unexpectedly encountering fentanyl at concentrations lethal to people without opioid tolerance. The overdose risk is not limited to people with opioid use disorder. It extends to anyone who uses any illicit substance in a supply contaminated by fentanyl adulteration.

Fentanyl test strips are inexpensive, widely available and effective at detecting fentanyl in a drug sample. Research by researchers at NEXT Distro and University of North Carolina found that people who used fentanyl test strips and received a positive result changed their behavior in ways that reduced overdose risk: using less, using more slowly, having naloxone available and using with others rather than alone. A 2018 study in the International Journal of Drug Policy found that fentanyl test strip availability reduced overdose likelihood with no observed increase in drug use.

Until 2023, fentanyl test strips were classified as drug paraphernalia in a majority of US states, making their distribution illegal. The classification reflected paraphernalia laws written decades before fentanyl contamination was a public health issue, but the laws had not been updated to accommodate the changed landscape. Advocacy efforts produced exemptions or reclassifications in most states by 2023, supported in part by federal public health guidance endorsing test strip distribution. The delay in making this inexpensive, effective intervention available represented years of preventable overdose deaths attributable directly to a legal classification that had no current public health justification.

The Portuguese Decriminalization Experiment

Portugal's 2001 decriminalization of personal possession and use of all drugs represents the most comprehensive natural experiment in drug policy reform available to researchers. The policy did not legalize drugs: production and supply remain criminal offenses. It reclassified personal possession as an administrative violation rather than a criminal offense, with people found in possession of small amounts referred to "dissuasion commissions" that can recommend treatment, community service or small fines rather than criminal prosecution.

The Portuguese experience in the two decades following decriminalization has been studied extensively. Drug-related HIV infections fell by more than 95 percent. Drug-related incarceration rates fell dramatically. Treatment uptake increased. Drug use rates did not increase significantly and remain below the EU average for most substances. The policy change was accompanied by significant investment in treatment infrastructure and social services, making it difficult to attribute all outcomes to decriminalization specifically, but the overall picture is of a policy change that improved measurable outcomes while not producing the increases in drug use that opponents predicted.

The Portuguese example is frequently cited in harm reduction advocacy and frequently mischaracterized by critics. It does not show that drug use disappears or that addiction stops being a problem. It shows that treating drug use as a health issue rather than a criminal one produces better health outcomes without producing worse drug use outcomes. The lesson is not that drugs are harmless but that criminalization is not an effective tool for addressing the harms of drug use and imposes significant additional harms of its own.

Why the Evidence Does Not Determine Policy

If the evidence for harm reduction is as strong as the literature suggests, why does harm reduction remain so politically contested? The answer is partly about the nature of the evidence, which, while robust by public health standards, involves populations and outcomes that are easy for political opponents to dismiss. But it is primarily about values.

Opposition to harm reduction is largely driven by the belief that accommodating drug use is morally wrong regardless of its public health consequences. From this perspective, a needle exchange that prevents HIV infections is objectionable not because it doesn't prevent HIV infections but because preventing HIV infections in people who use drugs is not the right response to drug use. The right response, from this perspective, is to make drug use costly enough through punishment and social consequence that people stop doing it. The evidence that this approach has not worked, that drug use rates in high-punishment jurisdictions are not lower than in harm reduction-oriented jurisdictions, is genuinely difficult to absorb within this framework because the framework is not primarily empirical. It is moral.

This means that arguments for harm reduction that center exclusively on evidence are often structurally ineffective with the audiences who most resist harm reduction, because those audiences are not primarily persuaded by the evidence outcomes. The more productive argument may be one that takes the moral framework seriously enough to engage with it: that the people who use drugs are human beings whose lives have value, that their deaths are losses rather than outcomes, and that a public health system that allows preventable deaths because the prevention requires tolerating the behavior that creates the risk has made a moral choice, not merely a policy choice. The evidence tells us what works. The argument about whether "works" means keeping people alive is the argument that actually needs to be won.
