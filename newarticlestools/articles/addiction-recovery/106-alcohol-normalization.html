How Society Normalized Alcohol While Demonizing Everything Else

There is a party trick that works almost every time. Tell someone you do not drink and watch their face cycle through a sequence of reactions: mild surprise, a search for an explanation, and then, reliably, an implicit demand for one. Are you in recovery? Religious reasons? Pregnant? A designated driver? The requirement to account for your abstinence is automatic and almost never examined. Nobody asks you why you are not smoking. Nobody demands a reason why you skipped the cocaine. But alcohol's absence is a social anomaly requiring explanation in a way that no other drug's absence is, which tells you something fundamental about how deeply embedded alcohol is in the cultural infrastructure of daily life.

Alcohol is a class one carcinogen. It is linked to seven distinct cancer types. It is the third leading preventable cause of death in the United States. It is involved in approximately 40 percent of violent crimes and a significant proportion of domestic violence incidents. Globally, alcohol causes more disability-adjusted life years than any other substance. And it is sold at every grocery store, prominently featured in virtually every social gathering, marketed with a sophistication and volume that no other drug approaches legally, and embedded in cultural rituals from graduation to grief with a naturalness that has made questioning its centrality feel eccentric rather than reasonable.

The normalization of alcohol is not accidental. It is the product of a specific historical trajectory, a remarkably effective industry marketing apparatus, and cultural practices that have accumulated enough social weight to feel like nature rather than choice. Understanding how alcohol achieved and maintains this status, while other substances far less harmful by most measures are criminalized and stigmatized, is essential context for understanding addiction, recovery and the social environments that make both harder than they need to be.

The Historical Construction of Alcohol's Legitimacy

Alcohol's privileged position in American culture is historically contingent in ways that are easy to forget. Prohibition, which lasted from 1920 to 1933, was not a fringe movement but a mainstream reform effort backed by a broad coalition including temperance organizations, progressive reformers, women's suffrage advocates and religious denominations. The Women's Christian Temperance Union was among the largest women's organizations in American history, and its concerns were substantially grounded in documented social harm: domestic violence, workplace injury and economic devastation of working-class families driven by alcohol consumption were real phenomena, not moral panics.

Prohibition's failure, and the subsequent rehabilitation of alcohol as a legitimate consumer product, reshaped the cultural script around drinking in ways that have persisted for nearly a century. Repeal in 1933 was followed by an aggressive industry effort to re-establish alcohol as a normal, respectable part of American life after thirteen years of illegality. The strategy involved cultivating associations between alcohol and sophistication, social ease, celebration and masculinity. Advertising in the post-Prohibition decades built the cultural architecture that connects drinking with specific life moments: the toast at weddings, the drinks with colleagues after work, the tailgate, the wine with dinner, the beer at the game.

The key historical advantage alcohol has over other intoxicating substances is that it completed this normalization before modern public health frameworks existed to complicate the process. Cannabis, despite being less harmful than alcohol by most pharmacological measures, was criminalized during a period when public health messaging could be weaponized against it. Psychedelics achieved cultural salience in the 1960s exactly when political anxiety about counterculture provided the political fuel for aggressive criminalization. Alcohol was grandfathered into cultural acceptability before the regulatory and public health apparatus that might have constrained it was fully built.

The Wine Culture Industrial Complex

The specific cultural moment that most accelerated alcohol normalization for a specific demographic was the emergence of wine culture in the late 1990s and early 2000s. Wine had historically been associated with formal dining, European sophistication and, by implication, social class. The Wine Spectator, Robert Parker's 100-point rating system, the movie Sideways and the explosion of wine education programming made wine approachable across a broader demographic while retaining enough cultural cachet to feel like elevation rather than mere drinking.

The demographic that this newly democratized wine culture most aggressively targeted was women, particularly middle-class mothers. The marketing apparatus that followed was both sophisticated and retrospectively remarkable in its directness. "Mommy juice" became a cultural meme, a term used without irony in marketing materials, parenting humor and social media communities. Wine was positioned explicitly as the reward for the emotional labor of parenting, the substance that made it survivable, the gift mothers gave themselves at the end of a day the culture simultaneously praised as rewarding and acknowledged as exhausting.

The messaging was everywhere: wine glasses printed with "Mom's Sippy Cup," novelty items framing wine consumption as a maternal coping mechanism, parenting humor content on social media in which every difficult parenting moment led to a wine punchline. Television sitcoms featuring mothers as characters almost universally featured wine drinking as a character trait, a running gag and a sympathetic detail. The cultural message was consistent and cumulative: mothers drink wine, drinking wine is what mothers do, and anyone who questions this is either uptight or unaware of how hard parenting actually is.

The public health implications of this particular marketing moment were significant. Alcohol use disorder diagnosis rates among women increased substantially during the same period this marketing was most intense. A landmark study in JAMA Psychiatry published in 2017 found that high-risk drinking increased by 57.9 percent among women between 2001 and 2013, with an 83.7 percent increase in alcohol use disorder specifically. The study did not establish causation between marketing and these trends, but the temporal correlation between the wine culture marketing explosion and the increase in problematic drinking among the target demographic is difficult to explain without invoking the marketing as a contributing factor.

The Wellness Contradiction

One of the more intellectually jarring features of contemporary health culture is the coexistence of intense wellness optimization with routine alcohol consumption. The same culture that treats seed oils as a health emergency, optimizes sleep with $400 trackers, uses continuous glucose monitors recreationally and discusses protein intake with near-clinical precision often treats alcohol as a wellness-neutral or even wellness-positive substance, segregated from the optimization mindset that governs everything else.

The "red wine is good for you" narrative, which peaked in public awareness in the late 1990s following the popularization of the "French paradox" research, provided cultural cover for this compartmentalization. The research on resveratrol and other polyphenols in red wine was real but modest, and subsequent studies found that the cardiovascular benefits of light drinking were likely confounded by the health characteristics of people who drink moderately: they tend to have higher incomes, better healthcare access and more active social lives than both abstainers and heavy drinkers, making it difficult to attribute health outcomes to the alcohol itself.

The most authoritative analysis came from the Global Burden of Disease study published in The Lancet in 2018, which analyzed data from 195 countries and concluded that the safest level of alcohol consumption for overall health is zero. The study found that any health benefits of moderate drinking were outweighed by increased cancer risk, and that the net effect of any level of alcohol consumption was negative for population health. The finding received widespread news coverage and then largely disappeared from mainstream health discourse, which continued to treat moderate drinking as a benign or mildly beneficial habit.

The wellness industry's relationship with alcohol reflects a broader pattern in which commercially valuable behaviors are not subjected to the same scrutiny as commercially inconvenient ones. Gluten received intense wellness culture attention during a period when the gluten-free market was growing rapidly. Seed oils became a target when alternative fat products were gaining market share. Alcohol, which is sold by industries with considerable marketing budgets and whose moderate consumption is deeply embedded in wellness culture events (the wine at the yoga retreat, the beer at the Crossfit competition), has received protection from the same rigorous health scrutiny.

What "Normal" Drinking Actually Looks Like

The public health definition of "low-risk" drinking in the United States, as defined by NIAAA, is no more than three drinks on any single day and no more than seven per week for women, and no more than four on any single day and no more than fourteen per week for men. These guidelines, which are considerably more conservative than cultural norms in most social milieus, are exceeded by a significant portion of the American population that does not identify as having a drinking problem.

Surveys consistently find that Americans dramatically underestimate both their own drinking and the national average. When people report their drinking to researchers, they tend to recall fewer drinks than they consumed, count smaller servings than standard drink definitions, and anchor their self-assessment to a social comparison group whose drinking is itself elevated. The "standard drink" that health authorities use in their calculations (14 grams of pure alcohol, equivalent to a 12-ounce 5% beer, a 5-ounce glass of wine or a 1.5-ounce shot of spirits) is consistently smaller than what people pour in home settings. A home wine pour is typically 6 to 8 ounces, meaning one "glass" is closer to 1.3 to 1.6 standard drinks.

The gap between perceived normal drinking and actual public health guidelines is significant enough to create a situation where a large proportion of people who consider themselves moderate social drinkers are, by clinical definition, drinking at levels that carry meaningful health risks. This is not an argument for abstinence as the only acceptable choice. It is an observation about how thoroughly the cultural normalization of alcohol has shaped what drinking "looks like" in ways that work against accurate self-assessment.

The Sober Curious Movement and Its Limits

The "sober curious" movement, a term popularized by author Ruby Warrington's 2018 book of the same name, represents a cultural shift away from the binary of "alcoholic/normal drinker" toward a more nuanced exploration of people's relationship with alcohol that does not require a diagnosis. The movement has produced meaningful cultural change: a proliferation of nonalcoholic options at restaurants and bars, a reduction in the social pressure to explain abstinence and growing public conversation about alcohol's actual health effects that was largely absent from mainstream wellness discourse previously.

The limitations of the sober curious framing deserve acknowledgment alongside its contributions. The movement has been predominantly white, middle-class and wellness-adjacent, which shapes both its concerns and its solutions. It tends to focus on the personal optimization dimensions of reducing alcohol (better sleep, clearer skin, more energy, weight loss) rather than the public health and equity dimensions of alcohol harm, which fall disproportionately on lower-income communities and communities of color where alcohol marketing is heavily concentrated and treatment resources are least available.

There is also a question about whether "curious" is an adequate framework for people whose relationship with alcohol has become genuinely problematic. The sober curious movement's appeal is partly its low threshold, its freedom from the stigma of the "alcoholic" label and its framing of sobriety as a lifestyle experiment rather than a medical necessity. But for people who have developed alcohol use disorder, curiosity about sobriety is not the operative challenge. The physiological dependence that characterizes alcohol use disorder is one of the most dangerous of any substance, with withdrawal that can be fatal and a clinical complexity that requires medical management. The lifestyle reframing of sober curiosity, valuable for the significant population of gray-area drinkers, can inadvertently minimize the seriousness of the clinical problem for those who need medical rather than lifestyle intervention.

Recovery in a Drinking Culture

For people attempting recovery from alcohol use disorder, the normalization described in this article is not an abstract cultural observation. It is an environmental reality that shapes recovery difficulty in concrete, daily ways. Recovery from alcohol dependence requires abstaining from a substance that is present at virtually every social gathering, marketed on every screen, available at every corner store and embedded in social rituals that have no non-drinking equivalent.

Research on recovery environments consistently finds that the social context of drinking significantly affects relapse risk. Living with someone who drinks, socializing in settings where alcohol is central and having social networks in which drinking is the primary bonding activity are all associated with higher relapse rates. The challenge for people in recovery is not only the physiological craving for alcohol but the social negotiation of environments that were not designed with their needs in mind and that often actively complicate abstinence.

The stigma around alcohol use disorder, despite decades of public health messaging framing addiction as a brain disease rather than a moral failure, remains substantial. People with alcohol use disorder are more likely to face employment discrimination, relationship strain and social judgment than people with comparable non-substance-related health conditions. The stigma operates in complex ways: the disease model framing intended to reduce stigma has coexisted with persistent moral judgment in ways that research on stigma reduction has struggled to fully explain. One dimension of this is precisely the normalization problem: because moderate drinking is so culturally accepted, heavy drinking and dependence are judged not only on health grounds but as failures of the self-control that everyone else seems to manage.

The Treatment Gap and What Works

Of the approximately 29.5 million Americans who met criteria for alcohol use disorder in 2021 according to SAMHSA's National Survey on Drug Use and Health, only about 7.6 percent received any treatment in the past year. The treatment gap for alcohol is larger than for most other substance use disorders, a fact that reflects multiple factors including the normalization that makes self-identification as someone with a problem difficult, the cost and accessibility barriers to formal treatment and the continued cultural association of alcohol treatment with moral failure rather than medical care.

Treatment options have expanded considerably from the mid-twentieth century model in which Alcoholics Anonymous was the primary available resource. Medication-assisted treatment for alcohol use disorder is now supported by a substantial evidence base. Naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram are all FDA-approved medications that reduce craving and relapse risk with documented efficacy. Yet medication-assisted treatment for alcohol remains substantially underused relative to its evidence base, in part because the dominant cultural narrative around alcohol recovery, shaped heavily by 12-step tradition, frames medication with ambivalence or skepticism.

The evidence for AA itself is, by the standards of modern clinical research, modest and complex. A 2020 Cochrane review on AA concluded that it was more effective than other treatments at achieving continuous abstinence but that the evidence was limited by methodological quality and that outcomes for controlled drinking goals did not clearly favor AA over alternatives. Cognitive behavioral therapy, motivational enhancement therapy and contingency management approaches all have meaningful evidence bases. The diversification of recovery pathways, moving away from a one-size-fits-all model toward evidence-based treatment matched to individual characteristics and goals, represents the direction that clinical research most clearly supports.

The harder problem is not treatment methodology but treatment access. The normalization of alcohol in mainstream culture is mirrored by the marginalization of alcohol treatment in healthcare. Primary care physicians receive minimal training in addiction medicine. Insurance coverage for substance use disorder treatment, while improved by the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act of 2008, remains inconsistent and often inadequate in practice. The cultural infrastructure that makes alcohol consumption easy and visible has no counterpart in making treatment for alcohol use disorder easy and visible. Fixing that asymmetry would require not just expanding treatment capacity but confronting the normalization that makes the problem itself so hard to see.
