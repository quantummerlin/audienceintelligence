The Opioid Crisis Was Not an Accident: How Purdue Pharma Manufactured an Epidemic

Between 1999 and 2022, approximately 645,000 Americans died from opioid overdoses. The number is large enough to be difficult to hold in the mind as anything other than an abstraction, so consider it another way: more Americans died in the opioid epidemic than in every military conflict the United States has fought since World War II, combined. More than Vietnam, Korea, the Gulf War, Iraq and Afghanistan together. A catastrophe of that scale does not emerge from nowhere. It has causes, and the causes have names, addresses, balance sheets and, in some cases, legal records.

The opioid epidemic is not primarily a story about individual addiction, though it contains millions of individual stories. It is primarily a story about regulatory failure, corporate misconduct, physician co-optation and deliberate, documented deception about a product the industry knew was dangerous. The Sackler family, Purdue Pharma and the broader network of opioid manufacturers, distributors and retailers built the conditions for a mass casualty event over decades, with knowledge of the harm they were causing and under the protection of regulatory and legal systems that were repeatedly manipulated to limit accountability.

Understanding the opioid crisis accurately requires starting not with individual failure but with the corporate strategy that created the conditions for individual failure on a national scale.

The Pain Management Revolution and the Opportunity It Created

The opioid crisis has a specific origin point in the history of pain management in American medicine. Through most of the twentieth century, opioids were used cautiously and sparingly, primarily for acute post-surgical pain and cancer pain, with significant physician reluctance to prescribe them for chronic non-cancer pain based on well-founded concern about dependence risk. Medical education taught that opioid dependence was a serious risk and that chronic opioid therapy for non-malignant pain was generally inadvisable.

A significant shift began in the 1990s, driven by a genuine and in some respects legitimate concern: that American medicine was systematically undertreating pain. Palliative care advocates, patient rights organizations and some researchers argued compellingly that cancer patients and others with serious conditions were suffering unnecessarily because physicians were overly conservative about prescribing adequate analgesia. The American Pain Society in 1995 proposed that pain be considered a "fifth vital sign" alongside temperature, pulse, blood pressure and respiratory rate, to be measured and addressed routinely at every clinical encounter. The Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations adopted pain assessment standards in 2001 that created institutional pressure for hospitals to demonstrate pain management performance.

This legitimate clinical movement provided an opening that the pharmaceutical industry exploited with remarkable effectiveness. The movement's core insight, that pain was undertreated, was correct in specific contexts. The industry's response was to extend that insight indiscriminately to all pain in all patients, backed by marketing claims about the safety of opioids that were not supported by evidence and that the manufacturers either knew or should have known were false.

OxyContin and the Marketing of a Lie

Purdue Pharma launched OxyContin in 1995 with a marketing strategy built on a central claim: that the drug's extended-release formulation made it less likely to be abused and less addictive than immediate-release opioids. The claim was not supported by clinical evidence at the time of launch and was, in fact, contradicted by what Purdue's own scientists knew about opioid pharmacology. The FDA label for OxyContin stated that the drug's delayed absorption "is believed to reduce the abuse liability of a drug," language that was carefully hedged by regulators but which Purdue's sales force translated into unqualified claims that OxyContin was safer and less addictive than competing opioids.

The sales force deployed against the medical community was enormous. Purdue hired roughly 600 sales representatives to detail OxyContin at launch, eventually expanding to over 1,000. Their compensation included bonuses directly tied to the volume of OxyContin prescriptions written in their territories, creating direct financial incentives to maximize prescribing regardless of clinical appropriateness. Sales representatives provided physicians with sponsored educational programs, paid speaking arrangements and gifts that, while common pharmaceutical industry practice, created financial relationships between the prescribers and the company whose product was being prescribed.

The company commissioned and disseminated marketing materials that systematically minimized addiction risk. A promotional video titled "I Got My Life Back" featured patients with chronic pain describing OxyContin as transformative. Sales training materials emphasized techniques for overcoming physician resistance to opioid prescribing for conditions previously considered inappropriate indications. The message, in various forms, was consistent: the old caution about opioids was based on misunderstanding; OxyContin's unique formulation had solved the addiction problem; undertreating pain was the real harm; and physicians who were reluctant to prescribe were causing their patients to suffer unnecessarily.

The marketing worked. OxyContin prescriptions grew from 670,000 in 1997 to 6.2 million in 2002. Revenue grew from $44 million in 1996 to $1.5 billion in 2002. The Sackler family, which privately owned Purdue Pharma, extracted billions in profits during the period of peak OxyContin sales, much of it moved into overseas trusts and accounts in what subsequent litigation characterized as deliberate asset protection in anticipation of eventual legal liability.

What They Knew and When

The legal proceedings against Purdue Pharma and the Sackler family, which culminated in Purdue's bankruptcy and a controversial settlement, produced an extensive documentary record of internal company knowledge. That record is damning in specific and verifiable ways that go beyond the general allegation of corporate wrongdoing.

Internal Purdue documents revealed in litigation showed that the company received reports of OxyContin abuse and diversion as early as 1997, within two years of launch. A 1997 report from a Purdue sales representative described a patient in Virginia describing OxyContin as "hillbilly heroin," a term that was already circulating in Appalachian communities where the drug had begun to devastate communities with concentrated economic vulnerability. Rather than responding to these reports by modifying marketing practices or warning physicians, Purdue internally monitored the abuse reports and used them to anticipate regulatory scrutiny while continuing to push aggressive prescribing growth.

The FDA's own process was compromised. A 2003 General Accounting Office report found that OxyContin had been approved based on inadequate clinical trial data and that the initial label language about abuse potential was not supported by the evidence available at the time of approval. Curtis Wright, the FDA reviewer who approved OxyContin in 1996, left the agency in 1997 and was hired by Purdue Pharma, where he worked for several years. The revolving door between FDA and industry was not exceptional in the context of pharmaceutical regulation, but in the OxyContin case it was attached to an approval process that had meaningful gaps.

State attorneys general who began investigating OxyContin abuse patterns in the early 2000s encountered a company that denied, delayed and disputed at every stage. When West Virginia, a state with among the most acute opioid problems in the nation, sued Purdue in 2001, the company mounted an aggressive legal defense while continuing to grow its prescribing base. The 2007 federal guilty plea by Purdue Pharma LLC, in which the company admitted to misbranding OxyContin with the intent to defraud or mislead, resulted in a $600 million fine that was large in nominal terms but represented less than one year's revenue from OxyContin sales. The plea was structured in a way that allowed the parent company and the Sackler family to avoid direct criminal liability, a feature of the settlement that state attorneys general and victims' advocates have continued to criticize as inadequate to the scale of the harm.

The Three Waves: How the Crisis Mutated

The opioid epidemic is typically described by public health authorities as having three overlapping waves, a structure that is important for understanding both the scale of the crisis and the ways that the initial pharmaceutical-driven harm created downstream effects that continued long after prescribing patterns changed.

The first wave, beginning in the late 1990s, was driven almost entirely by prescription opioids: OxyContin, but also Vicodin, Percocet and other opioid formulations whose prescribing increased dramatically as pain management practices shifted. Overdose deaths in this wave were concentrated among white, rural and suburban populations in states with the most aggressive OxyContin marketing: Appalachian states, the rural Midwest and parts of the South.

The second wave began around 2010 and was driven by heroin. When Purdue reformulated OxyContin in 2010 to make it harder to abuse by crushing and snorting, and when regulatory and law enforcement pressure began reducing prescription opioid availability, many people who had developed physical dependence on prescription opioids transitioned to heroin as a cheaper, more available substitute. The transition was eased by the physiological similarity between the two drugs: people dependent on prescription opioids often found that heroin produced a comparable effect at a fraction of the cost. Heroin overdose deaths accelerated sharply beginning around 2010.

The third wave, which began around 2013 and continues to drive the majority of current overdose deaths, is characterized by synthetic opioids, primarily illicitly manufactured fentanyl. Fentanyl is 50 to 100 times more potent than morphine and its introduction into the illicit drug supply created a fundamentally different overdose risk landscape. Small dosing errors that would be tolerated with heroin are lethal with fentanyl. Fentanyl has contaminated not just heroin supplies but cocaine, methamphetamine and counterfeit prescription pills, creating overdose risk for people who have no history of opioid use or tolerance. The third wave has elevated overdose deaths to levels that dwarf the earlier waves and has defied straightforward intervention because it is driven by illicit manufacturing rather than pharmaceutical prescribing.

The Geographic and Economic Dimensions

The opioid epidemic's geographic distribution is not random. It correlates strongly with economic distress, industrial decline and the specific targeting strategies used by pharmaceutical companies and distributors. Purdue's sales force was deployed with particular intensity in rural communities with high rates of physical labor, injury and economic precarity: mining communities in Appalachia, manufacturing towns in the Midwest, agricultural areas in the South. These were communities with high rates of musculoskeletal pain, limited access to alternative pain treatment modalities and economic circumstances that made the promise of effective pain relief unusually compelling.

Research by economists Angus Deaton and Anne Case, whose work on "deaths of despair" documented the mortality crisis among middle-aged white Americans without college degrees, found strong correlations between the opioid epidemic and economic indicators of decline. Communities that had lost manufacturing employment, that had high rates of disability and chronic pain from physically demanding work and that had limited economic prospects showed the highest rates of opioid mortality. The epidemic was not simply the pharmaceutical industry acting on passive victims; it intersected with genuine suffering that existing healthcare systems were poorly equipped to address.

The distribution pipeline that moved prescription opioids from manufacturers to patients in these communities is a second story of documented institutional failure. Cardinal Health, McKesson and AmerisourceBergen, the three largest drug distributors in the United States, collectively shipped billions of opioid pills into communities that could not plausibly consume them for legitimate medical purposes. A single pharmacy in Williamson, West Virginia, population about 3,000, received approximately 12 million opioid pills over three years according to DEA records revealed in litigation. West Virginia as a whole received enough prescription opioids to supply every resident with 433 pills per year at the epidemic's peak.

The distributors were legally required under DEA regulations to report suspicious orders, defined as orders of unusual size or frequency, and to refuse to fill orders they could not verify were for legitimate medical purposes. Internal documents from the major distributors revealed in litigation showed that employees recognized the suspicious patterns and in some cases raised internal concerns, which were overridden by commercial considerations. The distributors' legal obligations to serve as a check on diversion were functionally nullified by business incentives that rewarded volume regardless of destination.

Accountability and Its Limits

The litigation against opioid manufacturers, distributors and pharmacies has produced settlements totaling more than $50 billion across multiple cases and defendants. Johnson & Johnson agreed to a $5 billion settlement. The major distributors agreed to a combined $21 billion settlement with states and localities. Walgreens, CVS and Walmart reached pharmacy settlements totaling approximately $13.8 billion. Purdue Pharma's bankruptcy settlement, approved in modified form after the Supreme Court rejected an initial version that would have shielded Sackler family members from civil lawsuits, distributed approximately $6 billion, with a significant portion reserved for opioid abatement programs.

Whether these settlements represent meaningful accountability depends heavily on how one defines the term. The dollar amounts are large in absolute terms but modest relative to the profits extracted from opioid sales, the costs imposed on communities and the scale of human suffering. No senior executive from Purdue Pharma went to prison for the company's actions. The 2007 guilty plea was by the corporate entity, not individuals. The Sackler family retained substantial wealth even after the settlement, with estimates of remaining family wealth in the billions. The architecture of corporate liability protection that allowed the family to benefit from OxyContin revenues while the company absorbed the legal risk was criticized by accountability advocates throughout the litigation but never fully pierced.

The policy response to the opioid crisis has been more substantial than the legal accountability, though still inadequate to the scale of the problem. The expansion of naloxone access, a medication that reverses opioid overdose, has saved hundreds of thousands of lives. The expansion of medication-assisted treatment (buprenorphine and methadone, both of which reduce opioid use and overdose risk with strong evidence bases) has made evidence-based addiction treatment more accessible, though access remains deeply unequal across geography and income. Harm reduction programs including needle exchange and supervised consumption sites have expanded, though the latter face significant legal and political obstacles in the United States despite strong evidence of effectiveness from international examples.

What the Crisis Reveals About Healthcare

The opioid epidemic is, among other things, a stress test of American healthcare's ability to resist pharmaceutical industry influence, and the test results are not good. The mechanisms by which the industry captured the prescribing behavior of physicians, the regulatory process and the academic pain medicine literature are not unique to opioids. They are standard pharmaceutical marketing tools applied with exceptional intensity to a product with exceptional addiction potential and exceptional commercial upside.

The pain medicine subspecialty was particularly thoroughly captured. The American Pain Foundation, which appeared to be an independent patient advocacy organization, received over 90 percent of its funding from pharmaceutical companies including Purdue before dissolving in 2012 amid scrutiny. Key opinion leaders in pain medicine who published influential research on opioid prescribing had financial relationships with opioid manufacturers that were often not disclosed in their publications. Continuing medical education programs on pain management were sponsored by industry and delivered messages consistent with industry marketing objectives.

The physicians who prescribed OxyContin were, in many cases, acting in good faith based on information they had been provided through channels they had no particular reason to distrust. Medical education does not train physicians to treat promotional materials as adversarial communications requiring rigorous skepticism. The trust infrastructure that makes the physician-patient relationship functional can also make physicians vulnerable to manipulation by well-resourced commercial interests willing to invest in the cultivation of that trust.

The opioid crisis is not history. More Americans died from opioid overdoses in 2022 than in any previous year, driven primarily by illicitly manufactured fentanyl that the pharmaceutical industry did not produce and cannot control. The epidemic that began with a deliberate corporate strategy has produced a public health crisis that is now substantially independent of its origins, driven by an illicit supply chain that no amount of pharmaceutical accountability can fully address. The half-million dead are a permanent record of what happens when profit is systematically prioritized over population health. The ongoing deaths are a reminder that the consequences of that prioritization can outlast any settlement.
