The Wellness Industry Is Selling You Back Your Own Health

27,482 upvotes · by u/snake_oil_skeptic · 14 min read
▲ 27,482 people found this relatable
Advertisement

There is a particular kind of marketing genius in selling people something they already have. The wellness industry, currently valued at approximately $4.5 trillion globally by the Global Wellness Institute, has built one of the most profitable commercial ecosystems in human history on exactly this premise. It begins by convincing you that your body is toxic, your sleep is insufficient, your stress is damaging your cells, your gut is leaking, your hormones are dysregulated, your emotional state is not optimized and your relationship with your own mind is broken. Then it sells you the solution: the supplements, the protocols, the devices, the retreats, the courses, the subscriptions and the communities that will return you to a state of wellness that, it strongly implies, you were in before you started paying attention to all of this.

The circular elegance of this model is worth admiring before critiquing it. The diagnostic apparatus that identifies the problem is built by the same industry that sells the cure. The anxiety about health that makes people susceptible to wellness marketing is itself partly produced by wellness marketing. The information environment that tells you about leaky gut, adrenal fatigue and toxic load is often populated by content created by or on behalf of companies that sell treatments for these conditions. You cannot fully trust the diagnosis when the entity diagnosing you profits from your illness.

This is not to say that wellness is all fraud. Human beings have genuine health needs that are often underserved by conventional medicine, and the wellness industry's emergence partly reflects real failures of a healthcare system that treats symptoms rather than causes, dismisses patient concerns and has limited tools for the management of chronic conditions. The problem is not that people are seeking wellbeing. The problem is the commercial infrastructure that has colonized that seeking and made it extraordinarily difficult to distinguish between the genuine and the manufactured, the helpful and the harmful, the evidence-based and the profitable fiction.

The Wellness-Industrial Complex and Its Origins

Modern wellness culture has several distinct genealogical lines that have converged into the current phenomenon. One line runs through the alternative medicine and natural health movements of the 1960s and 1970s, which positioned themselves in explicit opposition to mainstream medicine and pharmaceutical industry dominance. These movements carried a genuine critique of over-medicalization and the narrowness of the biomedical model, but they also created a framework in which "natural" became a synonym for safe and effective, and in which scientific skepticism of alternative claims was framed as establishment suppression rather than legitimate empirical inquiry.

A second line runs through the self-help industry, which from the mid-twentieth century onward built a commercial model around the proposition that personal transformation is available through the right knowledge, practices and disciplines. Self-help merged with wellness in the 1990s and 2000s to produce what sociologists sometimes call "therapeutic culture": the widespread application of therapeutic and personal growth frameworks to the ordinary challenges of everyday life, accompanied by the commercialization of the tools and communities through which this growth is supposedly achieved.

A third and increasingly dominant line runs through social media influencer culture, which provided both a distribution mechanism and a business model for wellness content. The influencer economy created a class of wellness-adjacent content creators whose income depends on the continuous production of compelling health-related content and whose financial relationships with supplement and product companies are sometimes disclosed and often not. The aesthetic codes of wellness content, the particular visual language of clean countertops, green juices, yoga postures at sunrise and linen-adjacent earthiness, have become so powerful that they function as a form of social proof independent of any underlying evidence for the practices being displayed.

It was just over $11,000. My bloodwork at the end of the year was almost identical to the start. I am a reasonably intelligent person. The Diagnostic Machinery: Creating Illness from Ambiguity

One of the wellness industry's most powerful tools is what might be called diagnostic capture: the identification and naming of health conditions that are vague enough to apply to almost anyone, sufficiently concerning to motivate purchasing behavior and conveniently treatable with the products the diagnosing entity sells. Several conditions have served this role with particular commercial effectiveness over the past two decades.

"Adrenal fatigue" is perhaps the clearest example. The term describes a supposed state of adrenal insufficiency caused by chronic stress, resulting in fatigue, brain fog, difficulty concentrating and a range of other symptoms that are common enough to apply to most adults at some point. The problem is that adrenal fatigue does not correspond to any recognized medical condition. Genuine adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease) exists and is diagnosable through hormone testing and has specific clinical characteristics. "Adrenal fatigue" as described in wellness contexts does not meet the diagnostic criteria for any recognized endocrine disorder. Multiple systematic reviews have concluded that no evidence supports it as a distinct medical entity. This has not prevented an extensive supplement and protocol market from developing around its treatment.

"Leaky gut" or intestinal permeability is more complicated, because intestinal permeability is a real biological phenomenon that researchers are actively studying in the context of inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease and other conditions. The wellness industry version of leaky gut is considerably expanded beyond what the research supports, attributing a remarkable range of conditions, from autoimmunity to mental illness to skin problems, to gut permeability caused by diet and lifestyle factors, and recommending interventions whose evidence base is thin at best. The kernel of legitimate science is real. The commercial superstructure built on that kernel is substantially fictional.

"Toxins" is the broadest and most durable category of wellness diagnostic. The body is framed as accumulating toxins from food, environment, stress and modern life, and as requiring regular cleansing, detoxification or resetting to restore optimal function. The specific toxins are almost never named, because naming them would require specifying mechanisms that are not actually established. The liver and kidneys are the body's actual detoxification systems, and they function continuously and effectively in healthy people without requiring supplemental detox support. This is sufficiently well established that the UK Advertising Standards Authority has ruled that companies cannot use the word "detox" in product marketing unless they can substantiate the claim, which they consistently cannot. The word remains ubiquitous in wellness marketing in most other jurisdictions.

The Supplement Market: Billions in Mostly Untested Compounds

The dietary supplement industry in the United States operates under a regulatory framework established by the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994, which is notable for what it does not require. Unlike pharmaceutical drugs, dietary supplements do not need to be proven safe and effective before they reach the market. Manufacturers do not need to notify the FDA before selling a supplement. They do not need to demonstrate that the supplement does what it claims. They are prohibited from claiming to treat, cure or prevent disease, but they are permitted to make "structure/function" claims, statements about the effect of the supplement on normal body function, which in practice are almost indistinguishable from disease claims to the average consumer.

The consequences of this regulatory environment are predictable. The supplement market is populated by products that range from genuinely useful (certain vitamins and minerals in people who are deficient) through possibly useful but poorly evidenced (many herbal supplements with promising but preliminary research) to actively useless (most detox, cleanse and metabolism-boosting products) to potentially harmful (some supplements that interact with medications or cause direct toxicity at the doses sold). The consumer has no reliable mechanism for distinguishing between these categories at the point of purchase, because the labeling standards do not require the disclosure of evidence quality.

Independent testing of supplement products by organizations like ConsumerLab and NSF International consistently finds that a significant fraction of products do not contain the ingredients listed on the label at the stated concentrations. Some contain unlisted ingredients, including prescription compounds and controlled substances. Some are contaminated with heavy metals or other adulterants. The supplement you buy because an influencer whose aesthetic you admire has associated it with her glowing skin and clear energy may not contain what it says it contains, and what it says it contains may not do what the marketing implies it does.

Wellness and Class: Who Gets to Be Healthy

Wellness culture presents itself as democratizing health, making information and practices available to everyone regardless of whether they can access conventional healthcare. This framing deserves scrutiny. The actual texture of mainstream wellness culture is profoundly class-stratified in ways that are rarely acknowledged.

The wellness ideal requires considerable financial and temporal resources. Organic food costs more than conventional food. A gym membership, yoga studio or boutique fitness class costs more than free activity. High-quality supplements are expensive. A retreat or immersive wellness experience costs thousands of dollars. Working with a functional medicine practitioner, a naturopath, a health coach or other wellness-adjacent practitioners is typically not covered by insurance and commands significant out-of-pocket fees. The time required to meal prep, maintain a morning routine, exercise consistently, meditate and engage in the various practices that wellness culture recommends is time that precarious and overworked people, people working multiple jobs, people with caregiving responsibilities, people with long commutes, do not have in the same quantities as their more affluent counterparts.

The implicit message of wellness culture, that the state of your health reflects the quality of your choices and the seriousness of your commitment to your own wellbeing, produces a framework in which the health disparities that track income, race and geographic access are reframed as outcomes of individual behavior rather than structural inequality. The person eating a nutrient-poor diet because they live in a food desert and cannot afford to shop anywhere else is, in the wellness framework, someone who has not yet learned the importance of clean eating. The person who cannot exercise because they work two jobs and have no childcare has not yet discovered the joy of joyful movement. The structural conditions that produce health inequality are laundered through a language of individual choice and personal optimization until they become invisible.

The Anxiety Amplification Loop

One of the wellness industry's most significant and least discussed consequences is the anxiety it produces in the people it claims to serve. The information environment that wellness culture creates is one in which the list of potential threats to your health is effectively infinite and constantly expanding. Every new study, every new influencer claim, every new product launch adds to the catalogue of things you should be worried about. Seed oils. Plastic in your blood. Electromagnetic fields. Mold in your home. Heavy metals in your protein powder. Microplastics in your water. Histamine intolerance. Oxalate sensitivity. Mineral deficiencies you did not know existed. Circadian rhythm disruption. Blue light. The morning cortisol spike you might be mismanaging.

Some of these concerns have legitimate scientific bases at certain exposure levels or in specific populations. Most of them, as presented in wellness content, are catastrophized beyond what the evidence supports. But the emotional architecture they create in aggregate is one of constant vigilance about potential biological threats, a state of health anxiety that is, by itself, a significant stressor. The cortisol produced by worrying about cortisol is real. The sleep disrupted by reading about the dangers of disrupted sleep is genuinely disruptive. The social anxiety generated by being uncertain whether everything you might eat in a restaurant is safe is a real impairment to the enjoyment of food and social connection.

Psychologists who study health anxiety recognize that the wellness industry's information environment is, for a subset of consumers, actively producing the condition it purports to prevent. People with subclinical health anxiety, a category that is likely very large given the current media environment, find their anxiety validated and amplified rather than relieved by wellness content consumption. Each new concern addressed temporarily by a new product or protocol is replaced by a new concern, often surfaced by the same content ecosystem that sold the previous solution. The wellness industry has no financial interest in resolving the underlying anxiety, because resolved anxiety does not continue purchasing.

What Genuine Evidence-Based Wellbeing Actually Looks Like

The evidence base for what actually promotes human health over a lifetime is, despite the complexity that wellness culture implies, relatively consistent and relatively boring. It does not require a $4.5 trillion industry to implement. Regular physical activity of moderate intensity, in forms that you enjoy enough to maintain. Adequate sleep of sufficient duration and reasonable consistency. A dietary pattern with substantial vegetables, fruits, legumes and whole grains, moderate in ultra-processed foods, and not so restrictive that it creates psychological distress. Social connection and relationships of meaningful quality. Management of chronic stress through whatever combination of approaches works for the individual. Not smoking. Moderate or no alcohol. These are the factors with the strongest, most replicated evidence for long-term health outcomes.

Notice what is absent from this list. Supplements, with the exception of specific deficiencies in specific populations. Detoxes, cleanses and resets. Optimization of biomarkers through continuous monitoring in the absence of specific medical indication. Expensive equipment. Elimination of entire food categories. Elaborate morning routines. Specific dietary philosophies with proprietary frameworks. These things are not on the list because the evidence for their specific health contributions, beyond the general factors already named, is weak or nonexistent or has not been studied at sufficient scale and rigor to support confident claims.

This does not mean that individual practices within wellness culture are all useless. Meditation has a genuine and reasonably robust evidence base for stress reduction and certain mental health applications. Some dietary supplements have evidence for specific uses in specific populations. Spending time in nature has real and well-documented psychological benefits. The problem is not that every wellness practice is fraudulent. The problem is the commercial system through which these practices are packaged and sold, which systematically overstates the evidence, creates unnecessary anxiety to motivate purchasing, exploits the information asymmetry between producers and consumers and directs enormous financial and attention resources toward expensive marginal interventions while the fundamental determinants of population health, access to healthcare, income security, clean environments, safe neighborhoods, social connection remain unaddressed.

Navigating the Noise

Developing a more skeptical and discerning relationship with wellness content is possible, and the tools for doing it are available even if they require some deliberate cultivation. A useful starting framework is to ask, for any wellness claim, what is the mechanism, what is the evidence and who benefits financially from your believing this. The mechanism question asks whether the biological pathway through which the claimed effect is supposed to occur makes sense given what is known about physiology. The evidence question asks whether the claim is supported by randomized controlled trials in human populations, by observational data with appropriate controls, or only by testimonials, case studies, animal research or in vitro studies that have not been replicated in human contexts. The financial interest question asks who profits if this claim is believed, and whether that profit motive creates an incentive to overstate the evidence.

Organizations like the Cochrane Collaboration, which publishes systematic reviews of health evidence, and Science-Based Medicine, which evaluates health claims against the available evidence base, provide counterweights to the commercial wellness information environment. They are less visually appealing than wellness content and significantly less emotionally satisfying than the narrative of personal transformation and optimization. They also tend to be accurate.

The deepest shift available is probably a philosophical one: moving from a stance of optimization toward a stance of sufficiency. The wellness industry is built on the assumption that health is a project of continuous improvement with no terminal state of completion. Sufficiency asks a different question: what do I actually need to feel well, function well and enjoy my life, and am I already doing most of it? For most people in wealthy countries who are not dealing with specific medical conditions, the honest answer to that question is probably yes. The noise of the wellness industry is very effective at preventing that answer from being heard.
