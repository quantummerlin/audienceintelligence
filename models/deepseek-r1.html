January 2025: The DeepSeek Shock

When DeepSeek released R1 in January 2025, the AI industry had a working assumption baked in: frontier-level reasoning required frontier-level compute budgets and was therefore accessible only through the most expensive model tiers. OpenAI's o1, launched in late 2024, charged $15 per million input tokens and $60 per million output tokens and was widely considered the ceiling of what was achievable in chain-of-thought reasoning.

DeepSeek R1 broke that assumption cleanly. At $0.70 per million input tokens and $2.50 per million output tokens, it delivered reasoning benchmark performance that matched or exceeded o1 on competitive math, scientific reasoning, and coding tasks — at 4.7% of o1's input cost and 4.2% of its output cost. The news sent AI company stocks tumbling and triggered a wave of industry reassessment about the true cost floor of reasoning-capable AI.

The DeepSeek Shock — January 2025

DeepSeek R1 vs. OpenAI o1 at launch: $0.70 vs. $15 per million input tokens. $2.50 vs. $60 per million output tokens. Performance parity across math, coding, and scientific reasoning benchmarks. The assumption that frontier reasoning required frontier pricing was broken in a single release — by a Chinese lab operating at a fraction of OpenAI's disclosed compute budget.

The "DeepSeek shock" was not just about price. DeepSeek released the model weights openly, meaning developers could self-host R1 at zero marginal inference cost. The combination of open weights and API-accessible pricing via OpenRouter permanently changed the competitive calculus for anyone building reasoning-intensive applications.

Two facts amplified the impact. First, DeepSeek published detailed technical papers alongside the weights, allowing researchers to understand exactly how R1 had been trained — using reinforcement learning to reward reasoning quality rather than just final-answer correctness. Second, the model produced visible chain-of-thought tokens: you could read the reasoning, not just receive the conclusion. This transparency contrasted directly with o1, which concealed its internal reasoning trace from API consumers.

Architecture: Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning

DeepSeek R1 is a dense transformer architecture trained using a two-phase approach. The base model acquires general language capabilities through standard pretraining. The distinctive element is the second phase: reinforcement learning applied specifically to reward the quality and correctness of chain-of-thought reasoning, not merely the accuracy of final answers.

This RL-based training incentivizes the model to develop long, structured internal reasoning chains before committing to a response. The result is a model that behaves differently from standard instruction-tuned LLMs: given a complex problem, it will naturally expand its response into a deliberation process, working through trade-offs and decision points explicitly before synthesizing a conclusion. The reasoning tokens are exposed in the API response under reasoning_content — readable, auditable, and searchable.

Key Finding — Only Model to Complete Naturally

DeepSeek R1 is the only model in our 12-model benchmark series to return finish_reason: stop. Every other model hit the token ceiling and was cut off mid-response — returning finish_reason: length. R1 delivered a complete, naturally concluded answer.

This is not a minor technical detail. finish_reason: stop means the model reached a natural stopping point on its own — it finished what it set out to say. finish_reason: length means the model was interrupted and the response is potentially incomplete. R1 generated 1,353 completion tokens (including 747 reasoning tokens) and still finished before the 600-token visible limit. This demonstrates that R1 naturally calibrates its response length to match the complexity of the question — rather than generating indefinitely until cut off.

The practical implication: when you call DeepSeek R1 on a well-scoped analytical question, you receive a complete answer. With most other models at tight token budgets, you receive a partial one.

SQL vs. NoSQL: Key Trade-offs

AspectSQLNoSQL
Data ModelStructured tables with predefined schemaFlexible — document, key-value, graph, column-family
ScalabilityVertical scaling (bigger hardware) or complex shardingHorizontal scaling across distributed clusters
ConsistencyFull ACID guaranteesBASE model — eventual consistency (ACID optional in some)
Query FlexibilityPowerful SQL with JOINs and aggregationsLimited joins; query patterns vary by database type
PerformanceStrong on structured relational queriesHigh throughput on simple reads/writes at scale
Use Case FitFinancial, transactional, relational dataUnstructured, high-volume, rapidly evolving schemas

Choose SQL When:

  • Data is structured and relational — accounts, customers, orders with clear foreign keys
  • ACID transactions are critical — banking, healthcare, any system where partial writes are catastrophic
  • Complex queries are required — multi-table JOINs, aggregations, ad hoc reporting
  • Data integrity constraints are non-negotiable — referential integrity, unique constraints, cascading deletes

Choose NoSQL When:

  • Data is unstructured or schema is rapidly evolving — user-generated content, product catalogs with varying attributes
  • Horizontal scalability is required — massive write throughput across many servers
  • Eventual consistency is acceptable — social feeds, analytics, recommendation engines
  • Access patterns are well-defined and simple — key-value lookups, document retrieval by ID

Concrete Examples

1. Banking System → PostgreSQL (SQL)

A retail bank's core ledger requires ACID compliance to prevent partial transactions — a transfer must debit one account and credit another atomically, or neither. The data is highly structured (accounts, customers, balances, audit trails), relationships are well-defined, and complex queries for compliance reporting are common. PostgreSQL's transaction isolation and referential integrity are exactly what this workload requires.

2. Social Media Feed → MongoDB (NoSQL)

A social platform's content feed involves massive write throughput (millions of posts per minute globally), semi-structured data (posts with varying fields — text, images, polls, reactions), and a need for horizontal scaling across many servers. Eventual consistency is acceptable — a user seeing a post 200ms later than another user is a non-issue. MongoDB's document model fits the varied post schema, and its sharding capabilities handle the scale that would make a relational JOIN-heavy schema impractical.

We are comparing SQL (relational) and NoSQL (non-relational) databases. Key trade-offs include:

Data Model: SQL uses structured tables with rows and columns, requiring a predefined schema. NoSQL offers flexible schema — document, key-value, graph, or column-family.

Scalability: SQL typically scales vertically (bigger hardware) or with complex horizontal sharding. NoSQL is designed for horizontal scaling across distributed clusters.

Transactions: SQL supports full ACID guarantees. NoSQL often uses BASE (Basically Available, Soft state, Eventual consistency) — though some now support ACID optionally.

When to choose each?

  • Choose SQL when data is structured and relational, ACID transactions are critical (e.g., banking), and complex queries are required.
  • Choose NoSQL when data is unstructured or evolving, horizontal scalability is needed, and eventual consistency is acceptable.

Concrete Examples:

  • SQL: Banking system — ACID compliance prevents partial transactions. The data is highly structured (accounts, balances, customers) and complex audit queries are common.
  • NoSQL: Social media feed — massive write throughput, semi-structured data (posts with varying fields), needs horizontal scaling across many servers.

What the reasoning trace shows is systematic: the model identifies the relevant dimensions (data model, scalability, consistency, query patterns), works through the ACID versus BASE trade-off explicitly, then selects concrete examples that cleanly illustrate the contrasting requirements. The banking and social media examples were not random — the reasoning shows they were chosen because they represent opposite ends of the ACID/consistency spectrum. This is the chain-of-thought doing its job: structuring the answer before a single word of the visible response is written.

The R1 Lineage: From January 2025 to Today

This article covers the original DeepSeek R1 released in January 2025 — the model that started the movement. Since then, DeepSeek has shipped an updated version.

DeepSeek R1 0528 (May 2026) is a meaningful capability update, identified by DeepSeek's date-based versioning convention. It is priced at $0.50/$2.15 per 1M tokens — slightly cheaper than the original — with refined reasoning quality. Aether Intel has a separate review for DeepSeek R1 0528 covering its specific benchmark results (including its behavior when hitting the token limit — a different result from the original R1's completion).

The broader DeepSeek ecosystem now includes DeepSeek V4 Flash — a fast, inexpensive model that carries R1-style reasoning capabilities as an unexpected feature. At $0.112/$0.224 per 1M tokens with a 1M context window, V4 Flash occupies a different position in the lineup: it is not as deeply reasoned as R1, but it brings visible chain-of-thought to a price tier and context scale that R1 does not reach. The original R1 remains the reference point for DeepSeek reasoning quality.

Who It Is For — And Who It Is Not

Good fit for

  • Multi-step analytical reasoning — architecture decisions, trade-off analysis, technical comparisons
  • Complex math, proofs, and algorithmic problems requiring step-by-step derivation
  • Code debugging and design with full reasoning trace for review
  • Scientific reasoning and research synthesis within 160K context
  • Cost-sensitive production reasoning pipelines replacing o1
  • Applications where visible reasoning chain is a product feature
  • Tasks requiring thorough, complete answers rather than fast partial responses

Poor fit for

  • Long-document tasks exceeding 160K context (use a 1M-context model instead)
  • Image, PDF, or audio input (text-only model)
  • Simple conversational queries where reasoning overhead adds latency without benefit
  • Strict real-time latency requirements — reasoning depth means slower first token
  • Very high-volume simple classification or extraction tasks
  • Tasks where reasoning transparency is a privacy concern
