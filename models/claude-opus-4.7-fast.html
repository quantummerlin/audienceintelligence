What is Claude Opus 4.7 Fast?

Claude Opus 4.7 Fast is Anthropic's production-optimised variant of their Claude 4.7 Opus flagship, released in May 2026. The "Fast" designation in the model family signals a specific inference configuration: the model is tuned for high-throughput, low-latency responses rather than extended deliberative reasoning. Where the base Opus 4.7 can engage chain-of-thought reasoning at the cost of additional time and tokens, the Fast variant skips that overhead entirely — the model produces its output in a single generation pass, without visible reasoning traces.

This is not a stripped-down model. The underlying weights retain the full Opus 4.7 capability profile: strong instruction following, clean structured output, and consistent performance across professional writing, code review, analysis, and complex question answering. The trade-off is deliberate — you get Opus-level quality at speeds and latencies compatible with interactive production applications, without paying for reasoning tokens you may not need.

The model is available through Anthropic's direct API and via OpenRouter under the ID anthropic/claude-opus-4.7-fast (actual model slug: claude-4.7-opus-fast-20260512). Context window is 200,000 tokens — solid for professional tasks, though notably below the 1M context of the standard Opus 4.7 or Google's frontier offerings. Vision input is supported; output is text only.

What "Fast" Actually Means

In Anthropic's model family, the "Fast" suffix describes an inference configuration, not a capability reduction. Extended thinking — the chain-of-thought reasoning mode that allows Claude models to work through complex problems step-by-step before generating a final answer — is disabled in this variant. That means no reasoning token overhead, no additional latency for deliberation, and no unpredictable cost spikes when the model decides a problem warrants more thought.

For many professional use cases, this is exactly right. Extended thinking shines on mathematical problem-solving, multi-step logical reasoning, and adversarial benchmarks designed to trip up direct-answer models. But for structured writing, document analysis, code review, and instruction-following tasks — the workhorse of professional AI automation — it adds cost and latency without meaningful quality improvement. The Fast variant delivers the response without the preamble.

Our benchmark confirmed zero reasoning tokens in the response. The model went directly to output: a clean structured table followed by well-organised sections, all formatted at professional standard. Response latency was noticeably lower than reasoning-enabled models at this quality tier. For production applications where users expect a response within seconds rather than waiting for an extended thinking pass, Opus 4.7 Fast is architecturally appropriate in a way that the base Opus 4.7 with extended thinking enabled is not.

Note on Reasoning Tokens
Reasoning tokens are billed in addition to completion tokens on models that support them. On a complex prompt, a reasoning-enabled model might silently consume 2,000–8,000 reasoning tokens before generating its actual response — costs you pay without seeing the output. With Opus 4.7 Fast, what you see is what you pay for: 0 reasoning tokens, every time.

Key Trade-offs

SQL gives you:

  • Data integrity and referential constraints enforced at the DB layer
  • Powerful ad-hoc querying across any combination of fields
  • Predictable consistency — every read sees the latest committed write
  • Cost: schema migrations are painful; vertical scaling has a ceiling

NoSQL gives you:

  • Horizontal scalability — add nodes rather than upgrading hardware
  • Schema flexibility — evolve your data model without downtime
  • High write throughput for time-series, event, or log data
  • Cost: joins are your problem; consistency is often eventual

When to Choose Each

Choose SQL when your data has clear relationships, you need complex queries across multiple entities, or correctness matters more than throughput (finance, inventory, user accounts). Choose NoSQL when you need to scale writes horizontally, your schema is fluid or document-like, or you're storing homogeneous high-volume data (logs, events, sensor readings, user sessions).

Context Window: 200K

The 200,000-token context window is substantial — roughly 150,000 words, enough to hold a full novel, a large codebase, or a stack of legal documents — but it deserves honest framing against the current frontier. The standard Claude Opus 4.7 offers 1M tokens. Google Gemini 2.5 Pro and DeepSeek V4 Flash both offer 1M contexts. GPT-5 supports 1M tokens. At $30/$150 pricing, paying a massive premium while receiving a 5× smaller context window than competitors is a significant trade-off.

In practice, 200K is sufficient for most professional use cases: a single long contract, a 50-page financial report, a medium-sized codebase, or a full conversation history spanning hours of interaction. Where it becomes a constraint is in research synthesis spanning hundreds of documents, full-book analysis, or complex agentic workflows that accumulate large amounts of intermediate state over extended runs. For those workloads, the base Opus 4.7 with its 1M context is the better choice — despite the extended thinking overhead.

Vision Input

Claude Opus 4.7 Fast supports image inputs alongside text, making it viable for workflows that involve screenshots, diagrams, charts, scanned documents, or visual interfaces. Anthropic's vision implementation is among the strongest in the market for tasks requiring careful description, OCR-adjacent extraction from structured visual documents, and UI analysis — areas where the model's high instruction-following quality translates directly into better structured outputs from visual inputs.

For the price point, you would expect nothing less. Vision-capable workflows — processing scanned legal contracts, extracting data from financial charts, reviewing UI screenshots for bugs or copy — are exactly the category where output quality has clear dollar value. If your pipeline involves visual documents and you need reliable structured extraction, the Opus 4.7 Fast vision capability justifies its own line in the ROI calculation.

The Cost Question: When Does $0.345/Exchange Make Sense?

The honest answer is: rarely, by volume — and often, by value. The $0.345 per 3,500-token exchange figure sounds high until you compare it to the cost of a human equivalent. A lawyer reviewing a contract for one hour bills $400–$800. A senior engineer doing a code review for two hours costs $150–$400 in salary equivalent. An analyst synthesising a research report might take half a day. When the benchmark exchange represents a meaningful slice of work that would otherwise cost 10–100× more in professional time, the model pricing becomes secondary.

The use cases where Opus 4.7 Fast earns its premium are concentrated in high-stakes professional automation: the first-pass legal review that flags issues for a lawyer to assess, the code audit that surfaces architectural concerns before a senior engineer dives in, the financial report summary that a portfolio manager can act on without wading through 80 pages. In each case, the quality ceiling matters — a missed clause, a misread metric, a failed code smell that a cheaper model skips becomes a liability rather than an efficiency gain.

For internal tooling, experimentation, high-volume data processing, or any workflow where response volume exceeds a few hundred exchanges per day, the calculus inverts sharply. At 1,000 exchanges/day, you are spending $345 per day — $125,000 per year — on a single model endpoint. At that volume and budget, it is worth asking whether Claude Sonnet 4.6 at one-tenth the cost achieves 95% of the quality for your task. In most high-volume scenarios, it does.
