The Dead Internet Is Not a Theory Anymore

May 21, 2026 • 7 min read

One agent. Fifty accounts.

In March 2025, a video circulated on X and Reddit showing a single AI agent called Manus managing 50 social media accounts simultaneously. Not scheduling posts in advance. Not running a batch job overnight. Running them, all of them, continuously, 24 hours a day, posting content, liking, commenting, growing followers, adapting to engagement.

The video was captioned simply: "Chinese AI agent running 50 social media accounts 24/7 automatically." It collected 14,469 upvotes on r/ThatsInsane.

The top comment on the thread, upvoted 4,101 times, was two words: "Dead internet."

The second comment, at 3,784 votes: "If you think this ain't on Reddit too, you're mistaken."

No one in the thread argued with either of them.

What the theory actually claimed

The Dead Internet Theory has circulated online since roughly 2016. The core claim: most internet activity is already automated. Most engagement is generated by bots. Most of what you see trending, most of what gets amplified, is not the result of real humans choosing to share things. It is the output of automated systems designed to manufacture the appearance of consensus.

For years this was treated as a conspiracy theory, the kind of thing you found on paranormal forums alongside chemtrail discussion. The Atlantic ran a piece in 2021 titled "The Dead Internet Theory Is Wrong But Feels True." Experts acknowledged the internet felt hollow but were reluctant to endorse the structural claim.

The data, however, kept pointing the same direction. A 2023 report from security firm Imperva found that 49.6% of internet traffic was automated, up from 47.4% the year before. The increase was attributed to AI models crawling the web for training content. Nearly half of all internet traffic, generated by machines, before AI social agents became a product you could buy.

What changed in 2025

The shift was not a single event. It was a compression of several things happening at once.

In January 2025, Meta's vice president of product for generative AI, Connor Hayes, described the company's plans directly: "We expect these AIs to actually, over time, exist on our platforms, kind of in the same way that accounts do. They'll have bios and profile pictures and be able to generate and share content powered by AI on the platform." Those accounts were quickly removed after the announcement drew backlash, but the plan had been named.

In March 2025, the Manus video circulated.

In June 2025, Reddit co-founder Alexis Ohanian said at a Wall Street Journal panel that he had "long subscribed to the dead internet theory," acknowledged it was regarded as a conspiracy theory a decade ago, and called it "a very real thing" because of the proliferation of bots and humans using AI to create and amplify content.

In September 2025, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman posted on X: "I never took the dead internet theory that seriously but it seems like there are really a lot of LLM-run twitter accounts now."

The CEO of the company most responsible for making large language models accessible to the world was surprised to find large language models running most of his social media feed.

Why AI makes this qualitatively different

Old bot networks were detectable. They used repetitive phrasing. They posted at mechanical intervals. They responded to the wrong things or not at all. Platform detection improved. Bot farms got taken down. The cat-and-mouse had clear enough contours that researchers could study it.

AI agents operating 50 accounts simultaneously don't work that way. Each account can generate distinct content, vary posting times to mimic human rhythms, respond to comments with contextually appropriate language, shift tone based on what's getting engagement. A 2024 study from the University of Melbourne and University of New South Wales described the result as a "vicious cycle of artificial engagement": AI bots create content, other AI bots engage with it and amplify it, follower counts rise, the accounts gain legitimacy with real users, and none of this requires human involvement at any step.

The researchers cited "Shrimp Jesus," an AI-generated image that went viral on Facebook in 2024, as an example. An algorithmically optimized absurdity, created and amplified by machines, spread widely among real humans who found it funny or confusing enough to share.

The third top comment on the Manus thread, at 2,171 votes, put it plainly: "AI is already cannibalizing itself. AI 'art' is drawing on the already-existing AI slop and becoming inbred. AI text generation is pulling info from AI-generated bullshit and regurgitating it."

That is not an overstatement. It describes the mechanism accurately. Models trained on content generated by other models produce outputs that are progressively further from whatever the original human signal was.

The economics of why this keeps growing

Manus was not a secret government program or a sophisticated state intelligence operation. It was a product. The video showed a dashboard. Someone built a tool and was demonstrating it, probably to sell it or attract investment.

The economics are straightforward. Social media influence is worth money. Growing 50 accounts to meaningful follower counts takes time and labor if done by humans. An AI agent can do it continuously at near-zero marginal cost per account. The incentive to use these tools is not limited to state actors running disinformation campaigns. It extends to marketers, small businesses trying to build brand presence, political campaigns, anyone trying to shape how a topic trends, and people who just want to make money from the engagement monetization that platforms now offer verified accounts.

Elon Musk reportedly commented on the Manus video that it "seems more human than humans." He has also, on the platform he owns, implemented policies that critics argue made the economics of automated accounts more attractive, not less. Verified accounts earn engagement-based revenue. Large follower counts get more distribution. If you can manufacture both with a sufficiently convincing AI, the platform's own incentive structure works in your favor.

What you are actually reading

A 2024 paper in PMC that analyzed social media chatter across roughly 200 million users and seven global events found that 20% of posts came from bot accounts. That figure applies to a measured sample across structured events, which likely represents a lower bound for the internet more broadly, where coordination and manipulation are more common in unmonitored spaces.

The fourth top comment on the Manus thread, at 1,311 votes, said: "This is who creates the majority of reddit comments and determines what comments are upvoted for you to see them."

That is probably an exaggeration, but it is the kind of exaggeration that people make when the underlying anxiety is real. When you read that an opinion is widely held, that a topic is trending, that a product is beloved, the source of that signal is increasingly impossible to verify. The platforms show you numbers. They do not show you whether those numbers reflect human choices.

Swiss researchers published a paper in 2024 noting that the Dead Internet Theory "used to be rather speculative" ten years ago but "with the wake of generative AI, it can now be observed first-hand."

This is the actual shift. Not that the theory became true overnight. That it became observable. You can watch a dashboard. You can see one agent managing fifty identities. The thing that was paranoid speculation in 2016 is a product demo in 2025.

What the theory got wrong, and what it got right

The original Dead Internet Theory had a conspiratorial cast: a coordinated, deliberate effort by governments or corporations to replace human interaction with manufactured content. The evidence does not support a single coordinated actor. What it supports is something more banal and in some ways harder to address: a distributed set of individual incentives, all pointing in the same direction, operating independently across thousands of accounts and dozens of tools.

No one needs to coordinate for the outcome to look like coordination. Everyone running AI agents on social media is responding to the same incentive structure. The result is statistically indistinguishable from a conspiracy because the incentives are universal.

The comment at 391 votes in the Manus thread: "Ignore popular subs swarming with bots; embrace niche subs for topics and hobbies you enjoy where there are humans interacting nicely."

That is, at this point, probably the most realistic advice available. Not a solution. An adjustment. The internet is not dead, but the parts of it where AI agents are economically motivated to operate look nothing like what they did a decade ago. Finding the parts that don't fit that description requires active navigation.

That navigation is now a skill.
