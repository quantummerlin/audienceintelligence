Your AI Skills Are No Longer Yours — They're Company Infrastructure Now

Six months after Anthropic launched skills, the majority of skill calls are made by agents, not humans. Microsoft and OpenAI aligned on the format. The people trading skills like baseball cards online are compounding daily. Everyone else is still copy-pasting prompts.

By Aether Intel

Something fundamental shifted while most people were still figuring out how to write a decent system prompt. In October 2025, when Anthropic launched skills, the mental model most people brought to it was personal — a better way to store your best prompts, a configuration file that lived in your project folder, something you built for yourself. Six months later, that framing is obsolete. Skills have become organizational infrastructure, and the gap between the people who understood that early and everyone else is widening by the day.

The numbers crystallize how fast this moved.

50,000+ Lines of skills in one RE business

>50% Skill calls made by agents, not humans

That last number deserves more attention than it's getting. When skills launched, humans were the ones invoking them — you'd open a conversation, pull in a skill, get a structured output. A person might call a handful of skills across a session. Now, the majority of skill calls are being made by agents. This isn't a detail. It's a structural change in what skills are for. Agents running autonomously can make hundreds of skill calls in a single run, pulling in the right methodology for each sub-task without ever needing human direction in the loop. The math on human-as-caller simply doesn't work at that scale. Agents are the primary consumers now, and every skill you write is increasingly being written for them.

Four Shifts, Six Months

The transformation from personal tool to organizational layer didn't happen all at once. It came through four overlapping shifts, each one accelerating the next.

01
Personal to Organizational
Team and enterprise admins are now rolling out skills workplace-wide — version controlled, available in the sidebar, callable inside Excel, PowerPoint, Claude, and Copilot in the same breath. The question has shifted from "what skill should I build for myself?" to "how do we treat skills as agent-readable and human-readable infrastructure across the entire organization?"
02
Humans to Agents as Callers
The majority of skill calls are now being made by agents, not people. Agents running a multi-step workflow can invoke skills hundreds of times per run — pulling in the right methodology at the right moment without human intervention. We underestimated this transition entirely when skills launched.
03
Developer Niche to Open Standard
Anthropic and Microsoft formalized a partnership to bring skills into Copilot. OpenAI aligned on the same format. Skills are no longer a terminal-first developer artifact — they're an open standard that underpins how AI works across enterprise toolchains. If you're treating this as a power-user feature, you're already behind the frame.
04
Closed Alpha to Community Compound
Best practices with skills aren't documented anywhere authoritative — they're being discovered in public, shared online, and traded like baseball cards. The instruction book doesn't exist yet. Everyone is writing it together in real time. The people who showed up early to that process are already several compounding cycles ahead.

The Texas Paintbrush Problem — Solved With a Repo

The most instructive real-world example right now is a real estate GP operating under the handle Texas Paintbrush on X. They've built more than 50,000 lines of skills spread across 50 repositories — covering rent roll standardization, comps analysis, cash flow handling, and handoff protocols between different parts of the business. The scope alone is worth pausing on: 50 repos, one operator, six months.

What they've actually built isn't just a productivity stack. It's a solution to one of the oldest problems in professional services: methodology that lives inside people's heads. When the analyst who knows how you do comps analysis leaves, the methodology walks out with them. Skills repos don't quit. They don't have bad days. They don't forget the edge cases they've been trained on. When Texas Paintbrush onboards a new team member now, the skills repository serves as the context layer — here is how we do things, written down in a format that both the human and every AI tool they touch can read and execute against. The organizational knowledge is externalized, versioned, and callable.

The Specialist Stack Pattern

Alongside organizational rollouts, a distinct production pattern has emerged in how developers are actually deploying skills inside Claude and tools like Cursor. The pattern is straightforward enough that it's showing up across industries: a developer drops a folder of skills into a project. One skill converts vague input into a structured PRD. Another decomposes that PRD into GitHub issues. Another writes the test suite for a given issue. The agent — running inside whatever IDE or interface — can then receive a simple instruction like "build me this feature" and invoke the appropriate skills in sequence to do the work.

The insight embedded in this pattern is underappreciated. The agent doesn't need specialist direction because the specialist direction is already in the file. You don't have to prompt carefully every time for the outputs you want — the quality standard is encoded in the skill, callable by anything that reads it. Skills absorb the expertise so the conversation doesn't have to carry it.

Skills Compound. Prompts Don't.

"The people who have been building with skills have been compounding them. You can improve your skills, hone and refine them. The people who have been prompting all along are just copying and pasting the same stuff. Skills compound for you — by the weight of industry investment in the ecosystem and by the weight of your own commitment to having a predictable pattern. Prompts don't compound in the same way."

Nate Jones AI

This is the crux of why the divergence between early adopters and everyone else keeps accelerating. A prompt you wrote six months ago is the same prompt today — it has no memory of what worked, no mechanism for refinement, no way to absorb the institutional knowledge you've accumulated since. A skill file can be updated, versioned, and improved incrementally. Every time you find an edge case your skill doesn't handle well, you document it. Every time the output format isn't quite right, you sharpen it. The skill gets better as you get better, and it retains those improvements for every agent and teammate that touches it afterward. There's also a second compounding force: the entire ecosystem is investing in the format. Toolchains, integrations, interoperability improvements — all of it flows disproportionately to people who are already working in the skills paradigm.

How to Build a Skill That Actually Works

Most skills fail before the agent ever reads the body. The description field — a single required line that tells the model when and why to invoke the skill — is where the majority of poorly performing skills go to die. Vague descriptions like "helps with competitive analysis" give the model almost nothing to work with. A description that names specific artifact types, includes concrete trigger phrases such as "analyze our competitors," and states what the output will look like gives the model a clear signal to route against. Spend 80% of your attention on this field. And note the technical constraint: it must remain a single line. If your code formatter wraps it to two lines, Claude will not read the second one. That's not a guideline — it's a hard parsing limit.

In the body, the failure modes are different. Most skill bodies are too prescriptive about steps and not explicit enough about reasoning — the model needs to understand why a step matters, not just that it's step three of seven. Specify your output format explicitly. Document the edge cases you know about. Include at least one worked example the model can pattern-match against. And keep the file lean: under 100 to 150 lines for the core skill. A short skill that fires reliably every time will outperform a long skill riddled with competing instructions. Brevity is a quality signal, not a shortcut.

The organizations that internalize this now — that start building skills as infrastructure rather than as personal shortcuts — will find themselves with a compounding asset that gets more valuable as the ecosystem around it grows. The ones still copy-pasting prompts will find the distance between them and the leaders getting harder to close with every passing month. Six months in, that gap is already visible. Give it another six and it will be structural.
